Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cost of replication factor explained well nowhere else

Replication factor 3 costs $3,148 a month and claims 11.25 nines on paper, but a 1-in-10,000-year shared-fate event caps it at 4.0 nines — the same as factor 2

"RF=3 costs 3x and buys 3x the safety" is two separate wrong sentences glued together. Storage is the one line where the first half is exactly right. The bill's other line — cross-zone replica traffic — is capped by the zone count, not the replication factor, and durability isn't priced in dollars at all: it rises fast per copy and then hits a ceiling no copy count can cross.

Read first — cross-az-trafficreliability-availability-durability

01

Two bills for one dial, and only one of them is what everyone quotes

“Replication factor 3” is read as one decision with one price: three times the storage, three times the safety. It is one dial that writes two completely different kinds of receipt, and neither of them is 3×.

An append-only ledger — 2,000 GB, 5,000 ops/s, 40% reads, spread across three availability zones. At replication factor 3 the combined storage-plus-cross-zone-traffic bill is $3,148 a month: $600 of storage, exactly 3× the single-copy figure, and $2,548 of cross-zone replica writes, which is not 3× anything — it is capped by the zone count, and it is explained in full by cross-az-traffic, cited rather than re-derived here.

Durability tells a different story on a different axis entirely. Three independent copies of the same disks, at the annualised failure rate and rebuild window reliability-availability-durability already cites and computes, give 11.25 nines of durability against disk failure — a number so large it stops being intuitive around the fourth nine. That number is real. It is also not the number that survives contact with a fact almost every real incident confirms: things fail together. A bad deploy, one IAM policy, one expired certificate, reaches every copy at once, however many there are. Priced at a shared-fate rate of 1 event in 10,000 per year — the exact figure the sibling lesson uses — that 11.25 becomes 4.0 nines in fact. And two copies, not three, already reach that same 4.0, because two independent copies alone are already worth 6.56 nines, past the ceiling.

Replication factor is not one dial with one price. It is two mechanisms sharing one integer: a dollar mechanism that is exactly linear on one line and sub-linear (sometimes even falling) on the other, and a durability mechanism that rises fast per copy and then goes flat the moment a cause reaches every copy at once. The two mechanisms do not agree on when N stops paying for itself, and the bill keeps rising after the durability line already has.

About every number on this page. The dollar figures — storage and cross-zone price per gigabyte — are the same declared, dated, low-confidence inputs rfcost.mjs and cross-az-traffic.mdx already use (asOf: '2026-08'), substituted for your own bill before quoting an absolute. The durability figures — disk AFR, rebuild hours, the shared-fate rate — are reliability-availability-durability.mdx’s own cited inputs, reused unchanged. Nothing here is a new price; this page’s own contribution is putting the two already-priced axes on one x-axis and reading where they disagree.

02

Drag the dial: the dollar line and the nines line do not even agree on direction

The two curves live in src/sim/cost-of-replication-factor.mjs’s tradeoffCurve, and it computes nothing new — it calls rfcost.mjs’s bill for the dollar side and guarantees.mjs’s shardDurability / correlatedAckFloor for the durability side, at the same N, and reports both side by side.

Predict At a shared-fate probability of 1 in 10,000 per year, replication factor 2 already gives 6.56 nines of durability against independent disk failure — past the 4.0-nine floor that rate sets. What does going from 2 copies to 3 copies buy, on the durability axis, in fact?
Once independent durability has already passed the shared-fate floor, every further copy adds nines on paper that the correlated-failure floor erases in fact — the marginal correlated nine is (numerically) zero from N=3 on in the worked example, and already most of the way there at N=2→3. “4.7 more” is the nominal, uncapped rate this lesson is contrasting against a ceiling. The read/write ratio decides the dollar side, which is a separate axis from durability entirely — that separation is the whole point of drawing two curves instead of one.
Show it without answering

Three things worth saying about what just moved.

The dollar line’s shape depends on the workload; the nines line’s shape does not. Durability here comes entirely from the disk model and the replication factor — nothing about request rate, read fraction or payload size enters shardDurability at all. So switching the workload button redraws the top panel completely and leaves the bottom one identical to the pixel. That is not a missing feature. Durability is a property of how many independent copies of the bytes exist; dollars are a property of how the bytes are moved and stored, and those are different questions with different answers.

The floor moves five orders of magnitude for one slider. A shared-fate rate of 1-in-100 puts the ceiling at 2.0 nines — one copy is already there. A rate of 1-in-100,000,000 puts it at 8.0 nines, which needs the fourth copy to reach. The floor is not a property of the storage system at all; it is a property of how correlated your deploys, credentials and configuration actually are, and that number does not live in rfcost.mjs or in any cloud pricing page — it lives in your own incident history, which is the same conclusion reliability-availability-durability.mdx reaches from the other direction.

”$ per nine” is a trap unless you say which nine. On paper, dollars per nine sit in a narrow band — roughly $180–$315 across N=2 through N=6 on the ledger workload — because both the numerator and the paper denominator are close to linear in N, and a ratio of two near-linear things stays close to constant. In fact, dollars per nine is finite only until the floor, then it is infinite: the denominator stops moving and the numerator does not. Quoting the first number after the floor has been crossed is quoting a price for something that no longer happens.

03

The intuition: fire extinguishers in a building that also has one shared breaker panel

A landlord installs a fire extinguisher on every floor. One floor, one extinguisher: a fire on that floor either gets caught early or it does not, and the odds are whatever one extinguisher buys. Install extinguishers on two floors and — this is the useful fiction — the two fires are independent events, so the chance that both floors catch fire and both extinguishers fail is the product of two small numbers: much smaller than either alone. Install a third and the number gets smaller again, by roughly the same factor each time. Ten floors, ten independent extinguishers, and the odds of a floor burning down are vanishingly small. This is the “near-constant nines per copy” result, playing out one fire alarm at a time.

Now add the fact about the building nobody put on the fire-safety inspection sheet: every floor shares one breaker panel in the basement. A short in that panel starts a fire that reaches every floor’s wiring simultaneously, and no number of per-floor extinguishers helps, because the failure did not start on a floor. If that panel fails once a decade, the building’s real fire risk is bounded below by “once a decade,” no matter how many extinguishers are on how many floors. Ten independent extinguishers and three independent extinguishers protect the building equally well against the actual dominant risk, and the landlord who bought seven extra fire extinguishers bought protection against a cause that was never going to be the one that mattered.

The bill for the extinguishers, meanwhile, is a completely separate ledger. It is very close to $N$ times one extinguisher’s price — mostly linear, occasionally with a volume discount past a certain count that has nothing to do with the fire physics. Buying the tenth extinguisher costs almost exactly what buying the fourth did. It just stopped buying anything.

Redundant, independent units buy safety at a near-constant rate each, right up until something stops being independent — and the thing that stops being independent is very rarely the thing the redundancy was designed to survive. The bill for the units does not know when that happens; only an incident history does.

04

The mechanism: one formula that is exactly N×, one that is not, and one with a floor

Three closed forms, none of them new. Each is called from another file and cited rather than re-derived.

stored bytes = N × logical bytes

The one line on this bill where “N× the cost” is exactly true. From rfcost.mjs’s scheme().storageMultiplier, which is (k+m)/k for a general erasure scheme and collapses to exactly N for plain replication (k=1, m=N−1).

cross-zone replica-write bytes per write = (N − 1) ⁄ N of replicated bytes, while N ≤ AZ count

Established and cited in cross-az-traffic, called here through rfcost.mjs’s reuse of crossZoneReplicaWrites — not re-derived. Past N = AZ, extra copies land in a zone that already holds one, so the share grows sub-linearly rather than continuing at (N−1)/N. On a read-heavy workload the read-side term can fall as N rises, because more copies mean a reader is more likely to find one in its own zone — which is why the combined bill in slot 5 is not even monotonic on that workload.

durability(N) = MTTDL of a k=1-of-N repairable system, in nines, capped at −log₁₀(c)

The uncapped half is guarantees.mjs’s shardDurability, itself repair.mjs’s exact birth–death first-passage chain read off in the unit durability is actually quoted in. It buys a near-constant ~4.7 nines per independent copy, established in reliability-availability-durability.mdx and reproduced here at the same figure (11.25 nines at N=3, to two decimal places). The cap is correlatedAckFloor: a shared-fate probability c adds directly to the annual loss probability, so no copy count pushes the loss probability below c, and no nines figure above −log₁₀(c) survives contact with the number an incident history would report.

Putting the three together is this file’s only new code, and it is bookkeeping rather than mathematics: walk N = 1..6, call rfcost.mjs’s bill and guarantees.mjs’s shardDurability / correlatedAckFloor at each one, and report the marginal dollar and the marginal nine side by side. tradeoffCurve’s whole job is that pairing, because “diminishing returns” is a claim about the pairing and not about either curve alone — the nominal nines-per-copy is not diminishing at all (4.56, 4.69, 4.74, 4.76, 4.78 — if anything it rises slightly), and the dollar line on a read-heavy workload is not even monotonic. What diminishes, sharply, is the in-fact nine once a shared-fate term is added, and that is a fact about the floor, not about either curve’s own shape.

Two things to notice that a single “3× the cost, 3× the safety” sentence cannot hold at once.

The marginal dollar is not linear, and it is not because of a rounding error. $1,439 twice, then $837, then $1,092, then $1,219 — the drop and partial recovery at N=4 is crossZoneReplicaWrites’s own sub-linear behaviour past the zone count (3 zones here), the same formula cross-az-traffic already establishes. The “in fact” column stops moving at N=2 and never moves again. Not “moves slowly” — the correlated nine’s marginal gain from N=3 onward is, numerically, zero. The trade-off: three levers, and only one of them is the replication factor

Two of those rows are worth spelling out.

Adding copies past the floor is not neutral — it is not a wasted lever with zero side effect, it is a dollar-only lever from that point on. The combined bill in slot 5 keeps rising by hundreds of dollars a month per extra copy after the durability line has gone completely flat. That spend is not fraud and it is not always wrong — a fourth copy still buys the cross-zone read capacity cross-az-traffic prices, and a design review that wants “one more copy” for read locality should say so in those words instead of in the words of a durability number that is no longer moving.

The floor is the cheapest lever and the one nobody budgets for, because it has no line item. Cutting a shared-fate rate by an order of magnitude — separating deploy pipelines, splitting credentials so one leak does not reach every replica, testing that a “zone failure” drill does not turn out to be a control-plane failure that takes every zone at once — moves the floor by a whole nine, for free on this page’s dollar model, because none of those things are gigabytes or replica writes. reliability-availability-durability makes the same point from the availability side: the number that actually caps you is rarely the one with a price per unit.

And the honest limit of this page’s model. Every number above assumes independent per-disk failure except for the single shared-fate term c, which is a simplification the sibling lesson states plainly and this page inherits: real correlated-failure structure is richer than one flat add-on probability. What survives that simplification is the shape of the finding — a ceiling exists, it does not move with N, and no amount of “N× the cost” language will make it move — and that shape is not sensitive to how exactly c is modelled.

07

In the wild

Replication factor 3 is close to a universal default, and it predates the cost model on this page. The Google File System paper (Ghemawat, Gobioff and Leung, SOSP 2003) specifies three replicas of each chunk as the default, and HDFS — GFS’s open-source descendant — ships the same default today, documented in the Apache Hadoop HDFS architecture guide. Apache Cassandra’s production guidance and its NetworkTopologyStrategy (already cited in cross-az-traffic’s sources) are built around the same number. None of these documents derive 3 from a durability-versus-dollar curve; it is closer to “the smallest odd number that survives one failure during repair of another,” which is a majority-quorum argument, not a cost argument — this page’s contribution is what that same number costs once cross-zone traffic and a shared-fate floor are both on the table.

Cloud storage products separate the same two axes this page separates, as pricing tiers. Amazon S3’s storage classes and Azure Storage’s redundancy options (LRS: three copies in one datacentre; ZRS: three copies across zones; GRS: an LRS copy plus an asynchronous copy in a second region, six copies total) are sold as distinct products with distinct prices, precisely because “more copies” and “copies spread further apart” buy different things at different costs — durability against disk loss from the first, durability against a whole-datacentre or whole-region shared-fate event from the second. Amazon’s published “eleven nines” of S3 durability is a per-object annual figure of exactly the kind shardDurability computes, quoted for a scheme neither this page nor reliability-availability-durability.mdx has visibility into the exact fragment count of — which is the honest reason this page works from cited disk AFRs and a public replication-factor number rather than from S3’s own internals.

Amazon DynamoDB documents synchronous replication across a fixed count of facilities within a region — three, per AWS’s own DynamoDB and Global Tables documentation — as the mechanism behind its single-region durability and availability claims, the same N-of-N-facilities shape this page’s shardDurability models, with the region’s facilities standing in for the disks.

Erasure coding is the industry’s actual answer to “the storage multiplier without giving up the fault count,” and it is exactly the same page’s rfcost.mjs already models it as N=1 replication’s generalisation (scheme({k, m})), not as a separate mechanism — Backblaze’s own public engineering writing on its Reed-Solomon-coded storage vaults is the clearest account of trading a 1.5× storage multiplier for a wider read fan-out on rebuild, the same k-and-m trade this page’s sibling sim already prices.

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Stored bytes are exactly N× the logical size — the only axis of a replication factor's bill where "N× the cost" is literally correct
  • Cross-zone replica-write traffic is (N−1)/N of replicated write bytes only while N is at or below the zone count; past that, extra copies land in zones that already hold one and the cross-zone term grows sub-linearly
  • Durability buys a near-constant ~4.7 nines per independent copy at a cited disk AFR and a 12-hour rebuild window — that figure is reliability-availability-durability.mdx's, reused here, not re-derived
  • A shared-fate probability c — one bad deploy, one bucket policy, one expired credential that reaches every copy — puts a hard ceiling of −log10(c) nines on durability that no replica count can cross
  • At the commonly-cited c = 1 in 10,000 per year, replication factor 2 and 3 already deliver the same 4.0 nines in fact, even though factor 3 claims 11.25 on paper
  • On a write-heavy workload the combined bill rises with every copy; on a read-heavy one it can fall through N=3 before turning back up, because more replicas also mean more local reads
  • Dollars per nominal nine stay roughly flat because both dollars and nines-on-paper are close to linear in N; dollars per nine in fact go to infinity the moment the floor is hit

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A team argues replication factor 3 is safer than replication factor 2 because it has more "nines" of durability on paper. At a shared-fate probability of 1 in 10,000 per year, is that true in the number that matters?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No. Two independent copies already give 6.56 nines against disk failure alone — already past the shared-fate floor of 4.0 nines — so both configurations sit at 4.0 nines in fact. Factor 3's 11.25 nines on paper is arithmetic performed on a failure mode that is no longer the one that kills the system. The extra copy is still buying something — it is exactly zero on the durability axis and something real on the cross-zone-read axis, which is a different line on the bill.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Which line in a replication factor's bill is exactly linear in N, and which one is not, and why?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Stored bytes are exactly N× — GB times a price, nothing else in it. Cross-zone replica-write traffic is (N−1)/N of write bytes only while N is at or below the zone count (typically 3); past that, extra copies double up inside zones that already hold one, so the term grows sub-linearly. And on a read-heavy workload the read side of the cross-zone bill can fall as N rises — more replicas mean more chance a reader finds one in its own zone — which is why the combined bill in the worked example on this page actually goes down from N=1 to N=3 before it turns back up.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    A team doubles the disks on every node, hoping to buy some durability headroom, and changes nothing else — same replication factor, same rebuild process. Does durability improve?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not on this model, and probably the opposite. Durability here is a function of the replication factor N and the rebuild window, not of disk capacity directly — but bigger disks hold more bytes that have to be re-copied onto a replacement at the same network throughput, which lengthens the rebuild window. And the sibling lesson's own rebuild-window lever enters with an exponent of (N−1): halving the rebuild window is worth 144× at three copies, so doubling it — which is what bigger disks do if nothing else changes — costs almost two full orders of magnitude of durability at the same replica count. Bigger disks are a capacity decision that quietly taxes a reliability number nobody was watching.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The pattern is: independent redundant units buy safety at a near-constant rate each — until a single shared cause sets a hard ceiling that no further unit can cross. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Diversifying a stock portfolio. Standard portfolio theory calls this exactly: idiosyncratic risk — one company's fraud, one factory fire — shrinks at a near-constant rate with every uncorrelated stock added, the equity version of "nines per copy." But systematic risk — a recession, a rate shock, anything that moves the whole market at once — is the shared cause, and it sets a floor no amount of diversification removes. A hundred stocks and three stocks carry the same market-crash exposure, for the identical reason three disks and thirty disks carry the same one-bad-deploy exposure: the redundant units stopped being independent at the one point where it mattered.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
