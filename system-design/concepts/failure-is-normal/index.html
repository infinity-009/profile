Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Failure is normal explained well nowhere else

A drive that fails once a century fails every 3.65 days once you own ten thousand of them

Every reliability figure you are given is per component and per year. Every question you have is about the whole fleet and a much shorter interval. The conversion is one multiplication and it changes what a failure *is*: at Backblaze's published 1.4% annualised rate, a rack of 480 drives loses one every 54 days and a hundred thousand drives lose one every 6.3 hours. Then the harder finding — the failure rate is not constant with age, and a constant-hazard model calibrated on your own first year understates the next five by 3.02×.

01

One number in a datasheet, and a rota built on it

You are buying storage. The drive you have chosen has a published annualised failure rate of 1%, which the vendor is happy to explain: one failure per drive per 100 years. You are also told the datasheet MTTF is 1.2 million hours, which is 137 years. Both figures say the same thing, which is that this drive will outlive you.

You need 10,000 of them.

Something will break every 3.65 days, forever, starting the week they arrive. Not once in a hundred years. A hundred times a year. Nothing about the drive changed between the previous paragraph and this one; the only thing that changed is how many you bought.

Now the same conversion at a real published rate rather than a round one. Backblaze publishes a quarterly table of failure rates for every drive model in its fleet, and the pooled figure across the whole fleet has sat around 1.4% for years. At 1.4%:

The two ends of that table are the same drive. The first row is a component that essentially never fails and the fourth is a continuous process, and the whole distance between them is a factor of 365 in fleet size — which is what a company covers between its Series A and its Series C.

The rota built on the datasheet is planning for the first row. The fleet will be the fourth.

02

The obvious fix — measure your own rate and multiply — and the year it stops working

Fine, you say: datasheets are marketing, so measure. You install 10,000 drives, wait a year, count the failures, and get 210. That is your rate, measured on your drives in your racks with your airflow. Multiply by five for the fleet’s service life and you have 1,050 replacements to budget.

This is the right instinct and it produces a number that is wrong by a factor of three, because it contains one assumption that is doing all the work: that the rate is constant with age. Every MTBF calculation in this tier makes that assumption — a constant hazard is exactly what “mean time between failures” means — and disks do not obey it.

Here is what the field studies actually measured, age by age. Pinheiro, Weber and Barroso published an age-resolved curve for a large Google disk population (FAST 2007), and the shape is not a bathtub with a long flat bottom. It is a dip and then a climb:

These are readings off a published figure rather than numbers copied from a table, so treat them as the shape and the order of magnitude — but the shape is the claim, and it is corroborated independently. Schroeder and Gibson (FAST 2007) looked at large HPC and internet-service populations and reported the same thing in words: replacement rates keep growing with age instead of settling into a flat steady state after the first year.

Integrated across the first year, that curve gives 2.1% — which is your measured 210 failures out of 10,000. Your measurement was right. The multiplication was not.

Predict 10,000 drives, all bought at once, gave you 210 failures in year one. Using the age table above, how many replacements over five years?
Sum the table: 2.1% + 8.0% + 8.6% + 6.0% + 7.0% = 31.7% of the fleet per five years, so 3,170 replacements — 3.02× the 1,050 a constant hazard predicts. The fraction of drives that fail at least once is 1−e^(−0.317) = 27.2% against 10.0%. The trap in options one and two is not the arithmetic, it is the constant hazard: both answers are what you get by treating a measured rate as a property of the drive rather than of the drive's age. And the operationally worse number is not the total — it is that year three alone is 4.1× year one, so the same fleet goes from one failure every 1.74 days to one every 10.2 hours on a schedule you could have written down on day one.

03

The intuition: potholes

A stretch of road develops a pothole every few years. For one stretch that is not a thing you plan for — it happens, someone reports it, somebody comes out and fills it.

A city has a hundred thousand stretches of road. It gets potholes every hour of every day.

So a city does not have a pothole response process. It has a pothole crew: a permanent standing capacity, sized against a rate, running whether or not anything has been reported. The crew is not a sign that the roads are bad. It is what the arithmetic of owning a hundred thousand road segments looks like when someone has done the multiplication.

Two more things carry over exactly, and they are the parts people miss.

The crew has to be bigger than the rate, not equal to it. If potholes appear faster than they are filled, the backlog does not settle at some high-but-stable number — it grows every single day, forever. There is no equilibrium to find. That is not a service-quality complaint; it is arithmetic, and it is the difference between a city with bad roads and a city whose roads are getting worse.

And the rate is a property of the road’s age, not of the road. A city that laid most of its network during one twenty-year building boom has a known future decade in which everything comes due at once. The rate measured in the boom’s first years is the best rate that city will ever see.

04

The mechanism: superposition, the hazard function, and a stability condition

The conversion is exact, not an approximation

If a component fails at rate λ per year and failures are independent, then N of them produce fleet events at rate N·λ — exactly. This is the superposition property of Poisson processes: merge N independent streams and you get one stream whose rate is the sum, and the mean gap between events in the merged stream is its reciprocal:

events per year   = N · AFR
mean gap in days  = 365 / (N · AFR)
fleet size for a given gap = 365 / (AFR · gapDays)

That third line is the one to keep. Failures become daily at 365/AFR components — 36,500 at 1%, 26,071 at Backblaze’s pooled 1.4%, and 12,167 at the 3% that Schroeder and Gibson found in the field. Twelve thousand drives is a few racks.

The model grades this against a Monte Carlo that draws per-component failure times and measures the observed gaps, and knows nothing about superposition: at 200 components and a 50% rate the closed form says 3.650 days and 40,000 measured gaps average 3.652 days.

One notational trap. AFR as published — by Backblaze, and in the field studies — is a rate: failures per component-year of exposure, count divided by drive-years. It is not the probability that a given component fails in a year, which is 1 − e^(−AFR). At 1% the two differ in the fourth decimal place and it does not matter. At the per-machine figure of 56% a year they differ by a quarter, and conflating them is how a fleet gets sized wrong.

Nothing in that table is about the component. It is entirely about N, and every row is reachable from every published AFR by choosing a fleet size. This is what makes the lesson tier-0 rather than a scaling footnote: at any real scale you are not deciding whether to handle failure, you are only deciding whether the handling is a program or a person, and the fleet size decides that for you.

The hazard function, and how wrong the exponential is

A constant hazard λ gives the exponential model: the probability a component has failed by time t is 1 − e^(−λt), and expected failures per component is λt. Everything convenient about MTBF arithmetic comes from that constant.

For a hazard that varies with age, the honest version is the cumulative hazard — the integral of the rate over the period — and for a piecewise-constant curve like the published one that is just a sum of rate × duration:

H(5 years) = 0.03(0.25) + 0.02(0.25) + 0.017(0.5) + 0.08 + 0.086 + 0.06 + 0.07 = 0.317
fraction that ever failed = 1 − e^(−0.317) = 27.2%

Against the constant model calibrated on the observed first year — which is the only rate anyone actually has:

The graded Monte Carlo — 4,000 components aged through the published hazard, counting how many ever failed — returns 28.0% against the closed form’s 27.2%, which is inside the sampling error for 4,000 Bernoulli trials.

Two things fall out of that table, and the second is the one that costs money.

The total is understated by 3.02×, so any five-year budget, spares inventory or capacity plan built from a first-year measurement is short by two thirds.

And the failures are not spread evenly. A fleet bought in one purchase order is an age cohort, so its peak year is a known year. The same 10,000 drives go from one failure every 1.74 days in year one to one every 10.2 hours in year three — from a routine to a rate, crossing the paging ceiling, on a schedule that was predictable on the day the purchase order was signed. One expectation this lesson’s model corrected: the worst single year comes to 0.82× the constant model’s prediction for all five years, not more than it — because the calibration year has to include the infant-mortality months, which pull the first-year figure up to 2.1% from the 1.7% plateau it ends on. Four fifths of a five-year forecast landing inside one year is still the finding.

Repair is a stability condition

Broken components arrive at N·λ and leave at the repair rate. Write c for the number that can be under repair at once and MTTR for how long one takes:

arrivals per day = N · AFR / 365
capacity per day = c / MTTR
ρ = arrivals / capacity

If ρ ≥ 1 there is no steady state. The dead count grows by arrivals − capacity per day, every day, without bound. No availability figure computed from means is meaningful in that regime, because there is no mean to compute. This is not a preference about service quality; it is the condition under which the rest of the arithmetic exists at all.

If ρ < 1, Little’s law fixes the mean broken count at arrivals × W, where W is repair time including the wait for a slot. With slack, W collapses to MTTR and the result is worth memorising because it is independent of fleet size:

broken fraction of the fleet = AFR × MTTR

At 1.4% and a three-day replacement that is 0.0115% — 11.5 drives of 100,000, or 115 of a million. Stretch replacement to a fortnight and it is 0.0537%. Double the fleet and the fraction does not move, which is why this is the form to put in a design document.

Here is the same fleet at three crew sizes. 100,000 drives, 1.4%, three-day replacement, so 3.84 failures a day and break-even at 11.51 slots:

The middle row is the trap: twelve slots is above break-even, so it is technically stable, and it nearly triples both the repair time and the broken count. And then the age curve arrives — year three at 8.6% is 23.56 failures a day and needs 70.68 slots. A crew sized for year one at twelve slots does not merely fall behind in year three; it diverges, by 19.56 drives a day, about 7,140 a year. The graded simulation confirms the divergence is linear rather than saturating: 4,000 components with one repair slot accumulate dead units at the predicted slope for as long as you run it.

Where the human budget goes

The paging ceiling is 365/AFR components — 26,071 drives at 1.4%. The fleet size at which manual handling consumes one full-time engineer, at half an hour of attention per event, is 257,143. The second is 9.86× the first, and that ratio is the same for every component, because both thresholds scale as 1/AFR — it depends only on how much human attention each event takes.

So the ordering is always the same: you lose the ability to page a human long before failure handling looks expensive. Waiting for the cost to justify the automation means running for a factor of ten of fleet growth with a rota that cannot keep up.

06

The trade-off: what treating failure as a rate costs you

The position this lesson lands on is that past a few thousand components, failure handling has to be automatic and capacity has to be sized above the failure rate. Both of those have real costs, and one of them is a genuine loss of information.

You give up the human who would have noticed it was not normal. This is the big one. An automated replacement loop cannot distinguish “a drive died” from “a firmware bug is killing drives in this batch”. Both look like an event on the queue, and the queue is designed to absorb events without telling anyone. So the moment you convert failures into a rate, the rate itself becomes the only signal you have left — and nobody watches a graph that has been flat for a year. The mitigation is specific and cheap: alert on the failure rate and on the repair queue’s utilisation ρ, not on failures. A rate that has tripled is the alarm; the individual failure never was.

You give up capacity, permanently. The dead fraction AFR × MTTR is small, but the capacity you must hold to absorb it is not just that fraction — a failed component’s work has to go somewhere, and in a replicated store the rebuild traffic competes with serving for exactly as long as the repair takes. That is the real cost of a long MTTR: not the 0.0115% that is dead, but the rebuild bandwidth during the window. It is also why mtbf-mttr’s point about repair speed compounding under redundancy is a cost argument as much as an availability one.

You give up the ability to treat a rate change as an incident. Sizing to a rate means small excursions are invisible by design. The stability condition is what saves you here — ρ crossing 1 is unmistakable and terminal, so it makes a good alarm — but by the time ρ crosses 1 you are already accumulating dead hardware. Watching ρ at 0.6 is what you actually want, and that requires someone to have written down what the failure rate should be.

And you can give up correctness if you automate the wrong action. An automatic replacement loop that reacts to a health signal which is itself wrong will happily destroy healthy components at the fleet event rate — which is the whole point of this lesson, applied against you. Automation inherits the multiplication.

Three limits of the arithmetic, stated rather than buried:

Independence is doing all the work. Superposition’s N·λ is exact only for independent failures, and the last row of the published table — a rack event taking 40–80 machines at once — is the counter-example living in the same data set. correlated-failure is where that assumption is taken apart, and it is the first thing to check before trusting any number here.

The age curve is one population. The published figures are drives, mostly from the 2000s and 2010s, in a few large operators’ environments. SSDs fail differently (write-endurance-driven rather than mechanical), and the shape — measure early, get the best number you will ever see — is what transfers, not the percentages.

And the mean is not the experience. These are all means, and the published DRAM result is the sharpest reminder: error counts per DIMM are extremely skewed, so a small number of DIMMs produce most of the errors and the mean per-DIMM rate describes almost no actual DIMM. See percentiles for why that is the normal case rather than an anomaly.

07

In the wild

Backblaze publishes its drive failure rates every quarter, and has since 2013. That is the single most useful artefact in this area, because it is a large fleet’s real annualised failure rates broken out by model with the drive-day denominators shown, which means you can see both the 10× spread across models and the width of the confidence intervals on the small ones. It also demonstrates the design consequence: Backblaze’s storage architecture spreads data across drives with erasure coding specifically so that a drive death is a scheduled background repair rather than an event, which is what “design for failure” means once the rate has been computed.

Schroeder and Gibson’s Disk failures in the real world (FAST 2007) is why nobody quotes MTTF any more. They compared datasheet MTTFs of one to one and a half million hours against observed replacement rates in large HPC and internet-service installations and found the field rates several times higher, and — the part that matters more for this lesson — that replacement rates kept growing with age rather than flattening after the first year. Pinheiro, Weber and Barroso’s Failure Trends in a Large Disk Drive Population (FAST 2007) came from the same conference with the age-resolved curve, and also with the finding that SMART self-monitoring signals were far too weak to be used as a predictor on their own.

Schroeder, Pinheiro and Weber’s DRAM errors in the wild (SIGMETRICS 2009) settled the ECC question empirically. Memory errors turned out to be a hardware-fault phenomenon at rates orders of magnitude above what cosmic-ray models predicted, concentrated in a minority of DIMMs, and common enough per machine-year that a large fleet without ECC is silently corrupting data. That is the clearest case in the whole literature of a per-component rate deciding an architecture: the reason server memory is ECC and consumer memory often is not is a fleet-size argument.

And Google’s published cluster-failure list is the canonical statement of the tier-0 point. The first-year inventory for a new cluster — machine failures in the thousands, rack failures in the dozens, network events, DNS and power — was circulated precisely because it makes “assume failure” into a number, and the whole design of Borg, GFS/Colossus and MapReduce follows from accepting it: tasks are rescheduled rather than protected, data is re-replicated in the background, and no individual machine’s death is an event anyone is told about. That is the automatic-versus-paged choice made once, at the top, on the basis of an arithmetic anyone can redo.

  • A per-year rate becomes a per-day rate at 365/AFR components — 26,071 drives at Backblaze's pooled 1.4%, which is one storage hall rather than a hyperscaler
  • The whole distance from "once a year" to "once a day" is a factor of 365 in fleet size, and real fleets cross it
  • The published figures span 0.3% to 3% per drive-year inside one fleet in one quarter, and Schroeder and Gibson found field replacement rates several times the datasheet MTTF implied
  • Disk hazard is not constant with age: the published curve runs ~3% in the first months, ~2.1% integrated over year one, then 8.0% and 8.6% in years two and three
  • So a constant-hazard model calibrated on the observed first year understates five-year cumulative failures by 3.02× — 3,170 replacements per 10,000 drives rather than 1,050
  • A fleet bought in one purchase order is an age cohort, so its worst year is a known year and it is not the year anyone measured
  • The steady-state broken fraction is AFR × MTTR and does not depend on fleet size at all — 0.0115% at 1.4% and a three-day replacement
  • Repair is a stability condition: if repair capacity is below the failure rate there is no steady state and the dead count grows linearly forever
  • Above about one failure a day a human cannot be in the loop at all, which arrives at 365/AFR components — long before failure handling costs a whole engineer

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A vendor quotes 1% annualised failure rate and you are buying ten thousand units. What have you actually been told?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    That something in the fleet will break every 3.65 days, forever. 1% a year is one failure per component per 100 years, which is the framing that makes it sound irrelevant, and the fleet rate is N·λ by Poisson superposition — 100 failures a year, one every 3.65 days. At Backblaze's published pooled rate of 1.4% the same fleet is 140 a year, one every 2.61 days. Nothing about the component changed; the only thing that changed is how many you bought. The useful inversion is that failures become daily at 365/AFR components: 36,500 at 1% and 26,071 at 1.4%. And the whole distance between "once a year" and "once a day" is a factor of 365 in fleet size, which a growing company covers in a couple of years.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    You measure 210 drive failures in the first year of a fresh 10,000-drive fleet. Plan the next four years.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not by multiplying. Four more years at the observed rate is 1,050 replacements over five, and the published age-resolved curve says 3,170 — an understatement of 3.02×. The measured rates are roughly 3% in the first three months (infant mortality), about 2.1% integrated across year one, then 8.0% in year two and 8.6% in year three, staying above 6% after. Two consequences. First, cumulative failures over five years are 27.2% of the fleet rather than 10.0%. Second, and worse operationally, a fleet bought in one purchase order is an age cohort: its peak year is a known year at 4.1× the first year's rate, so the same 10,000 drives go from one failure every 1.74 days in year one to one every 10.2 hours in year three. The staffing plan built from year one is wrong by a factor of four on a schedule you could have written down in advance.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    What fraction of a hundred-thousand-drive fleet is broken at any given moment, and what does that depend on?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    AFR × MTTR, and nothing else — not fleet size. At 1.4% and a three-day replacement it is 0.0115%, which is 11.5 drives of 100,000. This is Little's law: arrivals are 3.84 failures a day, each occupies a repair slot for three days, so the mean number in the system is 3.84 × 3. Stretch replacement to a fortnight and it is 0.0537%. The reason to hold the number as a fraction is that it survives growth: doubling the fleet doubles both the failure count and the broken count and changes the fraction not at all, so it is the one figure from this lesson you can put in a design document and not revisit.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Your data centre has twelve repair slots and drives break 3.84 times a day, taking three days each. Is that enough?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Barely, and only this year. Break-even is 11.51 slots — the point where repair capacity exactly equals the failure rate — so twelve puts you at 95.9% utilisation. That is stable in the sense that a steady state exists, and it is a bad place to be: the queueing multiplier means a replacement now takes 8.12 days end to end rather than three, and the broken count sits at 31.1 rather than 11.5. Then the age curve arrives. In year three the fleet's rate is 8.6%, which is 23.56 failures a day and needs 70.68 slots. With twelve, the dead count grows by 19.56 a day — about 7,140 drives a year — and it never stops, because there is no steady state to converge to. This is a stability condition, not a service-level preference: below break-even no availability figure computed from means means anything at all.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Why is "we will automate this once it happens often enough to be worth the effort" the wrong test?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the constraint that binds is not effort, it is how often a person can be interrupted. Roughly one failure a day is the ceiling for a human in the loop, and that arrives at 365/AFR components — 26,071 drives at 1.4%. The point at which manual handling consumes one full-time engineer, at half an hour of attention per event, is 257,143 drives: 9.86× further out. So the paging ceiling arrives while the work still looks cheap in headcount terms, and it arrives at the same multiple regardless of the component, because both thresholds scale as 1/AFR. That is why automatic recovery is a design decision made before the fleet is built rather than a cost saving made after — at scale you do not choose whether failure is handled, only whether the handling is a program or a person.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team runs 4,000 GPUs for training. The vendor quotes 2% annualised failure rate for the accelerator and the job checkpoints every six hours. Evaluate the setup with this lesson.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Convert first, then look at the three things the conversion exposes.

    The rate. 4,000 × 2% is 80 failures a year, one every 4.6 days — an "incident" by the regime ladder, so a human can be in the loop. But a training job is not a fleet of independent servers: one accelerator failing stops the whole job, so the relevant mean time between interruptions of the job is that same 4.6 days. Against a six-hour checkpoint interval that is fine — you lose at most six hours of work about eighty times a year, roughly 20 days of the year's compute. Whether that is acceptable is a budget question the numbers now make answerable.

    The thing that breaks the estimate. 2% is the accelerator. The job also depends on host CPU, DRAM, NICs, optics, power and the network fabric, and the machine-level figure that includes all of it is far higher than any component figure — Google's published first-year cluster list works out at about 0.56 failures per machine-year, which across 500 hosts would be 280 a year, one every 1.3 days. Sizing an interruption budget from the accelerator's AFR alone understates it by several times. The published DRAM figures make the same point from the other side: roughly 8% of DIMMs see a correctable error in a year, so a fleet with eight DIMMs per host is seeing thousands of memory events a year whether or not anyone is looking.

    And the age term. If the accelerators were bought in one purchase order they are an age cohort, and if they follow anything like the published disk shape the rate in year three is several times year one's. The checkpoint interval that is comfortable now is the checkpoint interval for the best year the fleet will ever have. Cheap insurance: shorten the interval when the observed rate rises rather than when a job is lost, and measure the rate as a rate rather than counting incidents.

    The decision this lands on is not "buy more reliable accelerators". It is: checkpoint on the machine-level rate rather than the component rate, make restart automatic because 280 a year is well past the paging ceiling, and expect the rate to rise on a schedule.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a per-unit rate that is negligible for one unit becomes a continuous process once you own enough units, and the number of units at which it flips is smaller than intuition suggests — while the per-unit rate itself is usually not constant over the unit's life. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A city's water mains. A given length of pipe bursts perhaps once a century, which is why nobody thinks of a burst as a design event, and a city with tens of thousands of kilometres of main has one somewhere every single day. So a water utility does not have a burst *response plan*, it has a burst *crew* — a permanent standing capacity sized to a rate. And the two further results from this lesson both appear: the rate depends strongly on the pipe's age and material, so a city that laid most of its network in one twenty-year building boom has a known future decade of peak failures, and the crew has to be sized above the failure rate or the backlog of unrepaired leaks grows without bound, which is exactly what non-revenue water figures measure in cities that undersized it.

    Other instances. An airline's fleet, where component removal rates per flight-hour are so low per part and so high per fleet that maintenance is a continuous production line rather than a response to breakage — and where "hard time" limits exist precisely because the hazard rises with cycles. A hospital's mortality figures, where an individual's annual risk is small, the ward's daily count is not, and the whole discipline of separating expected rate from signal exists because staff otherwise treat each event as an incident to explain. A publisher's proofreading, where a typo rate per thousand words that any single page would survive guarantees dozens of errors in a book, which is why the answer is a process and never more care. And in every case the same trap: a rate estimated in the first period of the thing's life is the best it will ever be.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
