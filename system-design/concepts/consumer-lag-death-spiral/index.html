Tier 5 · Moving data around Consumer lag death spiral

A consumer 10% slower than its producer loses data 10× sooner once its own backlog slows it down further — and a 6th consumer on a 5-partition topic processes nothing at all

`queue-vs-log.mdx` established that a consumer tracks its own offset into a retained log, and `append-only-log-mechanics.mdx` established that retention deletes whole segments once they age out regardless of whether anyone has read them. This lesson puts those two facts together: what happens when a consumer's offset falls behind the log's head, and keeps falling behind. It computes three things — the growth rate of that gap (lag), why the growth is often self-reinforcing rather than self-correcting, and the two hard ceilings that follow: a retention window past which the gap becomes permanent data loss, and a partition count past which adding consumers stops helping at all.

Read first — queue-vs-logappend-only-log-mechanics

01

The dashboard showed lag climbing for three weeks before anyone lost data

A team’s consumer processes payment events at 90 messages/second against a producer writing 100/second — a 10% deficit nobody thought was worth paging over. Lag climbs, slowly, on a graph nobody watches closely. Three weeks later, a downstream reconciliation job flags missing events — not delayed, missing. Nothing crashed. No alert fired at the moment it happened, because there was no moment: the consumer’s offset simply fell far enough behind that the messages it still needed had already aged out of the topic’s retention window. A 10% speed deficit and enough time is sufficient, on its own, to lose data — no fault required. This lesson computes exactly how fast that clock runs, why it’s often faster than the naive math predicts, and the second, unrelated ceiling that makes “just add more consumers” fail in a completely different way.

02

Assume a small, steady deficit is a stable, tolerable state

The instinct: 90 msgs/s against 100 msgs/s is “10% behind,” which sounds like a utilization number — the kind of thing that settles into a stable, slightly-elevated backlog and stays there, the way a queue at 90% utilization has a stable (if long) average length. It doesn’t settle. A consumer’s backlog isn’t bounded by a queueing formula that caps it at some multiple of service time — it’s bounded by nothing at all except the two hard limits this lesson computes.

Lag growth, the simple case: if a consumer processes messages more slowly than the producer writes them — by any margin — the backlog grows at exactly the arithmetic difference between the two rates, forever, as long as the deficit persists. lagGrowthRate({ producerRate: 100, consumerRate: 90 }) is a flat 10 messages/second, and it stays 10 whether the deficit has existed for a minute or a month.

Lag growth, the dangerous case: that growth rate isn’t always fixed. If the backlog itself degrades the consumer’s own throughput — write contention on a downstream sink under catch-up load, a batch reader whose per-batch scan cost rises with backlog size — the gap doesn’t just grow, it accelerates. This is cascading-failure.mdx’s shape (a system’s response to trouble generates more of the trouble that caused it) showing up a second time, independently, in a consumer’s own throughput rather than a caller’s retry count.

Predict A consumer processes 90 msgs/s against a producer's 100 msgs/s -- a 10 msgs/s deficit either way. Retention is 3 hours. In one scenario the consumer's throughput never degrades; in the other, backlog past 300 messages degrades it by 60 msgs/s per extra 1,000 messages of backlog. 03

The retry storm's cousin: the backlog IS the cause, and the ceiling has nothing to do with it

cascading-failure.mdx already taught the diagnostic question for a self-sustaining failure: is the thing currently causing this the thing that started it? A self-reinforcing consumer lag answers the same way. The trigger might have been a five-minute blip in the downstream database — long since resolved — but once the backlog grew large enough to itself add write contention, the backlog is now the thing slowing the consumer down, and fixing the original blip changes nothing. This lesson doesn’t re-derive that mechanism; it’s the same shape, aimed at a different knob (consumer throughput instead of retry count).

The partition ceiling is a completely different problem wearing similar clothes. It has nothing to do with degradation, backlogs, or acceleration — it’s a hard structural limit that exists whether or not lag is even growing. A team correctly diagnosing “our consumer is falling behind” and incorrectly reaching for “add more consumers” runs straight into it: past the partition count, every additional consumer changes nothing, ever, no matter how the lag got there.

04

Two independent mechanisms, two independent ceilings

LAG GROWTH        Backlog = produced - processed, accumulating whenever the consumer is slower.
                  LINEAR:  constant rate, the plain difference between the two rates.
                  SPIRAL:  the backlog itself degrades consumer throughput, so the rate itself
                           grows over time -- same shape as cascading-failure's retry loop.

RETENTION CEILING A log only holds `retentionSeconds` of history (append-only-log-mechanics.mjs).
                  Once lag, expressed as seconds-behind, exceeds retention, the messages the
                  consumer still needs are already gone. No error fires at that moment -- the
                  consumer just silently stops seeing messages it should have seen.

PARTITION CEILING A consumer group has at most one active consumer per partition. Requesting more
                  consumers than partitions does not raise capacity at all -- the excess sits
                  idle. The only fix is more partitions, a structural change, not a scaling knob.

Both ceilings can bite the same system at the same time and have nothing to do with each other: adding partitions doesn’t slow the spiral, and fixing the degradation doesn’t raise the partition ceiling. Diagnosing which one is actually in play is the entire operational question.

06

The trade-off — retention costs storage, partitions cost coordination, and neither is free to raise

Widening the retention window buys more time to catch a slow consumer before it loses data, at the storage cost append-only-log-mechanics.mjs and queue-vs-log.mjs already priced exactly — retention × throughput × message size, unconditionally, whether or not any consumer ever actually falls behind. A team that doubles retention “just in case” is paying that bill indefinitely for a scenario that may never occur, which is the same retention-versus-storage trade those two lessons already walked through from the storage side.

Fixing the self-reinforcing mechanism (rather than just watching it) means finding and removing the specific feedback loop — the downstream sink that gets slower under catch-up load, the batch-size choice that scales with backlog — which is a real engineering task, not a configuration change. cascading-failure.mdx’s intervention table applies here with the nouns swapped: the fix that works is breaking the loop, not shortening timeouts or adding capacity elsewhere in the pipeline.

Raising the partition ceiling by adding partitions is not free either. Existing partition assignments change, and if the topic uses a partition key to preserve ordering for some entity (message-ordering-per-key.mdx’s territory), repartitioning can change which partition a given key lands on, which can reorder or duplicate in-flight processing during the transition. It is a real fix for the real ceiling — just not a lever you pull mid-incident without planning it.

07

In the wild

DDIA names the partition ceiling explicitly, as a downside of coarse-grained load balancing — Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., p. 497: “The number of nodes sharing the work of consuming a topic can be at most the number of log shards in that topic, because messages within the same shard are delivered to the same node.” The book’s own example (a message being expensive to process) is the same reasoning this lesson’s rig makes concrete with numbers.

DDIA also names the retention consequence directly, in the same chapter’s “When consumers cannot keep up with producers” section — pp. 498–499: “If a consumer falls so far behind that the messages it requires are older than those retained on disk, it will not be able to read those messages… You can monitor how far a consumer is behind the head of the log and raise an alert if it falls behind significantly.” That monitoring recommendation is exactly what this lesson’s timeToDataLoss computes a concrete answer for, rather than leaving as “keep an eye on it.”

cascading-failure.mdx’s metastability shape is the direct model for the self-reinforcing case — Bronson, Aghayev, Yu, Zhang, “Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems” (HotOS 2021) names the general pattern (a system whose degraded state sustains itself); this lesson computes one more concrete instance of it, in consumer throughput rather than retry-driven request rate, without re-deriving the underlying mechanics.

replication-lag.mdx already treats Kafka consumer lag and replication lag as the same shape — this lesson’s near-transfer question above works out exactly how far that equivalence extends (the backlog-growth math, not the partition-ceiling mechanism).

Now put the page away

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Consumer lag is the gap between the latest offset a producer has WRITTEN and the latest offset a given consumer has actually PROCESSED and committed — if the consumer processes even slightly slower than the producer produces, this gap grows monotonically over time rather than settling at some steady value, because nothing is removing the surplus.
  • Lag growth can be SELF-REINFORCING rather than a fixed rate: a growing backlog can itself slow the consumer down further (a downstream sink taking more write contention as it tries to catch up, a batch reader whose per-batch cost grows with backlog size), which makes the gap accelerate instead of growing at a constant difference — the same general shape `cascading-failure.mdx` names for retry amplification, applied here to a consumer's own throughput instead of a caller's retry rate.
  • A log only retains messages for a configured window (`append-only-log-mechanics.mdx`'s retention). If a consumer's lag — measured as how many seconds of production it is behind — exceeds that window before it catches up, the messages it still needs have already been deleted. This is not merely "the consumer is behind schedule"; it is silent, permanent data loss with no error at the moment it happens.
  • A Kafka-style consumer group can have AT MOST as many actively-consuming members as the topic has partitions, because each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer in the group at a time — this is DDIA's own limit (2nd ed., p. 497): "the number of nodes sharing the work of consuming a topic can be at most the number of log shards in that topic."
  • Requesting more consumers than partitions does not raise throughput at all — the extra consumers are assigned no partition and sit idle. `wastedConsumers = max(0, requested - partitionCount)`: a 6th consumer added to a 5-partition topic processes exactly zero additional messages, forever, regardless of how far behind the group is.
  • The only lever that raises the partition ceiling is adding more partitions — a real, separate operational change (existing partition assignments and any key-based ordering guarantees have to be reconsidered), not a free consequence of requesting more consumers.

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A consumer processes 90 messages/second against a producer writing 100/second — only 10% slower. An on-call engineer, seeing lag climbing slowly on a dashboard, says "it's only 10% behind, that should settle out." Will it settle, and why is the intuition wrong?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It will not settle — it grows without bound at a constant 10 messages/second, forever, because nothing is subtracting from the backlog while the consumer stays slower than the producer. The intuition that fails here is treating "10% slower" like a utilization number that stabilizes below 100% (the way a queue with utilization < 1 has a stable average length) — but a consumer's backlog isn't bounded by anything except retention. It doesn't matter that the deficit is small; given enough time, any constant deficit above zero eventually exceeds any finite retention window, exactly the way `lagGrowthRate` returning a small positive number still drives `timeToDataLoss` to a finite answer rather than infinity.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A team notices consumer lag rising and adds a 6th consumer instance to their consumer group. Lag doesn't move. The team's dashboard shows plenty of spare CPU on the new instance. What's actually happening?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The topic has 5 partitions, and a consumer group can have at most one active consumer per partition — DDIA's own limit. The 6th consumer is assigned zero partitions and sits completely idle; its spare CPU isn't a clue that it's underused, it's a symptom of it having nothing to do at all. `effectiveConsumerCount({partitionCount: 5, requestedConsumerCount: 6})` is exactly 5, and `wastedConsumers` is exactly 1 — this isn't a tuning problem to solve with more instances, it's a hard ceiling that only more partitions can raise.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why can a consumer's own attempt to "catch up" on a growing backlog sometimes make the backlog grow faster instead of slower?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    If the consumer's write-out step targets a shared downstream resource (a database, a cache) that gets slower under contention, then processing MORE backlog per second — the thing catching up requires — also means writing more per second to that same resource, which can push its own latency up and cut the consumer's effective throughput. At this lesson's own worked numbers (100 msgs/s produced, 90/s base consumer rate, a backlog threshold of 300 messages, degrading throughput by 60 msgs/s per extra 1,000 messages of backlog), the self-reinforcing case crosses a 3-hour retention window roughly 10× faster than the same starting deficit would if consumer throughput never degraded (about 3 hours versus about 30) — the backlog isn't a passive symptom here, it's actively making its own cause worse.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    `replication-lag.mdx` already computes a follower's replication lag using the same shape of formula this lesson uses for consumer lag — "the same queue with different nouns," in that lesson's own words. If the two lags share a formula, does replication lag also have this lesson's partition-ceiling problem — can a leader-follower replication setup run out of "partitions" to add followers to?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — the shared formula is only the BACKLOG-GROWTH shape (arrival rate versus service rate, accumulating a gap when the consumer/follower is slower). The partition ceiling is a completely separate mechanism specific to how Kafka-style consumer GROUPS divide work: it exists because a partition can only be assigned to one group member at a time, a rule about *parallelizing consumption of one stream*. Replication has no equivalent concept to "partition count" limiting how many followers a leader can support — a leader can in principle replicate to as many followers as it has bandwidth for, because each follower gets a full copy of the *same* stream rather than a disjoint shard of it. The lesson here is that sharing one piece of math (backlog growth) does not mean two systems share every consequence of that math — the retention/data-loss consequence transfers, the partition-ceiling consequence does not, because it depends on a mechanism (partitioned parallel consumption) that plain leader-follower replication never had in the first place.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a backlog that grows because a worker is slightly slower than the work's arrival rate can make the worker even slower, because the backlog itself becomes part of the worker's own overhead — and past some point, adding more workers stops helping because there is a hard limit on how many can be usefully assigned at once. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    An executive assistant triaging an inbox that has fallen behind. If new messages arrive slightly faster than the assistant can process them, the unread count grows without bound rather than leveling off — and once it's large, simply FINDING the important message among the backlog takes longer than it did when the inbox was current, which slows down every subsequent reply and makes the backlog grow faster still (the self-reinforcing part). Hiring more assistants doesn't fix it past a point either: if the work is organized into a fixed number of separately-manageable inboxes or projects (the "partitions"), a 6th assistant added on top of 5 already-assigned projects has no project of their own to own end-to-end — splitting one project across two people usually creates more coordination overhead than it saves, the same reason a Kafka partition isn't handed to two consumers at once. The only real fix past that point is restructuring the work into more independent projects, not hiring more people against the same five.
    Did you have that?
