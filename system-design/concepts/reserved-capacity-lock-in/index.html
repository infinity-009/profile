Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Reserved capacity lock-in

At an 18-month mean workload life, a 3-year 60%-off commitment still nets $545,361 more than on demand — shrink that to 14 months and the shallower 1-year discount wins while the deeper one loses $18,482

Provisioned-vs-spot's break-even is exact and it is also silent about time: a discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d, full stop, no term length in it. But a commitment is signed once and a workload's shape does not hold still for one to three years — it gets re-architected, downsized, or moved to a new instance family, and none of that un-signs the term. Model the time until that happens as a single number, a mean lifetime, and the deeper 60%-off three-year discount that needs only 40% utilisation turns out to be exposed to three times the term length a shift can land inside — so there is a real band of workload lifetimes where the shallower, safer one-year commitment wins and the deeper one already loses.

Read first — provisioned-vs-spot

01

A three-year commitment, signed at 95% utilisation

Twenty accelerated nodes, running a training pipeline at high, steady load. On demand they cost $4.00 an hour each, so $70,080 a month. A three-year committed-use discount is on offer at 60% off, and the team’s own utilisation over the last quarter has been north of 90%. The math on the ticket is provisioned-vs-spot’s own exact identity: a discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d, so 60% off needs only 40% utilisation to pay — and this fleet is running at more than double that. The commitment is signed. Three years, all upfront, 60% off.

Eighteen months in, the pipeline is rewritten. Not shut down — rewritten, onto a different architecture that needs a different mix of nodes, at roughly 40% of the footprint the reservation was sized for. The other 60% of the committed capacity did not disappear from the bill. It is still being paid for, every hour, for the eighteen months of term that remain, because a commitment is not a price on usage — it is a price on time, and the clock does not care what changed underneath it.

Run the arithmetic all the way through, and the fleet still comes out ahead of on demand for the whole three years — $545,361 ahead, in this scenario. But $286,431 of the money paid for that result bought capacity nobody drew on after the rewrite. Both numbers are real. The break-even utilisation check that approved this commitment saw neither of them, because that check has no term length in it at all.

Every dollar figure and discount on this page is a declared input, dated 2026-08, marked low confidence, and — for the discounts specifically — the same P.commit1yDiscount / P.commit3yDiscount values provisioned-vs-spot already declared, reused rather than re-guessed. Substitute your own workload’s mean lifetime, residual fraction and discount — every finding below is a ratio, a utilisation, or a break-even, and none of them depends on the absolute price being right.

02

The break-even was never wrong. It was answering a different question.

The belief this lesson is breaking is not “the break-even math is incorrect.” It is exact, and provisioned-vs-spot already proved it: 1 − d, no price, no workload, no term length. The belief being broken is “today’s utilisation is the input that identity needs.” It is not. The identity needs the utilisation averaged over the whole term you are about to sign, and today’s number is a sample of length one from a process that runs for one to three years.

What decides whether today’s sample generalises is a single new quantity: how long the workload keeps this shape before something changes it — a re-architecture, a downsizing, a migration to new hardware, the product itself being retired. Model the time until that happens as memoryless, the same assumption provisioned-vs-spot makes about a machine being reclaimed, and the whole problem needs only one number: the mean lifetime. Not a schedule of when the shift happens — just its average rate.

Before the shift, the committed capacity is used exactly as it was sized: fully. After the shift, only a fraction of it is still useful — call it the residual fraction, ρ — because the new shape of the workload does not need all of what was reserved for the old one. The committed bill never moves. Only the value coming back for it does, and the gap between the two, integrated over however long that gap is expected to last, is the stranded commitment cost this lesson prices.

Predict Same 20 nodes, same $4.00/hr, same 60% (3yr) and 35% (1yr) discounts, same family-locked commitment. The workload's mean lifetime before it changes shape is 14 months. Which commitment, if either, beats on demand?
The one-year commitment's effective utilisation over its own term comes out to 68.8%, clearing its 65% break-even. The three-year commitment's effective utilisation over its (three times longer) term comes out to 39.1%, just short of its 40% break-even. Same discounts, same price, same workload — the only thing that differs is which term the shift has to land inside, and a 14-month mean lifetime lands inside the three-year term far more often than it lands inside the one-year term. The deeper discount and the lower break-even it buys are not a strictly better deal: they are bundled with three times the exposure to exactly the risk this lesson prices.
Show it without answering

The crossing point is not a rounding effect. At a 14-month mean lifetime the two verdicts are on opposite sides of “beats on demand,” by a comfortable margin in dollars either way — +$26,621 against −$18,482 — not a coin flip that a slightly different price would flip back. The deeper discount is exposed to more risk in direct proportion to how much longer its term is, which is a one-line consequence of the math and not something either provisioned-vs-spot’s break-even or a purchasing team’s spreadsheet has any reason to compute, because neither one carries a term length.

03

The lease you sign because the rate card looks good

You are choosing between two ways to rent office space. Option one: a fixed desk, at one specific address, for three years, at a steep discount off the month-to-month rate. Option two: a membership at a serviced-office chain — any of their locations, any city — at a smaller discount, but the desk is not tied to an address.

Sign the fixed lease when you are certain your team, at this size, in this city, on this floor plan, still exists in three years. If that is true, the fixed lease is strictly the better deal — the deeper discount, banked for the whole term, with nothing lost.

But “your business does not change shape in three years” is the load-bearing assumption, and most businesses do not get to make it. A team doubles, a department gets cut, the company changes offices because the lease on the building itself changed hands. None of that ends the lease you signed. It just means the desks you are still paying for are not desks anyone is sitting at, for however many months remain — and “however many months remain” is doing all the work, because it is proportional to how long the term is. A one-year lease that goes empty for four months has wasted a third of itself. A three-year lease that goes empty for the same four months, on the same underlying event, has wasted a ninth.

The serviced-office membership does not have this problem, because it was never tied to a shape. The same monthly commitment reallocates to wherever the team actually is. It is a worse deal on the month you signed it, at the headline discount, and a much better deal on the month your business turns out to look different than it did then. Which one wins depends on a number neither rate card prints: how long you expect to keep the shape you have today.

04

The stranded-cost identity, and where it comes from

Reused, exactly: the static break-even

Nothing about the term length or the shift risk changes the core identity provisioned-vs-spot derived. It is imported unchanged:

break-even utilisation = 1 − discount

commitmentBreakEvenUtilisation, from spot.mjs, called directly rather than re-derived. 60% off needs 40%. 35% off needs 65%. Exact, with no price and no term length in it — which is exactly why it cannot see the risk this lesson is about, and does not claim to.

New: how much of the term is spent before the workload’s shape changes

Model the time until a shift as exponential with hazard μ per hour — memoryless, the same renewal assumption provisioned-vs-spot makes about a machine’s preemption, aimed here at a workload’s shape rather than a machine’s uptime. For a term of H hours, the expected hours spent before the shift is the same identity as E[min(X, S)] in that lesson’s stretch derivation, here computed directly as E[min(τ, H)] for τ ~ Exp(μ):

active hours = ∫₀^H P(τ > t) dt = (1 − e−μH) / μ

At μ → 0 (a workload that never changes shape) this tends to H — the whole term, by direct expansion of the exponential. At large μH (a shift that lands well inside the term with near certainty) it tends to 1/μ, the mean lifetime itself: past that point the term length has stopped mattering, because the shift already happened long before the term could have.

Everything downstream is this integral, reused three ways.

Effective utilisation, and the stranded cost it implies

Usage runs at full rate until the shift, then at a residual fraction ρ of it for the rest of the term. Expected total usage-hours over the whole term is active + ρ · (H − active); divide by H for a utilisation:

effective utilisation = ρ + (1 − ρ) · (active / H)

At ρ = 1 (nothing is ever stranded — a fully flexible, spend-based commitment) this is exactly 1, whatever the hazard. At ρ = 0 (a shift destroys all remaining value) it is exactly active/H, the plain fraction of the term spent before the shift. Every real commitment sits between those two poles.

And the dollar figure that a bare utilisation number cannot show — how much was paid for capacity that produced nothing — falls straight out of the same integral:

stranded commitment cost = level × (1 − discount) × price × (1 − ρ) × (H − active)

The committed hourly rate, times the share of capacity going unused after the shift (1 − ρ), times the expected number of hours that gap persists (H − active, the complementary integral). This is the number neither the sales conversation nor the break-even check produces, because both stop at a discount percentage and a utilisation ratio.

Composing all three against the imported break-even gives the full verdict function this lesson’s rig draws — effective utilisation against 1 − d, plus the stranded dollars either way — computed by commitmentVerdict and swept across both terms by compareTerms in src/sim/reserved-capacity-lock-in.mjs.

The 3-year term is not just “the 1-year term with a better discount”

Because the three-year term’s H is three times the one-year term’s, and (H − active) grows with H, the three-year commitment is mechanically exposed to more expected stranded hours than the one-year commitment, for the identical mean lifetime. It usually still wins on net dollars, because its discount is deeper by more than that exposure costs — but “usually” is doing real work, and the 14-month crossover in slot 2 is the case where it does not. minMeanLifeHoursToBeat inverts the whole identity by bisection: the shortest mean lifetime a term can survive and still beat on demand, given its own discount and a residual fraction. On a family-locked commitment that number is 12.0 months for the one-year term and 14.5 months for the three-year term — the three-year commitment needs a more durable workload to pay off than the one-year commitment does, exactly because it is betting on durability for three times as long.

06

What this model does and does not price

The exponential is a convenience, not a measurement. Real workload lifetimes are not memoryless — a workload that has already lasted eighteen months is probably more likely to last a few more, not equally likely to end in the next hour as it was on day one, the opposite of what an exponential assumes. That biases this model toward understating a mature, stable workload’s durability and toward overstating a brand-new one’s. Where you have an actual distribution of past project lifetimes, use its mean directly; the shape of the tail matters for the variance of the outcome, not for the identity above one it, because the identity only ever needed the mean.

A shift is modelled as a single step, and real ones are gradual. Workloads usually shrink in stages — a slow migration, a phased rollout of the replacement — rather than dropping from full use to ρ in one instant. The stranded-cost identity still applies to each stage if you re-run it with that stage’s own residual fraction and remaining term, but nothing here composes multiple shifts automatically.

Residual fraction is a declared input standing in for a real, well-documented product distinction, and the exact numbers here are illustrative, not a specification. What is real: reservations denominated in a specific instance family and region carry little of their value across a shape change, while commitments denominated in a dollar-per-hour spend rate carry most of it, because they keep applying as long as total eligible usage clears the committed rate. Check your own provider’s current mechanism — the sources below name the real, documented products this distinction maps to — rather than trusting ρ = 0.05 or ρ = 0.92 as anything but a stand-in.

Exit options exist and this model prices none of them. A reservation resold on a secondary market, an early-termination fee that caps the loss instead of running it to the term’s end, an exchange that recovers some value even from a family-locked commitment — all of these change the effective ρ or cut the remaining H short, and a team facing an actual stranded commitment should look for the specific mechanism their provider offers before accepting the full stranded-cost number this model computes.

This is the risk half of a purchasing decision, not the whole decision. The static break-even from provisioned-vs-spot still has to be cleared first — a workload that never clears 1 − d even at its current, unchanging shape has no business being committed regardless of how durable it is. This lesson only adds the second question, asked after the first one already said yes: for how long does that “yes” have to keep being true, and what does it cost if it stops being true early?

07

In the wild

The family-locked vs. spend-based split is a real, current product line, not a modelling convenience. Cloud providers sell reserved capacity two ways: tied to a specific resource configuration in a specific region, and as a commitment to a level of spend that applies across whatever configuration is actually running. The first kind is cheaper to build (the provider knows exactly what it is reserving) and the most exposed to exactly the risk this lesson prices; the second is more flexible for the buyer and is priced accordingly. Check your own provider’s current terms for which products fall on which side — the naming differs by provider and changes over time, which is itself evidence for the lesson’s point: the flexibility is the product feature that matters, not the brand name on it.

Exchange and resale mechanisms exist, and they exist because stranding is a known, recurring problem, not an edge case. More than one major provider documents a way to exchange a reservation for a different configuration (typically restricted to one of equal or greater value, and not a full refund), and at least one runs a secondary marketplace where unused reservations can be resold rather than left to run out unused. Their existence is itself a data point: providers built infrastructure specifically to help customers recover value from a commitment whose workload changed shape, which would not be worth building if this lesson’s failure mode were rare.

The square-root checkpoint interval and this lesson’s hazard model are the same piece of applied probability wearing two different hats. Both take a memoryless interruption — a machine reclaimed, a workload re-architected — and both need only its rate, never its schedule. Young’s 1974 result (provisioned-vs-spot) asks how often to checkpoint against that rate; this lesson asks how much term length is safe to sign against the same kind of rate. Same renewal equation, aimed at a different decision.

Financial contracts with an early-termination penalty are the same shape as a commitment with no exit at all, with one term changed. A mortgage prepayment penalty, a mobile-phone contract cancellation fee, a car lease’s excess-mileage and early-return charges: all of them cap the buyer’s downside at a known number instead of letting it run to the full remaining term, which is exactly the missing mechanism named in slot 6. Where such a mechanism exists, it replaces (H − active) in the stranded-cost identity with min(H − active, time to the penalty threshold) — a smaller number, and a computable one, once the penalty’s own terms are read.

    The one-year commitment still wins, netting $26,621, because its effective utilisation (68.8%) clears its own break-even of 65%. The three-year commitment now loses, netting −$18,482, because its effective utilisation (39.1%) falls just short of its own break-even of 40% — a workload that outlives the one-year term does not outlive the three-year one. This is the finding the lesson is built to show: the deeper discount and the lower break-even it buys are not a strictly better deal, because they come bundled with three times the term length for a shift to land inside. At a 14-month mean life the answer flips depending only on which term you signed, with the identical discount percentages, the identical price, and the identical workload.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    "Our utilisation today is 95%, well above the three-year break-even of 40%, so a three-year commitment is obviously safe." What is wrong with that sentence?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It compares today's utilisation to a threshold that has to hold on average across the whole term, and treats today's number as if it were that average. 95% today says nothing about whether the workload still exists in this shape in month 30 — the break-even utilisation is a static ratio with no term length in it, while whether you actually clear it depends on how long 95% persists against how long the term runs. A workload at 95% utilisation with an 18-month mean lifetime, committed for three years, can still fall to an effective utilisation in the 60s once the expected stranded hours after a shift are counted — comfortably above this lesson's example break-even of 40%, but not automatically, and a shorter mean life or a more family-locked commitment can put the same 95%-today workload underneath its own break-even by the time the term is scored. "High utilisation now" and "durable utilisation for the length of this specific term" are different claims, and only the second one is what the commitment is actually betting on.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    Name a non-computing situation with the same two knobs — a discount for committing to a fixed term, and a separate question of how much of that commitment survives if your needs change shape before the term ends.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A multi-year office lease at one specific address, against a serviced-office membership good at any of a provider's locations. Both can be sold at a discount to a month-by-month rate for committing to a term. But if the company doubles in size, moves teams, or gets acquired, the fixed-address lease is family-locked — the discount was for that floor, and if the shape of the business changes, the remaining months of rent buy almost nothing (ρ near 0, the lease can sometimes be sublet, at a loss, the way a Convertible Reserved Instance can be exchanged at less than face value). The serviced-office membership is spend-based — the same committed monthly fee reallocates to a different floor, a different city, even a different provider location, so a shape change barely dents its value (ρ near 1). Nobody signs the fixed-address lease because the desks are cheaper per month; they sign it because the discount looks attractive today and the multi-year commitment to a specific shape is the part that does not show up on the rate card. Other instances of the same pair: a multi-year phone contract bundled with one specific handset versus a SIM-only rolling plan; buying a block of theatre season tickets to one venue versus a subscription service redeemable at any venue in a network.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production.
