Tier 7 · Security, privacy, compliance PII classification & tokenisation

A classifier that over-flags more but misses less cuts a 40-field schema's handling cost from 74 units to 23, because a miss costs fifty times what a needless flag does

A classification pass makes two mistakes that have nothing in common except being wrong: missing real PII leaves it exposed everywhere ordinary data is exposed, and over-flagging ordinary data taxes it with controls it never needed. Once a field IS flagged, tokenisation decides how many of the systems that touch it ever see the real value — most of them don't have to. And the one lever that beats both isn't a smaller number: a field never collected can be missed by no classifier and leaked by no service, because it was never written down.

Read first — deletion-in-a-distributed-storeenvelope-encryption

01

The scanner protected the field nobody was worried about

A quarterly privacy review ran a keyword-based PII scanner across the customer table: 40 columns, and the scanner’s job was to flag which ones needed encryption, restricted access and slower, audited joins. It flagged loyalty_tier — an enum with four values — because the column name matched a pattern meant to catch tax identifiers. Encrypting it, wiring it through the access-review workflow and rewriting the two reports that joined on it took a sprint.

It did not flag support_notes, a free-text field where an agent had, eleven months earlier, pasted a customer’s callback number directly into a ticket because the structured phone column was empty and the number needed to go somewhere. The scanner looked for column names and types, not for what ended up inside a TEXT field at runtime. The real phone number sat there, unencrypted, readable by every service with ordinary read access to support tickets, until an unrelated audit found it by accident.

Nothing about this was a tooling failure in the sense of a bug. The scanner did exactly what it was built to do. The two mistakes — protecting a field that needed nothing, and missing one that needed everything — are not two versions of the same error. They don’t even trade off against each other in the way “more cautious” and “less cautious” usually do, and pricing them as if they did is the whole subject of this lesson.

02

Price the two mistakes, don't just count them

Predict A 40-field schema is 35% genuinely PII. Classifier A misses 10% of it and over-flags 15% of the rest; classifier B misses 2% and over-flags 35%. If a missed field costs 50× what a needlessly flagged one costs, which is cheaper overall?
A misses 1.4 fields (70 units at 50× each) plus 3.9 overflagged (3.9 units) = 73.9. B misses only 0.28 fields (14 units) plus 9.1 overflagged (9.1 units) = 23.1. B is wrong about more fields in total — 9.38 versus 5.3 — and costs two-thirds less, because it moved its errors into the cheap direction.
Show it without answering

Three things the rig is built to make visible.

The four buckets are not two. “Correct” splits into protected and correctly-ignored; “wrong” splits into missed and over-flagged. Reporting a classifier’s accuracy as one number collapses two failure modes that cost completely different things, in completely different places — one is a security exposure, the other is an operations tax.

Moving the miss rate down while the overflag rate goes up is usually a win, not a wash. The default schema goes from 73.9 units to 23.1 when the classifier trades a lower miss rate for a higher overflag rate — because 50 units of the 73.9 was one side of the ledger. This is the whole argument for tuning a PII classifier toward recall even when it visibly increases how often it’s wrong about ordinary fields.

The ratio between the two costs is a business decision, not an engineering one, and the slider makes that explicit: at cost-ratio = 1 (a missed field costs the same as a needless flag) there is no argument for recall at all — you’d tune for accuracy. The 50× default is a modelling choice representative of the shape (a security exposure usually costs far more than a masked join being slightly slower), not a number this lesson claims to have measured for any real organisation.

03

The intuition: a customs officer at two different borders

Imagine a customs officer checking bags for a banned substance, at two different crossings.

At the first crossing, a false alarm means five extra minutes while a bag is re-scanned. At the second, a false alarm means detaining a passenger for questioning. Any officer sensibly trained on the first crossing will accept a much higher false-alarm rate there than they would at the second — not because the substance is different, but because being wrong in the cheap direction costs five minutes, and being wrong in the expensive direction costs a person’s afternoon.

PII classification is exactly this, run against every column in a schema instead of every bag. Over-flagging loyalty_tier is the five-minute re-scan: a slower join, an unnecessary access review, an engineer mildly annoyed at a migration. Missing support_notes is the detained passenger: a real phone number sitting in cleartext, exposed to whatever that column’s ordinary access already reaches, for as long as nobody notices.

Once a field clears the checkpoint and is correctly flagged, a second, separate question opens up: who, downstream, actually needs to see what’s in the bag? Most of a customs process — the transport, the storage, the manifest — never needs to open it at all; it only needs to know that it passed. That’s tokenisation, and it’s a different question from whether the bag should have been checked in the first place.

And a third question sits upstream of both: did this passenger need to carry that item across the border at all, or was it packed on the assumption that customs might one day want to see it? That’s minimisation, and it’s the only one of the three that removes the bag from the suitcase before anyone gets to the checkpoint.

Classification decides WHICH fields get protected, and its two error directions cost different amounts almost every time — treat them as one accuracy number and you tune blind. Tokenisation decides, for a field that IS protected, HOW FEW places ever see the real value. Minimisation decides HOW MUCH exists to protect at all, and it’s the only lever of the three that reaches zero rather than a smaller number.

04

The mechanism: a cost function, a compound probability, and Little's Law with the tap off

totalCost = (piiFields × missRate) × costPerMiss + (nonPiiFields × overflagRate) × costPerOverflag

Two independent products, not one score. Driving either rate to zero moves only its own term — which is why a single “classification accuracy” percentage can improve while the number that actually matters (exposure cost) gets worse.

breach exposure = 1 − (1 − p)^k, k = services holding the real value

The same “at least one” compound survival function used all over this curriculum for a different event each time (a cache miss, a corrupted replica, a straggling task) — here the event is “this service’s datastore is compromised.” Tokenisation’s whole effect is making k smaller than the total service count N, without touching p.

standing inventory = collectionRate × retentionPeriod, or exactly 0 if collectionRate = 0

Little’s Law, reused unchanged from kernel.mjs — a population is an arrival rate times a residence time. Every other lever in this file can only shrink one of the two factors. Not collecting the field zeroes the rate directly, which is the only route to an exact zero.

The three levers operate on different objects, which is why none of them substitutes for another.

LeverOperates onReducesCannot do
ClassificationWhich fields get handlingFields left with zero controlsNothing once a field is correctly flagged — it hands off to the next lever
TokenisationHow many services see a flagged field’s real valueThe count of places that can leak itAnything for a field that was never flagged, or never collected
MinimisationWhether the field is collected at allThe population that exists to protectNothing retroactively — it’s a decision made before the first row is written

Classification’s asymmetry is the one most teams get backwards in practice. A scanner tuned for precision (few false alarms) looks better in a demo and worse in an incident, because precision is optimising the cheap error at the expense of the expensive one. This is the same shape as a failure detector tuned on mean delay instead of tail behaviour: the metric that’s easy to make look good is rarely the one the cost function actually cares about.

Tokenisation is worth pairing with envelope encryption, and they solve different problems. The envelope-encryption lesson’s data key protects a value that sits in one place — encrypt it, and only someone with the key can read the ciphertext wherever it’s copied. A token vault instead makes most copies not exist as the real value at all: the checkout service, the analytics pipeline, the support dashboard and the fraud model each hold a token that means nothing outside the vault, so there is no ciphertext for them to mishandle in the first place. A real system layers both — the vault itself typically encrypts the mapping it stores, at rest, using exactly the envelope-encryption pattern — because tokenisation protects everything outside the vault and envelope encryption protects what’s inside it.

And a token vault inherits envelope encryption’s hardest constraint under a different name. A data key can only be destroyed at the granularity it was issued — envelope-encryption’s whole “blast radius” argument. A token mapping has the identical constraint: you can only revoke a token at the granularity the vault issued it. Token per subject, and deleting one subject’s token is a single delete. Token shared across subjects — a common shortcut when a value like a device fingerprint is reused — and revoking it takes every subject who shares it down at once, which is deletion-in-a-distributed-store’s crypto-shredding constraint again, on a mapping table instead of a data key.

What to set.

  • Score a classifier on missed and over-flagged fields separately, never as one accuracy number. A scanner that improved from 90% to 96% “accurate” can have moved entirely in the wrong direction if the 6-point gain was closing false alarms while missed fields held steady.
  • Set the miss/overflag cost ratio explicitly, in writing, before tuning anything. It is a business call about what a breach costs relative to an operational tax, and leaving it implicit means whoever wrote the scanner’s default thresholds made the call by accident.
  • Decide the token vault’s granularity — per-subject or shared — before the mapping table has real rows in it, for the same reason a data key’s granularity has to be decided at write time: neither can be split finer after the fact without touching every value.
  • Ask “do we need to collect this” before asking “how do we protect this.” It is the only question in the set whose answer, chosen correctly, means the other two never come up for that field.
06

The trade-off: three levers on three different objects

Classification buys correctness and sells nothing by itself — its cost is entirely in tuning it wrong. There is no standing operational cost to classifying well; the cost only appears when the miss rate and overflag rate are set (explicitly or, usually, by accident) to the wrong ratio for what the business actually cares about. This makes it the cheapest of the three levers to fix once diagnosed, and the easiest to leave broken indefinitely, since a misconfigured threshold produces no error, no alert and no support ticket — only a slowly accumulating set of fields sitting in the wrong bucket.

Tokenisation buys a smaller blast radius and sells one hard dependency: the vault. Every service that used to hold the real value now makes a call (or reads a cache) to resolve a token, which means the vault’s availability is now load-bearing for every one of those services’ happy paths, and the vault’s own access controls are the whole security model. This is a real operational cost, and it is a fixed one — it does not scale with how much PII you add, unlike minimisation.

Minimisation buys zero exposure for what you skip and sells whatever that data would have been worth. A field never collected cannot power a feature, a report or a machine-learning signal built on it. This is not a rounding error the way it can feel in a design review — it is often the entire reason the field was proposed. The lesson isn’t “collect less always wins,” it’s that minimisation is the only lever with a zero on the table, so it deserves to be argued about explicitly rather than defaulted away by “we might need it later.”

07

In the wild

NIST SP 800-122 is the classification playbook this lesson’s cost function is a simplified version of. Rather than a binary PII/not-PII label, it scores each field’s confidentiality impact from factors including identifiability, the sensitivity of the specific data field, the context it’s used in, and how easily it can be combined with other data to re-identify someone — which is exactly the point that a keyword scanner checking column names cannot see: support_notes and loyalty_tier are not equally hard to classify from a name alone, and the standard’s answer is to score risk from context, not from the schema.

PCI DSS’s tokenisation guidance is where “most of the system never touches the real value” became a formal compliance argument rather than an engineering nicety. The PCI Security Standards Council’s tokenisation guidance documents exactly the mechanism modelled here: a service that only ever handles a token is out of the Cardholder Data Environment audit scope entirely, because it cannot leak what it never received. Scope reduction — not just risk reduction — is the reason large payment integrations are built around a vault rather than passing the real card number through every hop.

GDPR’s data minimisation principle (Article 5(1)(c)) is minimisation’s legal name, and its enforcement tier is not the cheap one. The regulation requires personal data to be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary” for its stated purpose. Kleppmann & Riccomini note that this runs directly against the “big data” instinct to collect everything speculatively in case it’s useful later, and — in the words of Bruce Schneier, whom they quote — that “data is the pollution problem of the information age, and protecting privacy is the environmental challenge” (Kleppmann & Riccomini, ch. 14, p. 595). Article 83 sets two fine tiers, and violations of core principles like minimisation sit in the higher one: up to 4% of global annual turnover or €20 million, whichever is greater — a fine tier, not a rounding error, for the lever that only costs you upside you chose not to collect.

DDIA makes the same point about minimisation from the systems side, independent of any regulation: “once all the risks are taken into account, it might be reasonable to decide that some data is simply not worth storing.” (Kleppmann & Riccomini, ch. 1, p. 24). The book has no treatment of classification or tokenisation as PII-handling mechanisms specifically — its one use of “tokenise” is splitting text into words for a search index, an unrelated meaning — so this lesson leans on NIST and PCI for those two, and on DDIA only for the minimisation argument, which is squarely in its “trade-offs and ethics” territory.

Model and numbers — every figure computed by src/sim/pii-classification-tokenisation.mjs and asserted in src/sim/pii-classification-tokenisation.test.mjs (12 tests): the four-bucket classification split and its asymmetric cost, the recall-tuned classifier’s 73.9 → 23.1 swing, the 1 − (1 − p)^k tokenisation exposure and its 5.4× reduction at k = 2, and the Little’s-Law standing inventory reaching exactly zero at collected: false.

NIST Special Publication 800-122, Guide to Protecting the Confidentiality of Personally Identifiable Information (PII) — the PII confidentiality-impact framework (identifiability, data field sensitivity, context of use, ease of re-identification) that this lesson’s classification cost function simplifies into two rates.

PCI Security Standards Council, Information Supplement: PCI DSS Tokenization Guidelines — tokenisation as a formal scope-reduction mechanism for the Cardholder Data Environment, the source of this lesson’s “most services never touch the real value” framing.

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), Article 5(1)(c) and Article 83 — the data minimisation principle by name, and the higher fine tier (4% of global turnover or €20 million) that violations of core principles including minimisation fall under.

Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., ch. 1, p. 24 and ch. 14, p. 595 — the data-minimisation argument (“some data is simply not worth storing”) and the Bruce Schneier quote on data as a pollution problem, both quoted directly above. Verified against the book’s own page-footer markers; DDIA has no coverage of classification or tokenisation as PII mechanisms specifically (its only other use of “tokenise” is text search, unrelated).

This repo — envelope encryption for the data-key blast-radius argument tokenisation is layered with and the “granularity fixed at write time” constraint both mechanisms share, and deletion in a distributed store for the “smaller number, not zero” trap minimisation avoids and the crypto-shredding constraint a shared token inherits.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Classification has two failure directions and they are not symmetric: under-classifying leaves real PII with no controls at all, over-classifying taxes ordinary data with controls it never needed
  • A 40-field schema that is 35% genuinely PII, missing 10% of it and over-flagging 15% of the rest, costs 73.9 relative units when a miss is priced at 50× an overflag — 70 of that 73.9 is the 1.4 missed fields
  • A classifier tuned toward recall (2% missed, 35% over-flagged) over-flags more than double the fields of the cautious one and still costs less overall — 23.1 units against 73.9 — because the expensive error got rarer
  • Tokenisation doesn't protect the field, it shrinks how many services can leak it: cutting a 12-service order path down to 2 services holding the real card number drops breach exposure from 21.5% to 4.0% at the same per-service risk
  • Minimisation is the only lever of the three that reaches exactly zero rather than a smaller number — a field collected at 2,000/day for 365 days sits at 730,000 records deep; never collected, it is 0, at any retention
  • Shortening retention divides the standing inventory but does not zero it, the same trap as backup retention in deletion-in-a-distributed-store: 90 days of the same field is still 180,000 records, not 0
  • Envelope encryption's key granularity sets the smallest thing a data key's destruction can shred; a token vault's granularity sets the smallest thing a token's revocation can unlink — the same "granularity fixed at write time" constraint, on a different mechanism

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A 40-field schema is 35% genuinely PII. Classifier A misses 10% of it and over-flags 15% of the rest; classifier B misses 2% and over-flags 35%. At 50× cost per miss, which is cheaper, and by how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Classifier B, at 23.1 units against A's 73.9 — despite B over-flagging more than double the fields (9.1 vs 3.9). A misses 1.4 fields at 50× each: 70 units of exposure alone. B misses only 0.28 fields: 14 units. The total error count went up (9.38 fields wrong vs 5.3) while the cost went down, because which kind of error you make matters more than how many you make.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A 12-service order path handles a card number; only the token vault and the payment gateway integration ever see the real value, at a 2%-per-service breach probability. What is the exposure with and without tokenisation, and why isn't it a 6× improvement (12 ÷ 2)?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    4.0% with tokenisation (2 services: 1 − 0.98²) against 21.5% without (12 services: 1 − 0.98¹²) — a 5.4× reduction, not 6×, because the relationship is 1 − (1 − p)^k, not 1/k: each additional cleartext-holding service adds risk multiplicatively against what's already survived, not linearly, so the ratio of two compound terms is never exactly the ratio of their exponents.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    A team cuts backup retention for a PII field from 365 days to 90 days and reports a 4× reduction in "data at risk." Using standingInventory, what actually changed, and what didn't?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The standing count fell from 730,000 to 180,000 records — a real, correct arithmetic reduction, and exactly Little's Law: same arrival rate, shorter time in the system. What did not change is that the number is still not zero: every one of those 180,000 records is still a record a classifier can mis-handle, a service can leak, and an attacker can steal. Shortening retention is a genuine improvement and it is not minimisation — it is the same "smaller number, not zero" trap this lesson warns about for classification and tokenisation, applied to the third lever. The only way to reach zero is collected: false: never write the field down.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: every control you build reduces risk for the data that reaches it, and only a decision made upstream of the control removes the risk for data that never does. Name an instance outside PII handling.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A firewall rule reduces risk for traffic that reaches the network edge; not exposing the port at all removes the risk for good, and no firewall rule is as reliable as a closed port. A code reviewer catches bugs in code that gets written; not building the feature removes the bug class entirely, which is why "do we need this at all" is the highest- leverage question in scoping, not the lowest. A hospital's infection-control protocol reduces spread among admitted patients; a vaccination campaign that keeps people from being admitted at all removes the exposure upstream of the protocol. In every case the control is real and worth having, and it is still answering a smaller question than the one "don't create the exposure" answers.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
