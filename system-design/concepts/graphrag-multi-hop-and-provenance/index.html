Tier 12 · Agentic systems and graph retrieval GraphRAG and provenance

Every claim your graph makes carries a citation, 27.8% of them point at a span that does not say it, and your review process catches 4.5% of those

Citation coverage is 100% by construction — an edge cannot exist without a source — and it therefore carries no information. Citation soundness is the extractor's precision raised to the edge count, and a wrong citation is topically plausible by construction, because plausible topical overlap is what fooled the extractor. The one lever that moves it is refusing to answer.

Read first — knowledge-graphs-as-a-retrieval-substratemulti-stage-retrieval-and-ranking

01

The answer with three citations, and the month nobody checked them

A compliance team asks: who approved the exception that the person who signed the Q3 refund policy requested? The graph traverses three hops and answers, and the answer comes with a citation for every hop — document, section, character offsets. Someone reads it, clicks two of the three, finds the cited sections plainly about approvals and refunds, and signs it off.

The answer is wrong. The second edge — requested-by — was extracted from a sentence that named the person in a distribution list, not as the requester. The span is real, the document is real, the topic is right, and the relation is not there.

It surfaces a month later when a second team gets a different answer from the documents themselves. The review process ran. The citations were checked. What made this survive was not an absence of evidence; it was the presence of evidence that looked right.

02

The belief this breaks: a graph answer is explainable, so it is verifiable

Split the promise into the two numbers it contains, because they behave completely differently.

coverage = 1   ·   soundness = precisionedges

Coverage — the share of claims carrying a citation — is 1 by construction. An edge cannot exist in the graph without the span it was extracted from, so every claim has a source and the number can never be anything else. It is therefore not evidence about this answer. Soundness — the share of citations whose span actually says what the edge claims — is the extractor’s precision raised to the edge count: 85% per edge over a three-hop answer’s two edges is 72.25%.

The gap between a number that is always 1 and a number that is 0.72 is the whole of a graph’s explainability claim, and the demo only ever shows the first one.

And the failure is adversarially shaped, without anyone being adversarial. A wrong edge exists because the extractor found a span that looked like the relation. The topical overlap that fooled a language model is the same overlap that will satisfy a human skimming for relevance. So the wrong citations are precisely the ones that pass review, and they are attached to claims that a reader now believes more strongly than they would have believed an uncited claim.

03

The intuition: read before you cite

The scientific literature ran this experiment for a century and the results are published.

Simkin and Roychowdhury’s 2003 paper “Read before you cite!” worked out how often authors cite things they have not read, and the method is the part worth stealing. When a paper’s reference list contains the same typo as an earlier paper’s list, the later author copied the reference rather than opening the source. Repeated errors propagate through citation chains like a genetic marker, and their distribution lets you estimate what share of citers ever read the thing. The estimate was: a small minority.

Two things about that transfer exactly.

The citation’s presence is what satisfies the reader. A claim with a footnote reads as checked. That is the entire function of a footnote and it works whether or not the source supports the claim, which is why an unsound citation is worse than none — it does not merely fail to help, it actively purchases credibility.

The errors that matter are drift, not fabrication. The quotation-error literature in medicine repeatedly finds that a meaningful minority of citations misrepresent their source, and the misrepresentations are rarely inventions. The source says something narrower, or about a different population, or hedged. Topically right, propositionally wrong — the same failure the extractor makes.

And the remedy the literature converged on was not “cite more”. It was quote the sentence and, for anything consequential, have a second person independently extract each claim from each source. Those are abstention and independent confirmation, which is where the arithmetic below arrives too.

4.5% of the bad citations are caught, and both multiplications that produce that number are about the review process rather than the graph. The share of answers looked at (10%) and the reviewer’s recall on a plausible wrong citation (45%, an input, and low for the reason in slot 2). Everything the graph gives you sits upstream of both.

The instinct is to raise the sample rate. Read every single answer at the same reviewer recall and 153 of 1,000 still ship, because recall is the binding term and sampling is the term teams tune. Getting to zero requires a reviewer who catches every unsound citation, which is 1,000 careful span-by-span reads per 1,000 answers — a real option, at a real price, and one worth costing rather than assuming.

The lever that moves the top of the funnel is refusing to answer. Multi-pass extraction gives every edge a confidence score for free — how many passes agreed on it — so “answer only if every edge on the path cleared k of n passes” is implementable rather than hypothetical:

the strictest filter is not the most precise one

Soundness peaks at 4 of 5 and falls at 5 of 5. Unanimity removes almost every ordinary fact — recall drops to 37% — and cannot remove a wrong triple produced by a genuinely ambiguous sentence, because every pass produces that one identically. So the surviving population is enriched in exactly the errors the vote was meant to remove.

05

The numbers: a query priced per corpus, and a summary layer that is stale every week

The other half of GraphRAG is global search, and it is the mechanism that actually earned the technique its results: cluster the graph into communities, summarise each one at build time, and answer a corpus-wide question by map-reduce over those summaries. It is aggregation machinery, and it works.

What nobody quotes is that it reads every summary at its level, so its per-query cost is proportional to the corpus rather than to the question. No chunk-retrieval query has that property.

Two readings, and the second is the more useful one.

Leaf-level global search is 25.8× a retrieval call and grows with the corpus. That is the number to put in a capacity plan, because it is the one that turns into a surprise.

And level 2 reads fewer tokens than one ordinary retrieval call — $0.077 against $0.088 — while still representing a third of the corpus. Global search is not the expensive thing. Insisting on the leaf level is the expensive thing, and the level is a free parameter almost nobody exposes.

The fidelity column is the cost of that choice, and it comes from compaction’s own measured retention applied at the compression ratio a community summary actually runs at: 400 documents of 2,000 tokens into 600 tokens is 1,333×, against the 5× a context compaction does. Every further factor of five in compression multiplies retention by 0.85 again, which puts a leaf summary at 48.4% and the root at 23.4%. That column doubles as the chance a claim in the answer can be traced back to a document that actually says it — so a global answer’s citation points at a summary, three or four lossy steps from a span. The provenance chain is a chain too, and it decays like one.

06

The trade-off: what this buys, stated as fairly as the arithmetic allows

The accuracy results are real. One study puts GraphRAG at 91.5% against BM25-RAG’s 84.8% — 6.7 points, which removes 44% of the error — and AWS reports precision lifts of up to 35% from adding graph structure. A 2025 head-to-head on a unified task set had neither substrate winning outright, which the previous lesson reconciles: the studies were weighting different query shapes. Take the lift as genuine on aggregate and global questions.

What this lesson adds is that the lift is measured on accuracy and the bill is denominated in three other currencies, and the honest decision needs all four:

  • Explainability is an audit path, not a proof, and it is worth having on those terms. An edge that names its span is genuinely more debuggable than a chunk that was silently not retrieved — you can go and look. What it is not is verified, and the difference matters most exactly when the answer is surprising, because a surprising answer is disproportionately likely to be one of the 27.8%.
  • Abstention is the highest-value change available and almost nothing implements it. The confidence score is already there in the pass agreement; the plumbing is a threshold and a “not enough evidence” response. Twelve points of overall correctness for four points of coverage is a better return than any extraction improvement in either lesson.
  • Expose the community level as a query parameter. It spans two orders of magnitude of cost and about 25 points of fidelity, and it is usually hard-coded to the leaf level because that is what the demo did. Most global questions do not need 48.4% fidelity at $2.26; a routing rule as crude as “start at level 2, escalate if the answer is thin” removes the cost problem entirely.
  • Make communities as large as the summary can bear, and measure what it can bear. The cost pressure is monotone toward larger communities across three orders of magnitude and the only thing stopping you is fidelity, so the binding constraint is a quality measurement nobody takes. Take it: summarise one community at three sizes and have a human score the summaries against the documents.
  • Budget the summary layer as a weekly job, not a build step. At any realistic community size it is stale every week, and the difference between a graph programme that works and one that quietly rots is whether that job is scheduled and funded on day one.

The honest limit, and it is the deepest in this tier. The extraction quality figures are inputs, as in the previous lesson. Two more inputs are load-bearing here and both are mine rather than anyone’s measurement: the reviewer’s 45% recall on a plausible-but-wrong citation, and the power-law extension of compaction’s retention to compression ratios three orders of magnitude past where it was measured. Set reviewer recall to 1 and the funnel becomes the arithmetic everyone already assumes — and the reason not to is that the failure being reviewed is topically plausible by construction, which is an argument rather than a measurement. What survives all of that is the structure: coverage is exactly 1 and therefore uninformative, soundness is precision to the edge count, the funnel leaks through two multiplications neither of which is about the model, the abstention optimum is interior, the strictest bar is not the most sound, global search’s cost is proportional to the corpus, community staleness is 1 − (1−churn)^size, and there is no interior optimum in community size. Every one of those is a shape or an identity. The percentages are illustrations of the shapes at plausible inputs.

07

In the wild

GraphRAG’s own paper was about global sensemaking and the industry read it as being about multi-hop. Its mechanism is community detection plus hierarchical summarisation — aggregation machinery — and its evaluation was on corpus-wide questions that no retrieval budget can cover. The multi-hop framing arrived later and, on the previous lesson’s arithmetic, is the one query shape the substrate is worst at. If you are adopting GraphRAG, adopt it for the thing it was built for.

The provenance failure has a name in the graph world and it is not usually treated as one. Extraction provenance is stored, displayed, and almost never evaluated — teams measure extraction precision on a sample of triples and then present per-edge citations to users as though the two were the same claim. They are not: precision is a property of the population, and the citation on the screen is a claim about one edge, on a path, where the compounding happens. Measuring “what share of our answers have a fully sound citation chain” is a different sample and a different number, and it is the one users are relying on.

And the operational tell is the summary layer’s job history. If a graph has been in production for six months and the community summaries were last rebuilt at launch, the global answers are describing the corpus as it was — confidently, in fluent prose, with no signal of age anywhere in the output. A stale chunk is text a model can notice is old. A stale community summary is an assertion about the present, generated from the past, with a citation.

  1. Cued recall

    Your graph answers a three-hop question and shows a citation for every hop. A reviewer checks all three, finds each span relevant, and signs it off. How confident should you be?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not much more than before the citations. Coverage was always going to be 100% — an edge cannot exist without a source span — so it told you nothing. Soundness over two edges at 85% per-edge precision is 72.25%, and the 27.75% that fail do so by pointing at a span that is topically relevant and does not say the thing, because that plausible overlap is what made the extractor believe it. A reviewer skimming for relevance is exactly the wrong instrument: the check that has to be made is "does this sentence assert this relation", one span at a time, and the input's own recall on that task is around 45%.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    You want fewer wrong answers from the graph. Do you increase the review sample rate or lower the rate at which the graph answers at all?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Lower the rate at which it answers, and it is not close. Sampling is a multiplication on a term that is already small: going from 10% to 100% reviewed takes catches from 12.5 to 125 per 1,000 but still ships 153, because the reviewer's recall on a plausible wrong citation is the binding factor. Abstention is the only lever that moves the top of the funnel.

  4. Cued recall

    A corpus-wide question — "what are the recurring themes in last year's incident reports" — is answered by GraphRAG's global search. What does that cost, and how does it scale?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It reads every summary at its level, so the cost is set by the corpus rather than the question: 750,000 prompt tokens and $2.26 at the leaf level, 25.8× one ordinary retrieval call — and it grows linearly as documents are added, which no chunk-retrieval query does.   5. Cued recall

    Your corpus changes by 2% a week. Your triples need re-extracting at 104% of the build per year. What do your community summaries need?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    52 full rebuilds a year — the whole layer, every week. A triple depends on one document, so its staleness is proportional to churn. A community summary depends on all 400 documents under it, so it survives a week with probability 0.98400 = 0.03%. Staleness has stopped being proportional to churn and become a constant, and the constant is "always".   6. Near transfer

    Same shape, different domain: citation chains in the scientific literature. What is known about how often a citation supports the claim attached to it, and how is it known?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is known to be wrong surprisingly often, and the method of knowing is the part that transfers. Simkin and Roychowdhury's 2003 "Read before you cite!" inferred the reading rate from copied citation errors: when a paper's reference list contains the same typo as an earlier paper's, the later author copied the reference rather than reading the source, and the distribution of repeated errors lets you estimate what share of citers ever opened the thing they cited. Their estimate was that only a small minority had. The mechanism is exactly the graph's: a claim acquires a citation, the citation makes the claim look checked, and the checking never happens because the citation's presence is what satisfies the reader. Two further parallels are worth having. First, quotation error surveys in medical journals repeatedly find that a substantial minority of citations misrepresent the source — and the errors that matter are not fabrications but drift, where the source says something weaker or narrower, which is precisely the topically-plausible failure. Second, the remedy the literature converged on is not "cite more" but quotation with the relevant sentence and systematic-review protocols where a second person independently extracts each claim from each source. That is abstention and independent confirmation, which is what slot 6 arrives at from the arithmetic.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: attaching a verifiable-looking provenance marker to a claim raises the claim's credibility by more than it raises its correctness, so the marker's presence displaces the verification it appears to offer — and the only effective controls are refusing to make the claim, or having a second party independently re-derive it. Name instances outside software.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Credit rating on a structured security. A tranche carried a letter grade produced by a documented model over documented inputs, which is provenance in exactly this sense — traceable, auditable, and about the model rather than about the mortgages. The grade's presence displaced the diligence it appeared to encode, and the documented remedy afterwards was not better ratings but retained risk and independent re-underwriting: abstention and second-party derivation. Others: chain of custody in forensic evidence, where a complete custody form makes a sample look sound and the errors that matter are substitutions upstream of the form — hence blind re-testing by a second laboratory; provenance in the art market, where a documented ownership chain raises price far more than it raises authenticity, and the forgeries that succeed are the ones with the best paperwork; food traceability labels, where a lot number that resolves to a real facility says nothing about what happened in it, which is why audits are unannounced and second-party; clinical trial registration, where a registry entry makes a published result look pre-specified and the discrepancy between registered and reported outcomes is the thing that had to be measured separately; and notarisation, where a seal certifies that a signature happened and nothing about whether the signer understood the document — which is why consequential instruments additionally require independent legal advice. In every instance the marker is real, its coverage is complete, its soundness is a different quantity that nobody measures, and the fix is a refusal or a second pair of eyes rather than a better marker.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
