Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Vertical vs horizontal explained well nowhere else

The choice between a bigger machine and more machines has a closed form, and the price of hardware is not in it

Vertical and horizontal scaling are not two settings of one dial — they raise N against different terms of the same equation. One machine of 50 units and 50 machines of one unit do exactly the same amount of work, and nine machines of 5.6 units do 2.7 times more from identical hardware.

Read first — single-machine-ceilingamdahl-and-usl

01

They sharded for six months and got what one big machine would have given

A service on one machine. It needs several times more throughput, and there is budget for fifty times the hardware it currently has. Call that budget 50 units, where one unit is the machine they already own.

The team does the thing everyone does. They shard. Six months later there are 50 machines, and measured throughput is 10.2 times the original single machine.

Three numbers were available before any of that work started, and all three are worse news than the 10.2:

  • 31 machines would have done 11.0 times. The fleet passed its own peak at 31 and machines 32 through 50 were subtracting. Nineteen machines were paid for, deployed, monitored and patched in order to reduce throughput.
  • One machine of 50 units would also have done 10.2 times. Not approximately — the same number, to three significant figures. Six months of sharding bought exactly what a resize would have bought, and threw in a network partition.
  • Nine machines of 5.6 units would have done 27.9 times. Same 50 units of hardware. 2.7 times the throughput of either thing anybody proposed, with a fifth of the machines to operate — and the same six months of sharding work, because a shard key either exists or it does not. The shape was free once the rewrite was paid for, and nobody chose it.

Nothing in that story is a bug and no machine was misconfigured. The team asked the wrong question — bigger box or more boxes — and both answers to a wrong question are wrong.

02

The belief that made this a six-month project

The belief is so standard it usually goes unsaid: vertical scaling hits a wall, so horizontal scaling is the answer that keeps working. Buy the bigger box while it is cheap and easy, and when you run out of box, shard.

It is wrong in two separate ways.

The wall is not made of hardware. A single machine’s ceiling is 1/σᵥ, where σᵥ is the share of its work that cannot happen at the same time as itself — a lock, one write-ahead log, one kernel, finite memory bandwidth. At 8% serial work that ceiling is 12.5 times one base machine, and it is a property of the software. You approach it by buying cores, and no number of cores passes it. But the fleet has a wall too, and it is a worse kind of wall: Amdahl’s ceiling is approached and never crossed, while the fleet turns over at N* = √((1−σₕ)/κ) — 31 machines at 3% contention and 0.1% per pair — and past that point adding machines subtracts. Horizontal scaling does not keep working. It stops working at a computable size, and then reverses.

They are not the same knob. Both strategies raise N in C(N) = N ÷ (1 + σ(N−1) + κN(N−1)). A bigger machine raises N against σᵥ, measured inside the box. More machines raise N against σₕ and κ, measured across the network. Those are different numbers on different boundaries, which means one strategy can be improving while the other is getting worse, and there is a budget where they are exactly equal. Set the two capacities equal, and because both have the same numerator the whole thing collapses to a comparison of denominators:

B* = (σᵥ − σₕ) ÷ κ

The hardware budget at which one big machine overtakes a fleet of small ones. Note what is absent: the amount of hardware, the price of hardware, the number of cores, and the traffic. Derived in slot 4 and asserted in machine.test.mjs against a bisected root of the same equality.

Predict A workload has 8% serial work inside one machine, 3% serial work across machines, and 0.1% coherency cost per pair of machines. At what hardware budget does one big machine overtake a fleet of one-unit machines?
B* = (σᵥ − σₕ)/κ = (0.08 − 0.03)/0.001 = 50 units. Two of the wrong options are right formulas answering the wrong question: 12.5× is 1/σᵥ, which is a capacity and not a budget, and 31 is √((1−σₕ)/κ), where the fleet stops improving — not where the box catches it. At 31 the fleet is still ahead, 11.0× to 9.1×; it has merely stopped getting further ahead, and the box needs another 19 units to draw level.
Show it without answering

Four things to do with it.

Find the crossing, then move it with σ and not with money. At the default parameters it is at 50 units. Halve κ and it moves to 100; raise σₕ from 3% to 5.5% and it moves to 25. Now press Charge 2× for the big machine: the capacity crossover stays at 50 while the crossover on cost moves out to 138 units. Both are true and they are different questions. The first says which arrangement does more work with the same hardware; the second says which does more work for the same money, and the 88-unit gap between them is what you are paying to keep one failure domain and no network.

Press the first button and watch the crossing disappear. With σₕ set equal to σᵥ, the fleet loses at every budget — at 8 units it does 4.95 against the box’s 5.13, and at 32 units 7.16 against 9.20. This is the half of the decision no purchase order can change. Horizontal scaling wins only when coordinating across machines is genuinely cheaper than coordinating inside one, and that is a property of the workload.

Notice where the fleet’s advantage is largest. It is at about 16 units, where the fleet does 1.30 times the single machine — well before the fleet’s own peak at 31. Past 16 the fleet is still improving in absolute terms and already losing ground relatively, which is exactly the region where a capacity review concludes that sharding is working.

Watch the strip. At 32 units the best shape is 8 machines of 4 units and does 20.4× against 11.0 for the flat fleet. At 128 units it is 13 machines of 9.8 units and does 49.4× against 6.1 — 8.1 times the throughput of the same hardware bought as 128 small machines. The strip is never at either end of the plot, at any setting.

03

The intuition: one enormous restaurant, or five hundred tiny ones

Imagine serving five hundred diners.

One 500-seat restaurant. Nobody has to coordinate with anybody else in another building. But inside, everything is shared: one kitchen, one pass, one walk-in fridge, one manager approving things. Every extra seat makes those queues longer, and no amount of hiring fixes the single pass that every plate goes through.

Five hundred one-seat restaurants. Now there is no internal contention at all — but there are five hundred deliveries, five hundred sets of accounts, and any menu change has to be agreed by everyone. The talking grows with the number of pairs of restaurants, which is the meeting from the previous lesson.

Two details matter more than the extremes.

The second restaurant is the expensive one. With one, you walk into the kitchen and ask. With two, you need a phone call, a shared recipe book, and a rule for what happens when a booking arrives at both. Opening the twenty-first restaurant does not introduce any kind of problem that the second one did not already introduce. It adds more of the same problem. So the step from one to two is where you buy a distributed system, and you buy all of it at once.

Nobody builds either extreme. Real chains have maybe twenty restaurants of maybe fifty seats, and that is not a compromise between two ideals — it is the actual optimum, because the two kinds of overhead are different overheads and each one is small when its own number is small.

Vertical scaling raises the number of workers inside one box. Horizontal scaling raises the number of boxes. They fight different overheads, so they are not alternatives, and the best answer is almost always some of each — a few large machines rather than one enormous one or a great many small ones.

04

The mechanism: the same equation twice, with different parameters

Let a hardware unit be one base machine’s worth of cores, memory and network. A budget of B units can be spent as one machine of size B, as B machines of size 1, or as any rectangle in between. Write C(N) as in the previous lesson: throughput relative to one base machine.

V(B) = B ÷ (1 + σᵥ(B−1))  ·  capped at 1/σᵥ

One machine of B units. This is Amdahl’s Law with N as the machine’s size. σᵥ is the serialisation inside one box: a lock, a single write path, one kernel, finite memory bandwidth. A bigger machine raises B and never touches σᵥ, which is why the ceiling is fixed — at 8% serial work it is 12.5×, whatever you spend.

H(B) = B ÷ (1 + σₕ(B−1) + κB(B−1))

B machines of one unit. The USL with N as the machine count. σₕ is serialisation across machines — a shared database, a leader, a global sequence — and κ is the pairwise coordination cost. Turns over at N* = √((1−σₕ)/κ): 31 machines at σₕ = 3%, κ = 0.1%.

Those two lines are the whole lesson: the same equation, with N pointing at two different things and σ measured on two different boundaries. Vertical scaling raises the per-machine constant. Horizontal scaling multiplies it and pays σₕ and κ for the privilege.

The crossover, in three lines

Set them equal. Both have numerator B, so B cancels immediately and what remains is a comparison of overheads:

B / (1 + σᵥ(B−1))  =  B / (1 + σₕ(B−1) + κB(B−1))
      σᵥ(B−1)      =  σₕ(B−1) + κB(B−1)
      σᵥ           =  σₕ + κB              divide by (B−1), valid for B over 1
      B*           =  (σᵥ − σₕ) / κ

B* = (σᵥ − σₕ) ÷ κ

Three overhead parameters and nothing else. The amount of hardware cancelled, and the price of hardware was only ever attached to the amount. Graded in machine.test.mjs against a bisected root of H(B) − V(B) with both denominators written out inline — algebra and a numeric scan, no shared code path, agreeing to 1e-6 across 24 parameter combinations.

Two consequences, and the second is the one that decides real arguments.

The window is bounded on both sides. Below B* the fleet wins; above it the single machine wins, if one that big exists to be bought. So “we will need to shard eventually” is not a theorem. Whether it is true depends on where B* sits relative to the largest machine you can buy, and both of those numbers have moved a long way since the advice was written.

If σₕ is not below σᵥ, there is no window at all. B* is only positive when σₕ is less than σᵥ. If coordinating across machines is as serialising as coordinating inside one, the fleet’s denominator exceeds the box’s at every N, and one machine of size B beats B machines for every B — including every B beyond the biggest machine sold. No price premium reverses that, because the capacity comparison has already been lost. The most common way to get here is to shard without removing the shared thing: one row, one leader, one sequence that every shard still takes. Then you moved σ onto a network and bought κ on top.

The second machine is not like the others

C(1) = 1 exactly  ·  C(2) = 2 ÷ (1 + σₕ + 2κ)

On one machine both cross-machine terms are zero, not small — there is no other machine to coordinate with. At σₕ = 3% and κ = 0.1%, C(2) = 1.94: you pay for one whole machine and receive 0.94 of one.

The 6% shortfall is the cheap half of the bill. The expensive half is not in the arithmetic: this is the step at which consensus, replication lag, split brain and a network partition between your own components become possible. Every machine after the second adds more of the same failure mode and no new one. So the discontinuity in this decision is not somewhere out at scale — it is between one and two, and it is where the entire cost of being distributed is incurred.

Both terms at once, and why the answer is a rectangle

A budget does not have to be spent at either end. Spend it as m machines of size units. Each machine’s own capacity is set by σᵥ, and the fleet multiplier by σₕ and κ, so the two overheads multiply:

total = B ÷ (Dᵥ(size) × Dₕ(m))

where Dᵥ = 1 + σᵥ(size−1) and Dₕ = 1 + σₕ(m−1) + κm(m−1), and B = m × size. The numerator is the budget however it is arranged, so the entire question is which split makes the product of the two overheads smallest.

Each denominator is small when its own N is small, and two moderate factors beat one large one. That is why the optimum is interior, and it is the same arithmetic as a sharded counter with a two-level rollup beating one global counter. At a 50-unit budget the best split is 9 machines of 5.6 units at 27.9×, against 10.2 for both pure strategies.

The assumption in that, stated plainly, because it is load-bearing: σᵥ and σₕ are different serialisations. If the same lock is contended both inside a machine and across the fleet, it is one σ, this form double-counts the relief, and re-shaping the fleet buys nothing at all. Whether the two are independent is the question to answer before believing any number the model produces.

Vertical scaling is sublinear in capacity, and it is computable in advance

Doubling a machine’s size multiplies its capacity by 2(1 + σ(n−1)) ÷ (1 + σ(2n−1)), which starts near 2 and decays to 1:

Solve that expression for a target gain and you get the size at which a doubling stops paying: n = (2 − g)(1 − σ)(2σ(g − 1))⁻¹. At 8% serial work a doubling stops returning 1.5× past 5.75 units and stops returning 1.25× past 17.25. Both numbers are set by σ alone — not by price, and not by how many cores the machine already has. If list prices within an instance family are close to linear in size, then price per unit of delivered throughput starts rising from the first doubling and rises faster after the knee, and that happens without any pricing superlinearity at all. A premium on the largest sizes, where it exists, only makes it worse.

Whether the working set fits is the largest lever here, and it is not a scaling strategy at all. Using the four-ceiling model from the previous lesson: a request making 20 disk reads caps one machine at 800 rps, and the same reads served from 13 GiB of resident memory cap it at 2,667 — the bottleneck moves from disk to CPU and the ceiling moves 3.3×. Three doublings of machine size, from 8 units to 64, buy 2.1×. So the first question is not vertical-or-horizontal, it is what is this machine waiting for.

Two sourced observations, because “it will not fit on one machine” is the assumption doing the most damage. Jordan Tigani, who worked on BigQuery before founding MotherDuck, reported that among BigQuery customers spending over $1,000 a year, 90% of queries processed under 100 MB of data, and that memory-optimised cloud instances reach 24 TB of RAM or 445 CPU cores. And McSherry, Isard and Murray measured the other direction in 2015: the hardware a distributed system needs before it beats one competent thread — which they named its COST — was 16 cores for one system in their survey, 512 for another, and unbounded for a third, meaning it never beat the single thread at any size they could test.

σₕ below σᵥ is the only row that is not negotiable with money. If it holds, the crossover is at 50 units and there is a window. If it does not, there is no window at any budget, and that is a fact about how the work partitions rather than about what you can afford.

The failure-domain row is arithmetic too, and it runs opposite to everything else here. Spare capacity to survive losing one machine is 1/(m−1) of the fleet: one machine cannot have it at all, two machines need 100%, nine need 12.5%, thirty-one need 3.3%. Redundancy gets cheaper as the fleet grows while every other cost in this lesson gets dearer, which is why this row so often decides the question on its own — see the trade-off slot.

06

The trade-off: one failure domain, or a rewrite

Vertical costs you the failure domain, and it costs it discontinuously. One machine is one failure domain — 100% blast radius by construction — and it cannot have N+1 redundancy at all without becoming a two-machine fleet. Which means the first machine you add for availability charges you the entire coordination entry cost of slot 4: σₕ and κ switch on from zero, and you get 0.94 of a machine’s throughput for one machine’s money. Most systems are horizontal for this reason and not for the throughput, and it is worth knowing which argument you are actually making, because they want different fleet sizes. Availability wants the smallest N above one; throughput wants the interior optimum.

Horizontal costs you a rewrite, and that is an asymmetry in reversibility rather than in throughput. Resizing a machine is a configuration change and a reboot: minutes of downtime, reversible by changing the number back. Sharding is a code change plus a data migration — routing, cross-shard queries that used to be joins, transactions that used to be atomic — and it is not reversible on any timescale that matters. So the two options are not symmetric in risk even where they are symmetric in throughput, and at the crossover budget they are exactly symmetric in throughput. Given two equal options, take the reversible one.

The model’s own limits, three of them.

The multiplicative form assumes σᵥ and σₕ are independent serialisations. They often are not. One database row that every shard still updates is one σ, and splitting the fleet re-labels it rather than reducing it — the 27.9× becomes 10.2× and the arithmetic gives no warning. This is the assumption to test first, and the test is: after the split, what does the shared thing look like?

σᵥ, σₕ and κ are fitted from measurement, not read off a design, exactly as in the previous lesson — and you cannot fit them without data from past the knee. Measurements at 1, 2 and 4 units fit a straight line beautifully and tell you nothing about where B* is. So the honest use of B* is not “compute it and decide” but “notice that it exists, and that its value is a fact about your software rather than about your budget”.

The price of hardware is modelled as flat per unit, which is the case most generous to the big machine. Turning on a 2× premium moves the crossover on cost from 50 units to 138 while the crossover on capacity stays at 50. If you care which arrangement does more work with the same hardware, use the first. If you are signing an invoice, use the second, and the gap between them is the number to argue about.

And the trade nobody names: the interior optimum is more operationally expensive than either end. One machine has one thing to monitor and no sharding logic. A uniform fleet of tiny machines is homogeneous and trivially autoscaled. Thirteen machines of 9.8 units is neither — it has the sharding code and the large-instance procurement and a machine size that has to be re-derived every time σ or the budget changes. The 4.3× is real and it is not free, and a team that cannot operate it should take one of the ends deliberately rather than arrive at the middle by accident.

07

In the wild

Stack Overflow published its own numbers, and they are the vertical answer at real scale. Nick Craver’s architecture write-up records 9 web servers serving 209 million HTTP requests and 504 million SQL queries in a day, against SQL Server machines with 384 GB of RAM and 2×12 cores each, one master taking almost all the load. The line worth memorising is his: “we’re down to needing only 1 web server. We have unintentionally tested this, successfully, a few times.” The nine exist for rolling builds, headroom and redundancy — which is the availability argument from slot 6, made explicitly, and separated from the throughput argument.

The COST paper is this lesson’s crossover, measured on real systems. McSherry, Isard and Murray asked how much hardware a distributed graph processing system needs before it beats a competent single-threaded implementation, called that number the system’s COST, and found values of 16 cores, 512 cores, and unbounded. A system with unbounded COST is one where σₕ and κ are so large that the crossover in this lesson never arrives at any size — the distributed system is not scaling the work, it is scaling its own overhead. That is the strongest available evidence that “horizontal keeps working” is an assumption rather than a property.

Managed databases default to the vertical answer for writes. The standard topology for managed PostgreSQL and MySQL is one primary plus replicas: reads scale out, writes scale up, and the recommended fix for a write bottleneck is a larger primary. Sharding is available and it is a separate product — Vitess for MySQL, Citus for PostgreSQL — which is itself the evidence that sharding is a code change rather than a setting. Both of those tools exist because retrofitting a shard key onto an application that did not have one is expensive enough to be worth an entire project.

Consensus groups are the case where everyone already knows the answer. Raft and Paxos groups run 3 or 5 voting members, never 9, because every write waits on a majority: κ is large and N* is tiny. Nobody argues for horizontal scaling of a consensus group, and the reason is exactly the arithmetic here — it is simply that in that one case the peak arrives early enough that the industry found it the expensive way and wrote the answer down.

“Big data is dead” is the working-set row of the table in slot 5. Tigani’s argument is not that large datasets do not exist but that the data a query touches is usually small, and that single machines have grown two orders of magnitude while the advice to shard has not moved. His measurement — 90% of queries under 100 MB — is a statement about σᵥ never having been the binding constraint for most of the systems that were sharded anyway.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Vertical raises N against σᵥ inside one box and is capped at 1/σᵥ; horizontal raises N against σₕ and κ across boxes. Same C(N), different terms.
  • The crossover budget is B* = (σᵥ − σₕ)/κ — the amount of hardware cancels out of the equality, and with it the price of hardware
  • If σₕ ≥ σᵥ there is no crossover at all: horizontal scaling does not win at any budget, and no price premium changes that
  • C(1) = 1 exactly, because both cross-machine overhead terms are zero on one machine — so the second machine switches them on rather than easing into them
  • The two overheads multiply, so total = budget ÷ (Dᵥ × Dₕ) and the best split of a budget is in the interior, not at either end
  • N+1 redundancy costs 1/(m−1) of the fleet, so it is most expensive in the smallest fleet and impossible in a fleet of one
  • Sharding is a code change and resizing is a reboot, which is an asymmetry in reversibility rather than in throughput

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why are vertical and horizontal scaling not two settings of the same dial?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the N they raise lands in different terms of C(N) = N ÷ (1 + σ(N−1) + κN(N−1)). A bigger machine raises N against σᵥ — the serialisation inside one box — and never touches σᵥ itself, so it is capped at 1/σᵥ however much you spend. More machines raise N against σₕ + κN, which is a different pair of numbers measured on a different boundary. That is why they are not substitutes and why doing both is not a compromise: the overheads are separate, so they can be kept separately small.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why does the price of hardware fall out of the crossover, and what does that leave the price doing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because both strategies buy the same amount of hardware, so both capacities have the identical numerator B and the equality reduces to equal denominators: 1 + σᵥ(B−1) = 1 + σₕ(B−1) + κB(B−1), then σᵥ = σₕ + κB, then B* = (σᵥ − σₕ)/κ. B itself cancelled, and money was only ever attached to B. Price re-enters only if you let the two strategies buy different amounts for the same money, and then it acts as a threshold on the capacity ratio: at 2× per unit for the big machine the capacity crossover stays at 50 units and the crossover on cost moves to 138. The gap between the two is what you are paying to keep one failure domain.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    What single property of the workload decides whether horizontal scaling can win at all — and how would you know it is absent?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Whether σₕ is below σᵥ: whether coordinating across machines is genuinely cheaper than coordinating inside one. If it is not, the fleet's denominator exceeds the box's at every N, one machine of size B beats B machines for every B, and no price premium reverses it. You would know it is absent when the shared thing survives the split — one database row, one leader, one global sequence, one lock that every shard still takes. Then sharding moved the same σ across a network and added κ on top, which is strictly worse than leaving it in one box.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Why is the second machine categorically different from the twenty-first?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because C(1) = 1 regardless of σₕ and κ: on one machine both cross-machine terms are exactly zero, not small. The step to two switches them on — C(2) = 2 ÷ (1 + σₕ + 2κ) = 1.94, so you pay for a whole machine and receive 0.94 of one. The 6% is the cheap half of the bill. The expensive half is that this is the step at which consensus, replication lag, split brain and a network partition become possible, and every machine after it adds no new failure mode, only more of the same one.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A 50-unit budget gives 10.2× as one machine and 10.2× as 50 machines. Nine machines of 5.6 units give 27.9×. Where does the extra come from?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The two overhead denominators multiply, and total = B ÷ (Dᵥ(size) × Dₕ(count)). The numerator is the budget however it is arranged, so the whole question is which split makes that product smallest — and each denominator is small when its own N is small, so two moderate factors beat one large one. It is the same reason a sharded counter with a two-level rollup beats a single global counter. The result is real only if σᵥ and σₕ are different serialisations; if it is one lock contended at both levels, the product double-counts the relief and re-shaping buys nothing.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Why is surviving one machine failure most expensive in the smallest fleet?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Spare capacity for N+1 is 1/(m−1) of the fleet: a pair needs 100% spare, nine machines need 12.5%, thirty-one need 3.3%. So the cost of redundancy falls as the fleet grows, which runs opposite to every other cost here — and a single machine cannot have N+1 at all without becoming a two-machine fleet, i.e. paying the entire coordination entry cost for availability rather than for throughput. That is why most systems are horizontal: not because they needed the throughput.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team is choosing between one 96-vCPU database primary and a 24-node sharded cluster, for the same money. What do you measure before deciding, and what would make the sharded cluster the wrong answer even though it is the more scalable-sounding one?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Measure three things. Does the working set fit in the big machine's memory — in the four-ceiling model, moving a 20-read query from disk to memory raises the ceiling 3.3×, more than three doublings of machine size buy (2.1×). What σₕ is: if every sharded transaction still touches a coordinator, a global sequence or a cross-shard join, σₕ is not below σᵥ and the cluster loses at every size. What the failure domain has to be: one primary is one domain and cannot have N+1 without a second machine anyway. The sharded cluster is the wrong answer when the shared thing survived the split — you have then paid a rewrite to move σ onto a network and acquired κ for it. The usual right answer is the interior: a few large primaries, sharded on a key that actually partitions.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: two coordination overheads that grow with different counts multiply rather than add, so the optimum is a rectangle rather than an end point — and which end wins is set by the overheads, not by the price of the units. Where else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Team structure: one 40-person team pays enormous within-team coordination, 40 one-person teams pay enormous between-team coordination, and everybody converges on teams of about five in groups of about eight — Amazon's two-pizza rule is this arithmetic with a slogan on it. Military organisation reinvented squads, platoons and companies for the same reason. Hierarchical aggregation in analytics: partial sums per node, then one rollup, because two levels of fan-in beat one wide one. Schools: class size against number of schools. Warehouses against local depots in a delivery network. The recurring error is arguing about which end to be at, when the ends are the two worst points on the curve and the answer was always a shape.
    Did you have that?
