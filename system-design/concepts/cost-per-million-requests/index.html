Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cost per million explained well nowhere else

They halved the latency, the bill did not move, and compute turned out to be 5% of it

Every system-design resource sizes a system in requests per second and bytes, and then stops — which leaves engineers optimising the one term that is a twentieth of the bill. Decompose a million requests into dollars and the dominant term is almost never compute, the thresholds are ratios of prices rather than prices, and the cheapest fleet is the one with the worst tail.

Read first — back-of-envelopelatency-numbers

01

A quarter of engineering, and the bill did not move

A read-heavy JSON API. Two thousand requests a second, twelve instances, responses averaging 12 KB. The team spent a quarter on latency: profiling, a better serialiser, an N+1 query removed, the p50 down from 41 ms to 19 ms. The graph was unambiguous. Everyone was pleased.

The bill did not move. Not “moved a little” — it was inside the month-to-month noise, and the finance business partner asked what the quarter had bought.

Here is that bill, decomposed per million requests. Every price in this lesson is a declared input, dated 2026-08, marked low confidence, and not checked against any provider’s pricing page. They live in PRICES in src/sim/unitcost.mjs with a caveat each. Substitute your own bill before you quote a single absolute number from this page. What survives the prices being wrong is the shape, and the shape is the lesson.

Three other things in that table are worth noticing before any explanation.

The observability bill is 18.6% — three and three quarter times everything compute costs. Nobody in the team could have told you that, because logs and metrics arrive as a separate invoice from a different vendor and are discussed in a different meeting.

There is a $2,632 line for a load balancer, a NAT gateway and bytes moving inside the provider — and $2,084 of it is bandwidth that never touched the internet. That is 20 GB per million requests crossing between availability zones, at two and a half times the entire compute bill. No dashboard in the company had a graph for it.

Storage is billed twice over, and the smaller of the two charges is the one for the bytes. Operations cost $3,266 and capacity cost $1,145, which is the opposite of how everyone describes storage.

The question “what does a million requests cost?” has an answer with about eight terms in it, and the term every engineer optimises is usually between 1% and 10% of it. You cannot know which term dominates without doing the division, and almost nobody does the division.

02

The obvious fix, and the expression it is missing from

The obvious reading of a cost problem is make the code faster. It is the reading the whole discipline trains: latency is the number on every dashboard, in every SLO, in every design review. The reasoning is that a faster service does more work per machine, so it needs fewer machines.

The reasoning is correct and the conclusion does not follow, because of a step in the middle that nobody performs. Write the compute bill out:

compute per request = instance-hours × price ÷ requests served = instances × price ÷ (rps × 3600)

There is no latency term in that expression. There is no service time, no p50 and no p99. Latency enters only through instances, and only if somebody actually removes some. A fixed replica count, an autoscaler minimum nobody lowered, a scaling policy keyed to request rate rather than to utilisation, or a one-year capacity commitment each break that link — and in all four cases a 2× latency improvement is worth exactly nothing.

That is the mental model almost everyone starts with, and the missing step is why it is wrong: you were not billed for speed, you were billed for instance-hours, and instance-hours are a function of the fleet you held rather than of how quickly it worked.

Predict A service holds 12 instances at $0.10 an hour and serves 2,000 requests a second. A latency project halves the p50. The autoscaler's minimum is unchanged, so the fleet stays at 12. What happens to compute cost per million requests?
Compute per million is 1e6 × instances × price ÷ (rps × 3600) = 1e6 × 12 × 0.10 ÷ 7,200,000 = $0.167, and every symbol in it is unchanged. The first option is the belief this lesson is about: it is what would happen if the fleet halved with the latency, which is a separate action that nothing in the story performed. And even that ideal case is smaller than it sounds — halving 12 instances to 6 halves a term that is 5% of the bill, which is 2.5% of the total. Show it without answering

Three experiments, in order:

  1. Drag utilisation from 35% to 5% and back. The idle bar grows tenfold, the serving bar does not move by a cent, the amber line — their sum — sweeps right by 7×, and nothing else on the chart moves at all. Two structural facts in one gesture: seven of the eight terms do not contain utilisation (only idle headroom does), and six do not contain the request rate either — only the metric-series term and the hourly part of the floor.
  2. Now put utilisation back and drag response size down. Watch the egress bar shrink past the line at about 1.8 KB — and notice that the line does not move while you do it. The crossing point is a property of the price ratio and the machine, not of the payload.
  3. Set response size to 200 bytes and logs to 20 bytes, then look at what is left. Storage operations and the load-balancer-and-cross-AZ floor. With both byte terms driven to nothing, the bill is still $1.64 per million and compute is still under a tenth of it.

What the rig makes hard to avoid: there is no single “cost” of a system. There are eight terms, they respond to different inputs, and the one the engineering organisation is instrumented to see is the smallest of them.

03

The intuition: the van is rented by the day

You run a delivery round with rented vans. The rental is £90 a day per van, whether the van does four drops or forty. You also pay the courier’s weight charge on every parcel, and you pay a tracking service a penny for every scan event your drivers generate.

Someone improves the loading process, and each van now does its round in half the time. Nothing about the money changed. You rented the same vans for the same day.

To save money you have to return a van — and that is a separate decision, with a separate risk, that the loading improvement merely made possible. If you had six vans and you now need three, you save half the rental. If you had six and you need 4.2, you return one and keep five, because there is no such thing as a fifth of a van.

Two more things fall out of the same picture, and they are the parts people miss.

The weight charge does not care how fast you drive. If your parcels are heavy, postage is your business and the vans are a rounding error. Nobody who has looked at the invoice would spend a quarter on loading speed.

The tracking service is billed per scan, and your drivers scan constantly. It is the line nobody mentions when describing the business, and it can quietly exceed the van rental — because it grows with activity, while the rental grows with how many vans you keep.

You are billed for capacity you hold, for bytes you move, and for events you record. Speed is not on that list. Speed changes the bill only by letting you hold less capacity, and only if you then actually hold less.

04

The mechanism: five kinds of charge, and where each one overtakes the others

Here is the same decomposition as a proportion, with the one immovable part hatched.

Each term is one multiplication. The useful part is not the multiplication — it is that they are driven by different inputs, so which one dominates is a property of your workload’s shape rather than of its size.

1. Compute is priced by utilisation, not by request count

compute per request = instance price ÷ (requests one instance serves per hour × utilisation)

The request rate cancels: doubling traffic doubles the fleet and leaves the quotient alone. So per-request compute cost is a property of one machine — how much it costs an hour, how many requests it can serve an hour, and how full you are willing to run it. At 500 rps per instance and 35% utilisation, one instance-hour has to cover 630,000 requests.

Split it at utilisation and you get the two rows in the table. The busy part is 1e6 × $0.10 ÷ (500 × 3600) = $0.056 and does not contain the utilisation at all. The idle part is that times (1−u)/u, which at 35% is 1.86× — you hold nearly two units of headroom for every unit of work.

2. Egress is priced by response size, which nobody profiles

One conversion covers every byte-priced term, and it is worth memorising because it removes an entire class of arithmetic error:

bytes per request ÷ 1000 = GB per million requests

A 12 KB response is 12 GB of egress per million requests. An 800-byte log line is 0.8 GB. A 40-byte difference in a response header is 40 MB per million requests, which is the kind of thing that shows up on a bill and never on a latency graph.

Now set egress equal to compute and solve for the response size. Do it symbolically first, because the symbolic form is the part that stays true:

response bytes at the crossover = (instance-hour price ÷ per-GB egress price) ÷ requests per instance-hour

Read it aloud: an instance-hour of money buys some number of gigabytes of egress, and that same instance-hour has to serve some number of requests — so the crossover is just the per-request byte allowance those two facts leave you. At these declared inputs an instance-hour buys 1.11 GB and serves 630,000 requests, so the threshold is 1,764 bytes. It contains no absolute price, only their ratio: double both prices and it does not move.

1.8 KB. Above that response size, shipping the answer costs more than computing it. That is about twenty fields of JSON with their keys, so a single small object clears it and any paginated list response clears it by an order of magnitude. The example workload is past it by 6.8× — and the only way to find out where your own service sits is to look at a response body, which is not a thing any dashboard shows you.

One direction in that formula is worth pausing on, because it is backwards from most intuitions: raising utilisation lowers the crossover. A busier instance makes compute cheaper per request and does nothing to the price of a byte, so the better you pack your fleet, the sooner egress dominates it.

3. Observability is priced on two axes, and one of them is cardinality

Log ingest is the same algebra with a different price, which gives the sharpest claim on this page:

log-bytes threshold ÷ response-bytes threshold = per-GB egress price ÷ per-GB ingest price

The two thresholds stand in the ratio of the two per-GB prices and in no other relationship. Ingest is usually priced above egress — several times above it — so your per-request logging budget is tighter than your response-size budget. At these inputs the ratio is 0.09/0.50 = 0.18, so the threshold is 317 bytes: one structured log line with a trace id, a request id and a duration, and you have spent more than the CPU spent.

The second axis is worse, because it is not per byte at all. A managed metrics platform bills per time series, where a series is one unique combination of label values. A label with 200 distinct values multiplies the series count by 200 on every metric that carries it.

affordable series = (instance-hour price ÷ series-month price) × instances × hours in a month

Which is your monthly instance-hours times a price ratio, and nothing else. Eleven and a half instances is about 8,200 instance-hours a month; at a 2:1 price ratio that is 16,457 series — one label with 250 values across 80 metrics. The example workload’s declared 20,000 series is already past it, which is why its metrics line ($0.193) is larger than its entire compute line ($0.159). A single high-cardinality label can cost more than the fleet it was added to describe.

Note what kind of cost this is: a series is billed monthly whether or not any query touches it, so its per-request cost falls as traffic grows. Cardinality is brutal on a small service and invisible on a large one, which is exactly the wrong way round for who has time to notice.

4. Storage is billed on two axes at once

Operations are priced per request and are blind to object size. Capacity is priced per GB-month and is blind to request count. So the same storage bill has two completely different shapes:

object size at the crossover = 1000 × operations per million ÷ (write fraction × months retained × per-GB-month price)

At these inputs: 183 KB. Below it you are buying operations, and moving to a cheaper storage tier saves you almost nothing. Above it you are buying capacity, and tiering or shortening retention is the only lever. Retention sits in the denominator, so keeping the same objects ten times as long moves the crossover to 18 KB and flips the same workload from operation-bound to capacity-bound without a single byte changing.

The example workload’s objects are 64 KB, so it is below the crossover: it is paying for operations, and the write operations cost roughly ten times the read operations per unit — the read/write asymmetry that makes a write-heavy design a different cost shape rather than a scaled-up read-heavy one.

5. The floor, and the idle capacity nobody counts

Two kinds of charge that survive traffic going to zero. Hourly and monthly ones — a load balancer, a NAT gateway per availability zone, a metric series — amortise over requests, so they are a tax on being small: at 20 rps they are 87% of the bill, at 2,000 rps 6.4%, and at 200,000 rps 0.1%. Per-GB ones do not amortise at all, and cross-AZ traffic is the one that hides, because the bytes never leave the provider and no dashboard calls them egress.

And the idle capacity. Since idle is the only term that moves with utilisation:

idle beats everything else below u = s ÷ (1 + s), where s is compute’s share of a fully-utilised bill

For this workload s is 1.8%, so the threshold is 1.77% — idle headroom is never the biggest line item at any utilisation anyone runs. That is the opposite of what I expected before computing it. On a compute-bound workload the same formula gives 29%, and most fleets run below that, so there the intuition is right. The formula is identical; the workload decides the answer.

Four readings, and the third and fourth are the ones that change what you do on Monday.

“Optimise compute” is advice that is right for one of these four shapes. On the transcode fleet compute is two thirds of the bill and every classic instruction applies. On the media workload compute is four hundredths of a percent and could be made free without anyone noticing. The advice is not wrong in general; it is untested against the workload, which is the same failure as tuning a block size in the KV cache lesson.

The same 3× and the same doubled p99 buy 5.6% on one workload and 51.8% on another. That is not a difference in how the fleets are run — the arithmetic of utilisation is identical in both rows. It is entirely compute’s share of the bill, which is the term the utilisation advice never mentions.

The internal service has no internet egress at all and still pays for bandwidth. Its egress line is exactly zero, and it moves 15 GB per million requests across availability zones for $0.30 — 2.2× its entire compute bill. If you take one habit from this page, make it looking for that line on your own invoice.

And the biggest term on the internal service is observability, at 44.3% of the total, of which log ingest alone is 38.4% and 5.4× compute. It logs 1,500 bytes per request, which is a normal amount for a service emitting one structured line plus a sampled span — and its threshold, computed the same way as the others, is 278 bytes.

Unit cost falls with scale by 7.8× between 20 and 200,000 requests a second, and every bit of that is fixed charges amortising. Past a few thousand requests a second it stops: the remaining terms are per-byte and per-operation, they do not amortise, and the curve is flat. “Economies of scale” here is entirely a statement about fixed costs, and it is exhausted by the time you have a dozen instances.

The other half of the same fact is the one that matters if you run something small. At 20 requests a second, a hobby-scale service, the bill is 7.8× per request — and 83% of it is 20,000 metric series, with the load balancer and NAT gateway adding another 4%. The cheapest thing a small service can do is delete dashboards.

06

The trade-off: the cheapest fleet is the one with the worst tail

Every lever in this lesson is paid for somewhere that is not the bill.

Utilisation buys money and sells the tail, at an exactly known exchange rate. Both sides are pure ratios, independent of every price and of the service time:

compute falls by u_to ÷ u_from · p99 wait rises by (1 − u_from) ÷ (1 − u_to) · bill saving = compute’s share × (1 − u_from ÷ u_to)

20% to 60% is exactly 3× on compute and exactly 2× on the p99, always. The third expression is the one that decides whether to do it, and it is bounded by a share: 5.6% of this bill for double the p99. The queueing lesson is the other half of this sentence — it writes the same utilisation as ρ, and response time as a multiple of service time is 1/(1−ρ), which is why the last few points of utilisation cost so much more tail than the first few.

And the part that is not on the bill at all: headroom is what absorbs a failure. Three zones at 60% utilisation become two zones at 90% when one is lost, where the p99 is 46 service times — 4× the 11.5 you had been living with, and 8× the 5.8 you had at 20%. The same failure at 20% lands you at 30%, where the p99 is 6.6 and nobody notices. The 5.6% you saved was the price of surviving a zone loss, which makes it a reliability decision that arrived disguised as a cost decision.

Cutting response bytes moves cost into latency or into CPU. Compression is a trade between two terms you now have numbers for: halving the response removes half of a 34% line item and adds to a 5% one, so it pays unless compressing costs more than 3.4× the CPU the request already spends — which no compressor does. That is the useful version of “compression is worth it”: not a rule of thumb, a break-even you can check. Paginating a response into three round trips to shrink each one is the other kind of answer, and it adds two RTTs to the user’s experience for a saving that appears on the invoice and never on the latency graph.

Cutting logs cuts what you can see during an incident. The 317-byte threshold does not mean log less than 317 bytes; it means know that you are choosing. Sampling, structured events instead of lines, dropping successful-request logs and keeping errors, and moving to a per-instance-hour self-hosted stack are all real answers — and the last one converts an observability invoice into instance-hours and storage rather than removing the cost.

Reserved capacity and commitments trade money for the ability to change your mind. A one-year commitment is a large discount on the term that was 5% of your bill, in exchange for making a fleet reduction — the only mechanism by which latency work saves money — worth nothing for a year. It also breaks the model above: instances stops being a variable you control monthly.

Integer fleets mean a small service cannot win. A perfect 2× latency improvement on a three-instance fleet returns 1.5×, because three cannot become 1.5. The premium is a sawtooth in the request rate — 750 rps needs 4.29 instances and you hold five, wasting 16.7% of the compute bill, while 700 rps needs exactly four and wastes nothing.

And the limit of this whole exercise: none of it is computable without attribution. Every number on this page divides a total by a request count, and a real bill arrives as one figure for an account containing several services, shared clusters, shared load balancers and a data platform. Getting from that to a per-request figure is a tagging and labelling problem, and it is engineering work with no user-visible output — which is why it does not get done, and why “what does a million requests cost?” is a question most organisations genuinely cannot answer. The decomposition is the easy half.

The bill and the tail latency are the same knob turned in opposite directions, and the exchange rate is knowable: 20% to 60% utilisation is 3× the compute efficiency, 2× the p99, and a saving on the whole bill capped at compute’s share of it. Decide it as a trade, with both numbers written down.

07

In the wild

This is the one slot where the no-web-access constraint bites, and the honest response is to cite structural facts — things about the shape of a pricing model, which change slowly — rather than figures or announcements, which change weekly. Every claim below is checkable on your provider’s current pricing page in about ten minutes, and that check is the actual exercise.

Two-axis billing is universal, and it is the most under-read fact about storage. Open any object-storage pricing page and you will find at least two tables: one priced per GB-month and one priced per thousand or per million operations, usually with requests split into classes so that writes cost roughly an order of magnitude more than reads per unit. The clearest teaching instrument is a database that puts the asymmetry in its billing unit: DynamoDB’s read and write capacity units are separate currencies, and a write unit costs several times a read unit. Look up the current ratio rather than trusting mine — the point is that the asymmetry is named in the pricing model, so a design that turns a read into a write has changed cost shape and not just cost.

Managed observability is priced per ingested byte and per time series, which makes cardinality a billing decision. This is why every managed platform’s documentation has a page about controlling cardinality, and why “high-cardinality label” is a phrase engineers learn from an invoice rather than from a design review. Self-hosting does not remove the cost; it converts it into instance-hours, storage and an operator, which are the terms in this model rather than a line on someone else’s invoice.

Cross-zone and inter-region transfer are billed separately from internet egress, and the bytes never leave the provider. Check specifically whether your provider bills both the sending and the receiving side, because several do, and whether same-zone traffic is free — because that is what makes where a byte is an architectural decision rather than an operational detail. This is the line item most likely to be missing from your mental model of your own system.

Egress pricing has been the subject of sustained public argument, and it is worth reading the argument rather than the numbers. Cloudflare’s Bandwidth Alliance campaigned on the position that per-GB egress prices bear little relation to the cost of moving a byte; Corey Quinn’s Last Week in AWS has made the same case at length, along with the more useful general claim that a cloud bill is an architecture document. There has also been regulatory pressure in the EU that led major providers to waive egress charges for customers leaving — which is a switching-cost remedy and not a price cut for anything you do day to day. All of that is a reason to check the current terms, not a reason to trust a figure in a lesson.

Nobody can compute unit cost without attribution, and there is now a standard for the input. The FinOps Foundation’s FOCUS specification exists precisely because every provider’s billing export had a different schema, so “cost per request per tenant” required a bespoke pipeline per cloud. If you want to do this exercise on your own system, the tagging and labelling work comes first and it is the part that takes a quarter.

What I could not verify, stated plainly. Every price in src/sim/unitcost.mjs is an invented input chosen to be plausible, dated 2026-08, and marked low confidence. No absolute dollar figure on this page is evidence of anything. What the page does claim, and what its tests assert, is that the crossovers are ratios: scale every price in the vector by any factor and not one of them moves. That is the part worth carrying, and it is the reason the arithmetic is worth doing with your own numbers rather than reading mine.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Compute cost per request is instance-hours divided by requests served, so no latency term appears in it and a latency win is worth zero until the fleet shrinks
  • Egress overtakes compute at the response size where the gigabytes an instance-hour buys divided by the requests an instance-hour serves equals the response size
  • Observability is billed per ingested byte and per time series, so the log-bytes threshold is tighter than the response-bytes threshold by exactly the ratio of the two per-GB prices
  • Affordable cardinality is your monthly instance-hours times the ratio of the instance-hour price to the series-month price
  • Storage is billed on two axes at once, so small objects pay for operations and large ones pay for capacity, and the crossover moves with retention rather than with request volume
  • Raising utilisation divides compute by the ratio of the two utilisations and multiplies the p99 wait by (1−u_from)/(1−u_to), while the saving on the whole bill is capped at compute's share of it

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A team spends a quarter halving the p50 of a service. The latency graph is convincingly better. The monthly bill is unchanged to within noise, and nothing is broken. What happened?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The compute bill is instance-hours × price ÷ requests served, and latency is not in that expression. Halving service time doubles what one instance could serve, which changes the bill only by letting you hold fewer instances — and the fleet did not change, because the autoscaler's minimum, the replica count, or a capacity commitment held it. Instance-hours were identical, so the bill was identical. Then the second half: even if the fleet had halved perfectly, compute was 5% of this bill, so a flawless 2× would have removed 2.5% of it.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Your API returns 12 KB of JSON. Without looking up a single price, how would you decide whether egress or compute is the larger line item — and what would you need to know?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Compare two quantities, not two prices. Ask how many gigabytes of egress one instance-hour of money buys, and how many requests one instance serves in an hour. The first divided by the second is your per-request byte allowance; above it, egress costs more than the machine. At the inputs in this lesson that is 1,764 bytes — an instance-hour buys 1.11 GB and serves 630,000 requests. 12 KB is 6.8× past it. The only two facts you needed were a price ratio and a throughput, and neither absolute price mattered.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why does adding one metric label with 200 distinct values cost more than it looks, and what is the right thing to compare that cost against?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the billing unit is a series — one unique label combination — so a label with 200 values multiplies your series count by 200 across every metric that carries it, and 80 metrics become 16,000 series. Compare it against instance-hours: affordable cardinality is monthly instance-hours × (instance-hour price ÷ series-month price), which for an eleven-instance fleet at a 2:1 price ratio is about 16,457 series. One label can therefore cost more than the fleet it was added to describe, and it is billed monthly whether or not anyone queries it.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A fleet running at 20% utilisation is packed to 60%. State exactly what was bought and exactly what was paid, and say what else changed that is not on the bill.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Bought: compute divided by 0.6/0.2 = 3 exactly — and, because compute was 5% of this bill, 5.6% of the total. Paid: the p99 response time multiplied by (1−0.2)/(1−0.6) = 2 exactly, from 5.8 to 11.5 service times. Not on the bill: the failure headroom. Three zones at 60% become two zones at 90% when one is lost, where the p99 is 46 service times — 8× the original. At 20% the same failure lands you at 30% and 6.6×. The 5.6% was the price of surviving a zone.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A team running its own hardware in a colocation facility says none of this applies: the servers are bought and paid for, so a request costs nothing. Which parts of the decomposition survive, which vanish, and what replaces them?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The shape survives and the denominators change. What vanishes: the hourly instance charge becomes capex plus power, so the marginal cost of one more request on an already-running server really is near zero — which correctly inverts the utilisation argument, because idle capacity you already own is a sunk cost rather than a recurring one. What survives unchanged: transit and peering bills, priced per 95th-percentile megabit rather than per GB but still proportional to bytes out; the log and metric storage, which is now an array you bought and an operator you employ rather than a per-GB invoice; and the cross-site links between facilities, which are the on-prem cross-AZ charge. What replaces the hourly price is amortisation plus power plus the operator — divide the three-year cost of a rack by the requests it serves and you are doing the same division. The reason the exercise is still worth doing is that it produces the same finding: the term the team can measure most easily (CPU) is not the term that dominates, and the terms that dominate have no per-request meter on them at all.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a unit cost is a total divided by a count, and effort flows to whichever term is easiest to measure rather than to whichever term is largest — so the biggest line item is routinely the one with no instrument attached to it. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Airline cost per seat-mile. Fuel is metered continuously and gets the attention; crew, maintenance, gate fees and the empty seat are most of the cost, and the empty seat has no meter at all — which is why load factor, not fuel efficiency, is the number airlines actually manage. A restaurant plate. Food is roughly a third of the cost and is the only part with a receipt, so chefs optimise the food while labour and rent decide the business. Freight per parcel. Line haul is easy to price per kilometre and the last mile is most of the cost, which is why the industry's structural changes have all been about the last mile. Cost per mile of owning a car. Fuel is visible at every fill-up and depreciation is larger and silent. The common structure in all four: the instrument creates the attention, so the term without an instrument grows unchallenged — and the fix is always the same, which is to divide the whole total by the count once, in public, before anyone optimises anything.
    Did you have that?
