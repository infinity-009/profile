Tier 1 · The network Head-of-line blocking explained well nowhere else

Thirty-two of the forty files were delayed without losing a packet of their own

HTTP/2 put forty requests on one connection and deleted the blocking that HTTP/1.1's six-connection workaround existed to hide. It did not delete the other one: TCP hands the application a single byte order, so one lost segment stalls every stream behind it — including the thirty-two that lost nothing. HTTP/3 moves the blocking below loss recovery. It cannot move the coupling nobody can remove, because one connection is still one congestion window.

Read first — latency-numberspercentilestail-at-scale

01

The bytes arrived. The browser has nothing to show.

A page fetches 40 files totalling 600 KB over a 10 Mbit/s link with a 50-millisecond round trip. One packet in fifty is lost — an ordinary congested cell or a saturated home router, not an incident. The team has just moved the site from HTTP/1.1 to HTTP/2, which is the recommended upgrade and does exactly what it advertises: all forty requests go out in the first round trip instead of six at a time.

Here is what the connection then does, measured in the model this lesson is built on:

  • Thirty-eight of the forty files finish later than they would have because of a lost packet.
  • Six of the forty actually lost one. Thirty-two were delayed without a single packet of their own going missing: their bytes were transmitted once and arrived once. Two were untouched.
  • 34% of every packet that reaches the machine sits in a receive buffer that the application is not allowed to read yet.
  • The link is 93% busy. No server metric moves. For thirty-two of those files, no packet capture anywhere shows a retransmission that belongs to them.

Turn the loss rate up and it gets stranger. At 9% loss the upgrade becomes a downgrade: the same forty files arrive sooner over six separate HTTP/1.1 connections than over one HTTP/2 connection, on the same link, at the same loss rate, carrying the same bytes.

Nothing here is a bug in HTTP/2, and nothing here is fixed by tuning it. What happened is that the upgrade removed one blocking problem and made a second one visible — one that had always been there and had been hidden by the very workaround HTTP/2 was designed to replace.

02

The obvious fix is one connection, and here is where it stops working

Work through the fixes in the order the web actually tried them, because each one is the obvious response to the last.

HTTP/1.1 lets a connection carry one request at a time. RFC 9112 §9.3.2 names the consequence in the specification itself: “Multiple connections are typically used to avoid the ‘head-of-line blocking’ problem, wherein a request that takes significant server-side processing and/or transfers very large content would block subsequent requests on the same connection.” So browsers opened several connections per host. That is where the number six comes from, and it is not in any specification — RFC 9112 §9.4 explicitly declines to give one, noting that “previous revisions of HTTP gave a specific number of connections as a ceiling, but this was found to be impractical.” Six is a constant in Chromium’s source, and the comment beside it is worth reading: “Default to allow up to 6 connections per host. Experiment and tuning may try other values (greater than 0). Too large may cause many problems, such as home routers blocking the connections!?!?”

Six connections is a concurrency ceiling, and a ceiling starves a link. At most six responses are in flight, so the connection set can only offer the link as many packets as those six responses have left. Six responses averaging 10.6 packets is 63 packets against a 43-packet window, which nearly fills it; the link runs at 89%. Ninety small resources on the same link is six responses averaging 2.3 packets — 14 packets against 43 — and the link runs at 32%. The number six does not scale with the page, and pages grew.

So put every request on one connection. That is HTTP/2, and it works: forty streams all have data ready, the link runs at 98%, and the median resource arrives in 200 ms instead of 300 ms.

Then a packet is lost, and the thing nobody chose becomes visible.

Predict Forty resources, 422 packets, all interleaved on one connection that delivers bytes strictly in order. One packet in a hundred is lost, and the average resource is only 10.6 packets. What share of the forty resources arrives without being delayed by a lost packet?
About 19% from the sizes alone and 22% measured. The 90% answer is 0.99^10.6 = 0.90 — the chance your own ten and a half packets all survive — and it is the right arithmetic with the wrong exponent. On a connection delivered in one byte order, a stream cannot be read past a hole anywhere earlier in that order, so its exposure is every packet ahead of it on the connection: Σ min(size_j, size_s), which is 80 packets for the smallest file on this page and all 422 for the largest, averaging 188. 0.99^188 = 0.19. The exponent is a property of the connection's total traffic, so every stream you add to a connection makes every stream already on it more fragile. Show it without answering

Three readings, and the third is the one that changes what you do.

At zero loss, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 are byte-for-byte identical. No crimson appears anywhere, and none can. Everything HTTP/3 does about blocking is conditional on something being lost, so a comparison that shows it ahead at zero loss is measuring something else. This is asserted as a test rather than left as a claim, because it is the assertion that catches a model built to flatter its own conclusion.

The crossover is a property of the traffic, not of the protocol. Six large files over six connections: one shared connection is worse from the first lost packet, because six connections already fill the link and multiplexing has nothing left to buy. Forty mixed resources: the crossing is at 9%. Ninety small resources: it never happens, at any loss rate the model will accept.

Switching from HTTP/2 to HTTP/3 does not change when the page finishes. The last byte lands at the same millisecond. Same connection, same rate, same lost packets, same retransmissions. What changes is when each individual resource becomes readable — and if the thing you measure is “page complete”, you will measure no improvement and conclude, correctly, that nothing happened.

03

The intuition: one lane, forty cars, and a rule about arrival order

Forget protocols. There is a single-lane road between a warehouse and a shop.

HTTP/1.1 is one van at a time. The van drives out, unloads, and drives back before the next order can even be placed. Six vans on six roads is six orders in flight, and that is why the number six exists — it is not a design, it is a way of getting six things moving on a road system that only understands one thing at a time.

HTTP/2 is one lane with forty cars on it, nose to tail. This is a much better use of the road: no gaps, no idle asphalt, forty orders in flight instead of six. And it comes with a rule that nobody asked for and everybody accepted, because it is how the road has always worked: the shop will not accept a car until every car ahead of it has been accepted. One breakdown and thirty-nine cars sit behind it, engines running, cargo intact, refused at the gate.

HTTP/3 is the same lane, the same forty cars, and the shop accepting each car as it arrives. Nothing about the road changed. Nothing about the number of cars changed. One rule changed.

Which is the honest end of the analogy, and worth staying with for a moment: the road is still one lane. Forty cars on one lane get one lane’s worth of throughput between them, and no rule about acceptance order alters that. Six roads is six roads. When people say HTTP/3 “fixes head-of-line blocking” they are describing the gate, and most of what they are unhappy about is the lane.

04

The mechanism: the blocking moved, and where it lives sets what a loss costs

Head-of-line blocking is a single structural condition: a shared channel that must be handed over in the order it was filled. It has appeared at three different layers of the web stack, and each protocol version removed it from one layer by relying on a layer that still had it.

Where the blocking livesWhat one loss or one slow response costsSaid where
HTTP/1.1 — one request outstanding per connectionEvery later request on that connection waits, whether or not anything was lost. This is a scheduling delay: the next request has not been sent yet.RFC 9113 §1: “HTTP/1.1 added request pipelining, but this only partially addressed request concurrency and still suffers from application-layer head-of-line blocking. Therefore, HTTP/1.0 and HTTP/1.1 clients use multiple connections to a server to make concurrent requests.”
HTTP/2 — many streams, one TCP connectionApplication-layer blocking is gone. Transport-layer blocking is not: every stream’s bytes are in one order, so one lost segment holds every stream sent after it. This is a delivery delay — the bytes are already in the machine.RFC 9113 §2: “Note, however, that TCP head-of-line blocking is not addressed by this protocol.” And RFC 9114 §1.1: “because the parallel nature of HTTP/2’s multiplexing is not visible to TCP’s loss recovery mechanisms, a lost or reordered packet causes all active transactions to experience a stall regardless of whether that transaction was directly impacted by the lost packet.”
HTTP/3 over QUIC — streams below the loss-recovery boundaryA loss holds only the streams whose bytes were in the lost packet.RFC 9000 §2: “Streams can be created by either endpoint, can concurrently send data interleaved with other streams… QUIC does not provide any means of ensuring ordering between bytes on different streams.” Stated as a consequence in RFC 9308 §4.1: “QUIC’s stream multiplexing feature allows applications to run multiple streams over a single connection without head-of-line blocking between streams.”

A detail in that last row is worth noticing: the phrase “head-of-line blocking” does not appear anywhere in RFC 9000. QUIC does not describe itself as fixing the problem. It describes a transport in which ordering between streams is simply not a guarantee it makes, and the absence of the problem is a consequence rather than a feature. That is usually how a good structural fix reads in a specification.

The exposure formula, and the only thing that differs between the three

The chance a stream is not delayed by loss at all is a plain binomial. What changes between the transports is not the expression — it is the exponent.

P(stream is undelayed) = (1 − p)^(packets ahead of it in its delivery order)

p — per-packet loss rate. HTTP/3: the exponent is the stream’s own packets. HTTP/2: it is every packet ahead of it on the connection. HTTP/1.1 with k connections: it is every packet ahead of it on its connection, which carries roughly 1/k of the bytes.

For the forty-file page at 1% loss, with the average resource at 10.6 packets on a connection carrying 422:

The middle column is computed from the file sizes and the round-robin schedule, never read out of the simulation, which is what makes the right column a check on the model rather than a restatement of it. The exponents are the argument: a stream on one shared in-order connection is exposed to the connection’s total traffic, so adding streams to a connection makes every stream already on it more fragile, and a small file is no safer than a large one.

What it actually costs, which is less than the mechanism suggests

The mechanism sounds like it should cost one round trip per lost packet. It does not, and getting this wrong in the optimistic direction is how you end up expecting HTTP/3 to be transformative.

A stream’s last packet goes out in some round trip. A packet lost in an earlier round trip was already retransmitted and lands at the same boundary, so it costs this stream nothing. Only a loss in the same round trip delays it, and then by exactly one retransmission. On average half a round trip of connection traffic sits ahead of a stream’s last packet, which is half the bandwidth-delay product in packets:

cost per stream ≈ RTT · (1 − (1 − p)^(BDP/2))

BDP/2 = 21.5 packets on a 10 Mbit/s, 50 ms link. At 1% loss that is 9.7 ms against 9.4 ms measured; at 2%, 17.6 ms against 17.4 ms; at 3%, 24.0 ms against 24.7 ms. Note the shape: it is bounded by one RTT, because there is only ever one round trip in flight.

That bound is the whole reason the crossover in the rig sits near 9% and not near the 2% the folklore quotes. Ordering coupling has to buy back HTTP/1.1’s structural handicap — 0.775 round trips of mean completion, from the starved link — and at 2% loss it is only worth 0.36 of one. Above about 5% the derivation stops holding, because retransmissions start being lost too and one round trip is no longer the ceiling; saying where a model stops working is part of the model.

And the dependency QUIC had to put back

Removing ordering between streams breaks header compression, and the fix is the clearest evidence that this problem is structural rather than solved.

HTTP/2’s HPACK compresses headers against a dynamic table that both ends build up as the connection runs. It works because both ends process every frame in the same total order. RFC 9204 §1 is direct about the consequence: “if HPACK were used for HTTP/3, it would induce head-of-line blocking for field sections due to built-in assumptions of a total ordering across frames on all streams.”

So QPACK sends table updates on their own stream and lets a request reference entries by an insert count. If a request’s headers reference an entry the decoder has not received yet, RFC 9204 §2.1.2: “the stream cannot be processed immediately and is considered ‘blocked’.” The dependency is back — a stream held up by data belonging to no stream in particular — and the protocol’s answer is not to remove it but to bound it: the decoder advertises SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS, and “an encoder MUST limit the number of streams that could become blocked to the value of SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS at all times.” RFC 9114 §2.2.1 describes what that setting is for in one line: it “gives an encoder some control over how much head-of-line blocking can be caused by compression.”

Set the limit to zero and you get no blocking and worse compression, because the encoder may only reference entries it knows have already landed. Set it high and you compress better and admit more blocking. It is a dial between the two, which is what a structural trade-off looks like when somebody has been honest about it.

The pattern is one sentence: six connections is worth having exactly when six responses can fill the link. With six large files it fills it completely, and a shared connection is pure cost. With ninety small ones it fills a third of it, and no loss rate saves it. The middle row is the case everybody actually has, and the crossing is at a loss rate high enough that ordinary networks do not reach it — 9% is a link most applications would already call broken.

Which brings up the number that has to be said plainly, because it is the most quoted claim about this topic. The published result that HTTP/2 loses to HTTP/1.1 above roughly 2% loss is real, and head-of-line blocking is not mostly what causes it. This model holds the aggregate rate equal on purpose, so it isolates ordering — and ordering alone puts the crossing at 9%. Robin Marx, who wrote the definitive explanation of HOL blocking in HTTP/3, attributes the crossover to the other mechanism in the same breath: “in some cases, HTTP/2 on a single connection has a hard time being faster than or even as fast as HTTP/1.1 on 6 connections. This is mainly due to TCP’s congestion control mechanism.” One connection recovers from loss with one window; six connections recover with six, five of which never saw the loss. That is a rate coupling, it is the subject of the previous lesson, and it is doing most of the work in the 2% figure.

I expected bursty loss to make head-of-line blocking worse. A hole five packets wide ought to hurt more than a hole one packet wide. Holding the marginal loss rate fixed and varying only the correlation:

Sander, Kunze and Wehrle measured the same direction on real browsers in 2022 — moving from random loss to bursts of five pushed HTTP/3’s measured advantage over HTTP/2 below their baseline. The practical consequence is uncomfortable and worth carrying: the loss pattern a tail-dropping router actually produces is a burst, and a burst is the pattern that makes QUIC’s headline feature worth least.

06

The trade-off: six loss domains or one shared fate, and QUIC's bill in CPU and middleboxes

There are three structures here and none of them is free.

Six connections buys six independent loss domains and pays for them in fairness, memory and a concurrency ceiling. Six TCP flows to one host means six congestion windows probing the same bottleneck independently, which takes more than a single flow’s fair share from everything else on the link — that is why RFC 9112 §9.4 tells clients to “be conservative when opening multiple connections” and why the Chromium comment worries about home routers. Server-side it is six sockets, six TLS sessions and six sets of buffers per client. And the ceiling is the real cost: at most six responses in flight, so the link runs at 32% on a page of ninety small files. Six was chosen when pages had a dozen resources. Nobody re-derives a constant.

One connection buys a full pipe and pays for it with one shared fate. Every stream is exposed to the connection’s total traffic rather than its own — that is the exponent in slot 4 and it is the whole cost. It is also one point of failure in a second sense: one connection is one path, one NAT binding, one middlebox state entry. When it breaks, forty streams break together, and forty requests need to be re-issued rather than one sixth of them.

QUIC removes ordering coupling and charges for it in CPU, in reachability and in operability.

  • CPU per packet. QUIC runs in user space over UDP, so per-packet work that TCP does once in the kernel — with hardware segmentation offload, checksum offload and a decade of tuning behind it — is done in the application instead, per packet, plus per-packet encryption. This is the reason a QUIC server can cost noticeably more CPU per gigabit than a TLS-over-TCP one, and it is the reason it matters most for the operator and least for the user.
  • Some networks do not carry it. UDP on port 443 is blocked or rate-limited on a non-negligible number of enterprise and mobile networks, so every HTTP/3 client needs a working TCP fallback path and every deployment needs to keep both stacks alive. You do not get to delete the thing you replaced.
  • It defeats the middleboxes that expected TCP. Load balancers that route on TCP state, firewalls that inspect a cleartext handshake, transparent caches, DDoS scrubbers keyed on SYN behaviour, WAN accelerators that terminate connections — all of them read a TCP flow and none of them reads a QUIC one, because QUIC encrypts almost all of its transport header. That opacity is deliberate and it is the reason QUIC could be changed at all after deployment; it is also a real migration bill that lands on the network team rather than the application team.
  • And it does not touch the coupling that dominates. One connection is one congestion-control domain. Marx again, and this is the sentence to remember: “crucially however, QUIC’s congestion control is still done at the connection level, and not per-stream.” Forty streams share one rate. If the reason your small requests are slow is that a large one is saturating the connection, HTTP/3 will not help you and the model in this lesson says so exactly: the p99 does not move.

The honest summary. If your losses are rare, one connection is better and it is not close. If your losses are frequent and independent, ordering coupling is worth up to about one round trip per resource and HTTP/3 recovers it. If your losses are frequent and bursty — which is what a full queue produces — most of that is already gone. And in every case, the thing that decides whether two workloads should share a connection at all is not the loss rate: it is whether one of them can starve the other, which is a rate question that no protocol version answers.

07

In the wild

The measurement that named the trade-off, in 2014. Wang, Balasubramanian, Krishnamurthy and Wetherall, How Speedy is SPDY? (NSDI 2014), tested SPDY — HTTP/2’s direct predecessor and the same one-connection design — against HTTP/1.1 across the Alexa top 200. Their finding is one sentence and it is this whole lesson: “most SPDY benefits stem from the use of a single TCP connection, but the same feature is also detrimental under high packet loss.” Note the date. The cost of one shared connection was measured and published five years before HTTP/3 was standardised, which is a reasonable model for how systems knowledge actually moves.

Google’s deployment, and what it credits. Langley et al., The QUIC Transport Protocol: Design and Internet-Scale Deployment (SIGCOMM 2017), reports QUIC serving Chrome and YouTube at a scale where “7% of Internet traffic is now QUIC”, with Google Search latency down 8.0% on desktop and 3.6% on mobile, and YouTube rebuffer rates down 18.0% and 15.3%. Read the attribution carefully: those gains are credited to lower-latency connection establishment, better loss recovery and reduced head-of-line blocking together. Nobody has ever published a clean decomposition of that into its three parts, which is worth knowing before you quote the 8% as evidence for any one of them.

The person who explained it best does not oversell it. Robin Marx, Head-of-Line Blocking in QUIC and HTTP/3: The Details (Web Performance Calendar, 3 December 2020) is the reference explanation, and its conclusion is a caution: “my personal gut feeling (which is backed by my results from several early experiments) says that QUIC’s HOL blocking removal probably won’t actually help all that much for Web performance.” Two years later Sander, Kunze and Wehrle (Analyzing the Influence of Resource Prioritization on HTTP/3 HOL Blocking and Performance, TMA 2022) measured it on instrumented browsers and found the benefit real but sensitive: strongest under high random loss and low bandwidth, and largely gone once loss came in bursts of five. Both findings match this lesson’s model, which was not fitted to either.

And the state of play, dated. Cloudflare’s Radar 2025 Year in Review, on data through 2 December 2025, puts request shares at HTTP/2 50%, HTTP/1.x 29%, HTTP/3 21%, and notes the shares are “largely unchanged from 2024 — HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 gained just fractions of a percentage point this year.” Two things in that. Nearly three in ten requests still use HTTP/1.x, so the six-connection workaround is load-bearing in 2026 and the top row of the table in slot 4 is not history. And HTTP/3 has plateaued at a fifth of traffic six years after standardisation, on a web where the protocol is available by default in every major browser. A protocol whose headline benefit was transformative would not sit there. The plateau is not an argument against HTTP/3; it is the market agreeing with the measurement.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The chance a stream is delayed by no loss at all is (1−p) raised to the number of packets ahead of it on its connection — and on one shared in-order connection that exponent is the connection's whole traffic, not the stream's own size
  • HTTP/2 removed application-layer head-of-line blocking and did not remove transport-layer head-of-line blocking; RFC 9113 says so in its own overview
  • Ordering coupling costs about one round trip per round trip that contains a loss, not one round trip per lost packet, so it is bounded by roughly one RTT until retransmissions themselves start being lost
  • QUIC removes ordering coupling and not rate coupling: one connection is one congestion-control domain, so HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 finish the same transfer at the same instant and differ only in the middle of the distribution
  • Six connections is a browser constant rather than a specification number, and what it buys is a concurrency ceiling that starves the link whenever the resources are smaller than the link's share
  • QPACK reintroduces a cross-stream dependency through its shared dynamic table, which is why it has a negotiated limit on how many streams may be blocked at once
  • At a fixed marginal loss rate, bursty loss produces LESS head-of-line blocking than independent loss, because the cost is charged per hole and a burst of five is one hole rather than five

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Forty resources totalling 422 packets share one connection that delivers bytes in order. One packet in a hundred is lost. The average resource is 10.6 packets. What share of the forty arrives without being delayed by a lost packet, and why is it not 90%?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About one in five — 19% from the sizes, 22% measured. The 90% answer is 0.99^10.6, the chance your own ten and a half packets all survive, and it is the right arithmetic for the wrong exponent. On a connection delivered in one byte order a stream cannot be read past a hole anywhere earlier in that order, so its exposure is every packet ahead of it on the connection. Under round-robin that is Σ min(size_j, size_s): 80 packets for the smallest resource on the page and all 422 for the largest, averaging 188. So 0.99^188 = 19%. The exponent is a property of the connection's total traffic and the stream's own size barely enters it — which is why adding more streams to a connection makes every existing stream more fragile.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    At zero loss, should HTTP/3 beat HTTP/2 on the same connection and the same workload? And at 10% loss, which of the two finishes the whole transfer first?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Neither, and neither. At zero loss they are byte-for-byte identical, because per-stream delivery can only pay off when something is missing — a model that shows HTTP/3 ahead at p = 0 has been built to flatter it, which is why that identity is asserted as a test. At 10% loss they finish at exactly the same instant: same connection, same rate, same packets lost, same retransmissions, so the same last byte. Measured p99 is 679 ms for both. What changes is the middle: HTTP/2's median is 328 ms and HTTP/3's is 228 ms. QUIC removes the ordering coupling between streams and not the rate coupling, because one connection is still one congestion-control domain.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    The mechanism says a lost segment stalls every stream behind it for a retransmission. Estimate what that costs a stream, and explain why the answer is not "one round trip per lost packet".

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About RTT · (1 − (1−p)^(BDP/2)), and the important thing about that expression is that it is bounded by one round trip. A stream's last packet goes out in some round; a packet lost in an earlier round was already retransmitted and lands at the same boundary, so it costs nothing. Only a loss in the same round delays the stream, and then by one round. On average half a round of connection traffic sits ahead of a stream's last packet — 21.5 packets on a 43-packet window — so at 2% loss the cost is 50 · (1 − 0.98^21.5) = 17.6 ms against 17.4 ms measured, and at 1% it is 9.7 ms against 9.4 ms. Losses inside one round trip are recovered together. Above about 5% the bound breaks, because retransmissions start being lost too.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Six connections is the number every browser uses. What decides whether it is a good number, and what happens to it as pages grow?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is a concurrency ceiling, and what decides its value is whether six responses in flight can fill the link. Six responses averaging 10.6 packets is 63 packets against a 43-packet window, so on a page it very nearly does — the link runs at 89% and the handicap is 0.775 round trips of mean completion. Ninety small resources on the same link is six responses averaging 2.3 packets, or 14 packets against 43: the link runs at 32% and one connection is nearly four times faster at any loss rate. The number does not scale with the page, and pages grew. It is also not a specification number — RFC 9112 §9.4 explicitly declines to give one, and 6 is a constant in net/socket/client_socket_pool_manager.cc whose comment worries about home routers blocking the connections.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    You are told that keeping losses in tight bursts rather than spreading them out will make head-of-line blocking worse. Is that right?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it makes it better, and this was the number that corrected me. Head-of-line blocking is charged per hole, not per lost packet: a burst of five consecutive packets is one hole and one retransmission round, where five scattered losses are five holes at five different points in the byte order. Holding the marginal loss rate at 2% and moving from independent loss to bursts of five takes the cost from 0.36 to 0.12 round trips and the share of untouched resources from 6% to 51%. Sander, Kunze and Wehrle measured the same direction on real browsers in 2022: moving from random loss to bursts of five pushed HTTP/3's advantage over HTTP/2 below their baseline. The consequence for practice is uncomfortable — the loss pattern that a tail-dropping router produces is the pattern that makes QUIC's headline feature worth least.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A service opens one gRPC channel to a data-access tier and multiplexes 200 concurrent calls on it. Almost all return 2 KB. One call streams a 40 MB export. Latency for the small calls degrades badly whenever that export is running, and no server-side metric shows anything wrong. What is the mechanism, and what are the two fixes?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    gRPC runs over HTTP/2, so all 200 calls share one TCP connection and one byte order. The mechanism is the same one in two layers at once. First, rate: 201 streams share one congestion window, so the small calls get 1/201 of it — that part is not head-of-line blocking and no protocol version fixes it. Second, ordering: any segment lost while the export is saturating the connection sits in front of whatever small responses were interleaved behind it, and those responses are held in the receiver's buffer, already delivered by the network and unreadable by the application. Server metrics show nothing because the server did its work and handed the bytes to the kernel. The two fixes are structural, not tuning: separate the export onto its own connection or channel, which is what six connections were for and is the right instinct here; or move to HTTP/3, which removes the ordering half and leaves the rate half exactly where it was. If you do the second and expect the small calls to stop being starved, you will be disappointed, and the reason is in the word "connection".
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a shared channel whose contents must be handed over in the order they entered means one delayed item stalls every item behind it, however healthy those items are — and the fix is to make the units independently completable rather than to make the channel faster. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The Suez Canal, March 2021. One ship, the Ever Given, ran aground across the channel. Several hundred other ships had nothing wrong with them, were fully crewed, fully fuelled and physically present at the entrance, and none of them could move — the canal's defining property is that it is a single ordered queue with no overtaking. Note which fixes work and which do not: a faster ship does not help, and neither does a bigger canal traffic-management system. What helps is passing places — the second Suez lane built in 2015 is the reason the northbound convoy was less affected — which is exactly HTTP/1.1's six connections, and reordering permission, which is exactly QUIC. Other instances: a single-track railway, where one late train delays every train behind it and the engineering answer is passing loops rather than faster locomotives; a supermarket checkout where a price check freezes eight trolleys that are ready to pay; a hospital with one operating theatre, where one complication postpones a whole day's list. In every case the tell is the same: the stalled units are individually fine, so there is nobody to blame and nothing to fix at the level where the delay is visible.
    Did you have that?
