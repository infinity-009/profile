Tier 10 · AI-era systems Distributed training explained well nowhere else

At 16,384 GPUs something breaks every 2.78 hours, and no synchronous checkpoint interval whatsoever reaches 90% effective training time

gpu-scheduling-gang-and-topology-aware priced getting a large gang of GPUs and placing it well; gpu-memory-arithmetic priced fitting the model on them. This lesson prices the thing that decides whether a months-long run finishes: interruptions. Meta measured 466 job interruptions across 16,384 H100s in 54 days — roughly one every 2.78 hours, sustained, with 419 of them unexpected and 78% of those hardware-attributable — and still reported over 90% effective training time. Those two numbers are not compatible with synchronous checkpointing at any interval. Reaching the second while living with the first needs two independent changes at once, and this lesson is about proving which two.

Read first — gpu-scheduling-gang-and-topology-awaregpu-memory-arithmetic

01

466 interruptions in 54 days, and a reported 90% effective training time

Meta’s Llama 3 herd paper publishes two numbers about the same 405B-parameter pre-training run that are hard to hold in mind together. On 16,384 H100s over a 54-day snapshot there were 466 job interruptions — 47 of them planned maintenance and 419 unexpected, with “approximately 78%” of the unexpected ones attributed to confirmed hardware issues, so roughly 327 events. That is 8.63 interruptions per day, or one every 2.78 hours, for the whole two months. And the same paper reports over 90% effective training time.

gpu-scheduling-gang-and-topology-aware already established why a synchronous job of this size is fragile in a way a single-GPU job is not: it has no way to run with a missing worker, so any one of 16,384 cards falling over stops all of them. This lesson is the arithmetic of living with that, and the arithmetic says the two published numbers are only compatible under a specific pair of conditions — neither of which is “we picked a good checkpoint interval.”

02

The belief this breaks: 'the checkpoint interval is the dial'

The belief this has to break
Failures at scale are a checkpointing problem, and checkpointing is a tuning problem. Find the right interval — often enough that you don't lose much work, rarely enough that the saves don't eat your throughput — and effective training time follows.

The interval genuinely has an optimum, and it is genuinely worth finding. That is exactly what makes it the wrong dial: an optimum is the bottom of a bowl, so moving it in either direction makes things worse, and the bottom of the bowl can be far below where you need to be.

Put the Young interval into the effective-time budget and the interval cancels out. What is left is a ceiling that does not mention the interval at all:

best effective training = 1 − sqrt(2 × saveDuration / MTBF) − restart / MTBF

Substituting interval = sqrt(2 × saveDuration × MTBF) into the budget makes the checkpoint term and the discarded-work term equal, each sqrt(saveDuration / (2 × MTBF)). Their sum is sqrt(2 × saveDuration / MTBF), and it depends only on the save duration and the mean time between interruptions. The interval you chose is nowhere in it. Verified as an argmax in distributed-training.test.mjs against a 400-point grid search over intervals from two minutes to eight hours — no interval beats the closed form, which is another way of saying no interval escapes the ceiling.

At 16,384 GPUs that ceiling stays under 90% for every plausible synchronous save-and-restart pair. The best cell in a six-by-six grid — a one-minute full-cluster checkpoint write and a one-minute restart, both close to physically implausible for a 405B optimizer state — reaches 88.5%. The published figure is not reachable by tuning.

Predict 16,384 GPUs, a checkpoint that takes 5 minutes to write synchronously, a 20-minute restart. Across every possible checkpoint interval, what is the best effective training time?
63.5%, at the Young interval of 40.85 minutes. Shorter intervals spend more wall clock writing (a 5-minute interval spends 100% of it writing and makes zero progress); longer ones discard more work per failure (a 6-hour interval loses more than it earns). The optimum is real and it is 63.5%. Swept from 1 minute to 24 hours, at every restart cost in the grid, in distributed-training.test.mjs.

03

The intuition: two walls, and a bowl between them that has a floor

Imagine writing a long exam in a room where the power cuts out at random, roughly every three hours, and every cut wipes whatever you have not yet copied onto a clean sheet. Copying costs writing time. Not copying costs re-writing.

Wall one: copy too often and you are a copyist. Copy every five minutes when a copy takes five minutes and you have stopped writing the exam altogether — you are spending all of your time duplicating. This is not a corner case in the arithmetic: at a 5-minute interval with a 5-minute save, the checkpoint overhead is exactly 100% and effective progress is exactly zero.

Wall two: copy too rarely and every cut is a catastrophe. Copy once every six hours against an outage every 2.78 hours and you will usually be wiped before you copy, so most sheets never survive. The discarded-work term crosses 100% of wall clock and the run makes no net progress at all, however fast the GPUs are.

Between the walls is a bowl, and its bottom is the Young interval. The uncomfortable part is what sets the bottom’s depth. It is not the interval. It is how long a copy takes and how long it takes to get back to writing after a cut — and if the copy takes five minutes and getting restarted takes twenty, the bottom of the bowl is 63.5%, no matter how carefully you place yourself in it.

An optimum tells you where to stand. It says nothing about how high the floor is. Every dial that trades one cost against another has this property, and the standard mistake is to spend the tuning effort on the dial and never ask what the two costs it trades are made of.

04

The mechanism: three sinks, a square root, and where the window actually opens

The failure rate is a per-GPU-day rate, projected. 466 interruptions over 16,384 GPUs and 54 days is 5.2671 × 10⁻⁴ interruptions per GPU-day. That rate is the citation; projecting it to other cluster sizes is the model, and the projection is linear, so MTBF is exactly inverse in cluster size. Doubling the cluster halves the time between interruptions. There is no scale at which this stops being true, which is the whole reason the checkpoint interval that was right at 1,000 GPUs is wrong at 16,000.

MTBF hours = 24 / (gpus × 5.2671 × 10⁻⁴)

44.50 hours at 1,024 GPUs. 2.78 hours at 16,384. 1.39 hours at 32,768. The same physical hardware reliability, restated as a job-level property, and it gets worse with exactly the thing you scale to go faster.

Effective training time has exactly three sinks, and they sum with it to one. The blocking part of each checkpoint write, once per interval. The work discarded on failure, on average half an interval. The restart itself, once per failure.

effective = 1 − blockingSave / interval − (interval / 2 + restart) / MTBF

Two of the three terms move with the interval and in opposite directions, which is what makes an optimum exist; the third does not move with it at all. Differentiating and solving gives interval = sqrt(2 × blockingSave × MTBF) — Young’s 1974 first-order result, which is what this lesson computes. It is a square root, so doubling the save cost only widens the interval by 41% and doubling the cluster narrows it by 29%. effectiveTrainingFraction returns the write term and the failure term, with the last two sinks fused into the second because both are paid once per failure and so share a denominator; the test suite asserts those two plus the effective fraction sum to exactly one until the floor clamps at zero.

Async checkpointing attacks the first sink only. Staging the write to pinned host memory and letting a background thread push it to durable storage means GPU compute is not blocked for the duration of the write. The sim bills the blocking residual at 10% of the full write rather than zero, deliberately — the staging copy is real, and claiming zero would be the same kind of overclaim this lesson is built to catch. That single change also shortens the optimal interval by sqrt(10), or 3.162x, because the interval is set by the blocking cost.

06

The trade-off: a fixed payload versus one the router decides at run time

All-reduce’s cost is set by the model; all-to-all’s is set by the router. The gang-scheduling lesson derived ring all-reduce and the one property that matters here: it moves a fixed payload over fixed edges, so to first order its cost does not grow with GPU count. Expert parallelism’s token dispatch does not have that property. Which expert each token goes to is decided per microbatch, at run time, by a learned router — so the payload is different every step, and the collective finishes when the most-loaded expert finishes. Every other rank waits.

Predict In a 16-way expert-parallel MoE, the router sends one expert exactly twice its fair share of tokens. Token dispatch is a reported 30-60% of the time to execute the MoE layers. How much longer do those layers take?
The imbalance penalty applies to the collective's share, not to everything around it: inflation = 1 + share x penalty. A 2x-hot expert is a penalty of exactly 1.0, so at a 30% share the layers take 1.30x and at a 60% share 1.60x. That composition is asserted at 16 ranks and 8,192 tokens in distributed-training.test.mjs. Two traps worth naming: the whole training step also contains attention, the dense FFN, embedding and the optimizer, so the all-to-all's share of a STEP is lower than its share of the layers and 1.30x-1.60x is only an upper bound on step inflation; and the Zipf-0.4 row in the table below is the same shape at a 104% penalty rather than a clean 100%.

The penalty applies to the collective’s share, and the reported share is of the layers rather than of the step. A 3x-hot expert triples the all-to-all, and at the cited 30-60% share that is a 1.6x to 2.2x slowdown on the MoE layers — bad, but not 3x. The whole step is worse off by less still, because it also contains attention, the non-MoE FFN, embedding and the optimizer, so the all-to-all’s share of it is lower than its share of the layers and the layer figure is an upper bound. That is two composition steps a team can get wrong in either direction, which is how one ignores a real regression and another chases a phantom. It is also why the fixes are specific: an auxiliary balancing loss (or DeepSeek-V3’s auxiliary-loss-free bias-adjustment scheme) attacks the penalty; a purpose-built dispatch kernel library like DeepEP attacks the share. They are different levers on different factors of the same product.

None of these numbers transfer without re-measuring, and five assumptions are worth naming.

The failure process. The model treats interruptions as homogeneous Poisson at one measured rate, which ignores both burstiness (a bad rack fails repeatedly) and the fact that the Llama 3 rate is one fleet’s hardware, not a law of nature.

What counts as a failure. That rate divides all 466 interruptions by GPU-days, and 47 of the 466 were planned maintenance — events a scheduler can put immediately after a checkpoint instead of eating at random. Recomputed on the 419 unexpected ones alone, the MTBF is 3.09 hours rather than 2.78, and every headline softens: 63.5% → 66.0% for sync with a 20-minute restart, 80.3% → 81.9% for async with one, and the best synchronous cell in the whole grid 88.5% → 89.1%. The conclusion survives — 89.1% is still under 90% — which is why the lesson keeps the harsher rate, but the softer one is graded in the test file rather than left as a hand-wave.

Young’s form is first-order. It charges the checkpoint write against wall clock where a real run pays it once per interval of useful work, and it assumes every failure discards half an interval where a real run has often already lost its progress toward the next write. Both errors point the same way, so it under-predicts: the rig measures 5.1 points above the closed form in the synchronous/20-minute configuration and 0.6 above at the async/2-minute corner, which is why it shows both numbers side by side rather than quietly reconciling them. Daly 2006’s higher-order estimate — sqrt(2 × save × (MTBF + restart)) − save — is a different expression and lands at 38.23 min here rather than Young’s 40.85; the lesson computes Young’s throughout. Past the point where the first-order overheads sum above 100% it clamps at zero and stops predicting anything at all, which the rig labels rather than printing as a number.

The all-to-all share. 30-60% is a reported range for the time to execute the MoE layers of an unoptimised model — and FSMoE cites it to earlier work rather than measuring it. A system with overlapped dispatch kernels already deployed sits below the range, which lowers the layer inflation without touching the imbalance at all; and the share of a whole step is lower again, by however much of the step is attention, dense FFN and optimizer.

The all-to-all cost model. One token, one expert, bf16 both directions. DeepEP’s own benchmark at this hidden size dispatches in FP8 and combines in bf16 (21,504 bytes per token round trip, not 28,672) and routes top-8, so the millisecond column here runs high in one direction and low in the other. It is a derivation for reading the shape off, not a number to quote.

07

In the wild

Meta’s Llama 3 herd paper (arXiv:2407.21783, July 2024) is the source of every failure figure here: 16,384 H100s, 54 days, 466 interruptions (47 planned, 419 unexpected, ~78% of the unexpected ones attributed to confirmed hardware), over 90% effective training time. It is also the clearest published statement of what makes the last number possible — automated detection and recovery, spare capacity, and checkpointing designed around not blocking the step.

PyTorch Distributed Checkpoint’s asynchronous save (torch.distributed.checkpoint.async_save) is the async checkpointing lever as a shipped API: the state dict is staged to host memory, control returns to the training loop, and a background thread completes the durable write. It exists because the synchronous version is exactly the ceiling this lesson computes. Worth knowing before you build on it: the API has been in the DCP reference since PyTorch 2.3 and the docs still carry “this feature is experimental and subject to change” on it, so treat the mechanism as settled and the signature as not.

Elastic and fault-tolerant launchers — torchrun’s elastic mode, Kubernetes-native equivalents — are the fast-restart lever: a pre-provisioned spare pool and a rendezvous protocol that re-forms the process group in seconds rather than requiring a human to notice, allocate a node and relaunch. The 2x2 above is the argument for why both levers get funded rather than one.

DeepEP (DeepSeek, 2025) is a communication library that exists solely for the second half of this lesson: expert-parallel dispatch and combine kernels with NVLink and RDMA paths and computation-communication overlap, published because the all-to-all share of an unoptimised MoE step is large enough to be worth a dedicated library. DeepSeek-V3’s auxiliary-loss-free load balancing is the other lever, attacking the imbalance rather than the share.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Interruptions scale with GPU-days, so mean time between interruptions is inversely proportional to cluster size — the Llama 3 measured rate puts it at 2.78 hours at 16,384 GPUs and 1.39 hours at 32,768, which makes failure the base rate rather than the tail
  • The optimal checkpoint interval is sqrt(2 x saveDuration x MTBF) — Young 1974's first-order result, and a square root, so doubling the save cost only widens the interval 41% and doubling the cluster narrows it 29%. Daly 2006 is the higher-order correction and a different expression, sqrt(2 x save x (MTBF + restart)) − save
  • Effective training time has exactly three sinks that sum with it to one: the blocking part of each checkpoint write, the work discarded on failure (on average half an interval), and the restart itself — the last two paid once per failure, so they share a denominator
  • The best any interval can reach is 1 − sqrt(2 x save / MTBF) − restart / MTBF, and at 16,384 GPUs that ceiling stays under 90% for every plausible synchronous save and restart pair — the best cell in the grid is 88.5%, at a one-minute save AND a one-minute restart
  • Async checkpointing alone and fast restart alone each fail: async with a 20-minute restart tops out at 84.6%, sync with a 1-minute restart at 88.5%. The 2x2 has exactly one cell above 90% and it needs both changes
  • All-reduce moves a fixed payload over fixed edges, so its cost barely depends on GPU count; MoE all-to-all moves a router-decided payload, so its cost is set by the most-loaded expert and no amount of extra fabric fixes a skewed payload

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A team running on 4,096 GPUs hits 87% effective training time and decides the fix is to tune the checkpoint interval, since that is the one number in their config file. Their checkpoint takes 5 minutes to write synchronously and a restart takes 2 minutes. What is the most that interval tuning can buy them, and why?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Nothing — they are already at the ceiling. At 4,096 GPUs the measured Llama 3 rate gives an MTBF of 11.12 hours, the Young interval is 82 minutes, and the best effective training time reachable at ANY interval is 87.5%. The interval is a genuine optimum, which is exactly why it is the wrong dial: it sits at the bottom of a bowl, so moving it in either direction makes things worse, and the bottom of the bowl is 87.5%. The mechanism is that the interval trades one overhead for another — shorter intervals spend more wall clock writing checkpoints, longer ones discard more work per failure — and the sum of the two at the optimum is sqrt(2 x save / MTBF), which depends only on the save duration and the MTBF, not on the interval at all. To move the ceiling they have to change one of those two: make the write non-blocking (async checkpointing, which bills roughly a tenth of the write) or shrink the restart. On this lesson's own numbers, async at 4,096 GPUs with the same 2-minute restart reaches 95.8%.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Async checkpointing removes almost all of the blocking write cost. Why is that on its own not enough to reach 90% at 16,384 GPUs, and what does the second fix have to be?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the three sinks are independent, and async only drains one of them. At 16,384 GPUs with a 5-minute save, async cuts the checkpoint overhead from 12.2% to 3.9% — but a 20-minute restart is a fixed toll paid every 2.78 hours, which is 12.0% of wall clock all by itself, and the discarded-work term adds more on top. The measured ceiling for async with a 20-minute restart is 80.3%, and even shrinking the save to one minute only gets it to 84.6%, because at that point the restart IS the whole overhead. The second fix has to attack restart directly: pre-provisioned warm spares so no node has to be allocated, a pre-formed or fast-rebuilding process group so the rendezvous is not a minutes-long barrier, and detection fast enough that a hung collective does not sit for ten minutes before anyone notices. At a 2-minute restart, async reaches 91.1% — the only cell in the 2x2 above the reported figure.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A team moves from a dense model to a mixture-of-experts model at the same parameter count and the same cluster, and the step time gets worse rather than better even though far fewer parameters are active per token. Their interconnect utilisation graph shows plenty of headroom. What is the mechanism, and why does the headroom graph hide it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The all-to-all token dispatch is paced by whichever expert the router overloaded, not by the average. With 8,192 tokens over 16 expert ranks a balanced router puts 512 tokens on each; on this lesson's own Zipf skew at exponent 0.4 the hottest rank gets 1,047, so the collective takes 0.65 ms instead of 0.32 ms — a 104% imbalance penalty — and every other rank spends the difference stalled at a barrier. Because token dispatch is a reported 30-60% of the time to execute an unoptimised MoE layer, that lands as a 1.31x to 1.63x slowdown on the layers, and on the whole step — which also contains attention, the dense FFN and the optimizer — by no more than that. The step figure needs the step-level share, which is a thing you have to profile rather than cite. The headroom graph hides it for the same reason an average hides a convoy: the fabric genuinely is mostly idle, because 15 of 16 ranks finished early and are waiting. Aggregate utilisation is exactly the statistic that cannot see a maximum, and this cost is a maximum. That is also why it is structurally different from all-reduce, whose payload is fixed by the model rather than decided per microbatch by a router.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    A data pipeline runs a 30-hour batch job across 900 spot VMs and checkpoints intermediate state to object storage every 20 minutes. Preemptions arrive roughly every 40 minutes. The team's proposal is to checkpoint every 5 minutes instead. Will that get the job's throughput close to what an un-preempted run would achieve, and what would you ask them for before agreeing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No, and the reason is the same square-root bowl. Checkpointing four times as often cuts the discarded-work term by four but multiplies the checkpoint-write term by four, and the sum at the optimum — sqrt(2 x writeDuration / MTBF) — never falls below what the write duration and the preemption rate jointly allow. Before agreeing, ask for two numbers they almost certainly have not measured: how long a checkpoint write actually blocks the job, and how long it takes from preemption to the replacement worker being productive again. Those are the only two levers on the ceiling. If the write blocks for 2 minutes and the restart costs 10 minutes, no interval helps; the fixes are to make the write asynchronous (stage to local disk, upload in the background) and to keep warm replacement capacity so the restart is seconds. The interval is worth setting correctly once and then never touching again.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system that loses uncommitted work on interruption has a throughput ceiling set by two independent quantities — how much the act of committing costs, and how long recovery takes — and the commit FREQUENCY is a genuine optimum, meaning it is the one dial that cannot raise the ceiling however carefully it is tuned. Name an instance outside GPU training.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A surgical operating theatre running a long procedure with periodic hand-off notes. Writing a hand-off note stops the procedure (the commit cost); an unplanned interruption forces the incoming team to redo everything since the last note (the discarded work) plus the time to scrub in and get oriented (the recovery cost). Writing notes more often is a real optimum and it caps out: the two ways to actually raise throughput are to make note-taking non-blocking (a scribe, so the surgeon never stops) and to make hand-off fast (a second team already gowned and briefed). Other instances: a long-running database migration with a commit-batch-size dial, where the levers are non-blocking commits and how fast a failed worker is replaced, not the batch size; a CI pipeline on preemptible runners, where caching intermediate build artefacts is the commit and warm runner pools are the recovery; and a writer working on a laptop with a failing battery, where autosave frequency is the optimum, background autosave is the async fix, and a charged second laptop is the fast restart.
    Did you have that?

Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production.
