Tier 10 · AI-era systems Continuous batching explained well nowhere else

Doubling a static batch's size makes its GPU utilisation worse, not better

llm-inference-phases already fixed the cost of one decode step: it reads every weight in the model once, for the whole batch, regardless of how many of that batch's slots are still generating anything. This lesson is about who gets to hold those slots, and for how long, when replies finish at wildly different lengths — a scheduling decision, not a memory or arithmetic one. It leans on kv-cache's distinction between a contiguous reserve-to-maximum allocation and paged, per-slot memory without re-deriving either, and treats gpu-memory-arithmetic's account of what a card's memory leaves for concurrent sequences as already settled.

Read first — kv-cachegpu-memory-arithmetic

01

The batch was full the whole time, and four-fifths of it produced nothing

A serving engine batches eight chat requests onto one GPU. Once they’ve actually run, their reply lengths turn out to be 40, 120, 80, 900, 60, 30, 150 and 100 tokens — seven ordinary replies and one nine-hundred-token essay a user asked for by accident.

None of this is known at admission time. The engine sees eight requests and one GPU, reserves eight slots, and runs decode steps until the batch is done. A step’s cost does not care how many of the eight slots still have something to say — llm-inference-phases already established that one decode step reads every weight in the model once, for the whole batch, whether that batch is producing eight tokens or one. So the essay in slot 3 keeps all eight slots alive for 900 steps, while five of the other seven finish inside the first 150 and then sit in their reservation, occupied, producing nothing, for however long is left.

Add up what was actually useful: 40+120+80+900+60+30+150+100 = 1,480 tokens of real generation, out of 8 × 900 = 7,200 token-steps the GPU spent computing. 20.6% of this batch’s decode work produced a token a user saw. The rest was a full-width step, paid for in full, computed for slots that had nothing left to say.

This is not a pathological batch — it is what happens whenever reply lengths vary and the scheduler’s unit of work is “the batch” rather than “the slot.” Run it again with a different draw of eight lengths and the number moves. It does not move toward 100%, and by the end of this lesson the number it settles on, averaged over many such batches, turns out to depend on exactly one thing: how many slots there are.

02

The wrong belief: a bigger batch is just a better batch

The belief this has to break
A batch of N requests does N times the useful work of one request, so a bigger batch is strictly a better batch.

llm-inference-phases’s free lunch is real: batching more sequences onto one decode step is free right up to the point compute saturates, because the weight read is shared across however many slots are occupied. The belief that fact builds is natural, and it stops being true the moment replies finish at different times: a bigger batch is only a better batch if every slot’s occupant finishes on schedule, and in a static batch none of them do independently.

A static batch is one tensor, admitted together and retired together. It runs for exactly as many steps as its longest member needs, and every one of those steps pays the full cost of a full-width step regardless of how many of the batch’s slots are still generating. The batch is the unit of scheduling, so the batch’s slowest member is everyone’s problem.

The fix does not touch the arithmetic of one decode step at all — llm-inference-phases’s cost model for a step is untouched by anything in this lesson. It changes the unit that gets scheduled, from “a batch” to “a slot, at every step.” The instant a slot’s sequence hits its stop condition, the scheduler admits whatever is next in the queue into that exact slot on the very next step — not at the end of the batch, because there is no longer a “the batch” to end, only slots and a queue.

Static batching is bounded by the longest sequence it contains. Continuous batching is bounded by the slowest single step — and a step’s cost does not depend on which sequences occupy which slots.

03

The intuition: a chairlift, and a shuttle bus that won't leave until everyone's off

A ski resort runs two kinds of transport up the same slope.

The shuttle bus seats eight. It leaves the base once all eight seats are full, and it does not return to the base until every passenger currently aboard has reached their own stop — even though five of them get off at the first stop and a sixth two stops later. The bus keeps driving its full loaded weight for as long as the slowest passenger’s ride takes, because it is one vehicle with one departure and one return, and the empty seats on the way back are not a second trip. They’re the same trip, continuing to happen, mostly empty.

The chairlift does something structurally different. Every chair is independent. The instant a chair reaches the top and its rider steps off, that exact chair is already on its way back down, empty, and there is another skier waiting at the bottom to take it on its very next pass. No chair is ever deliberately parked at the top waiting for “the others,” because the lift has no others to wait for — there is no batch of chairs, only chairs.

The bus is static batching: one admission, one shared departure, and a single straggler determines how long every seat stays occupied. The chairlift is continuous batching: admission happens per chair, at the moment a chair becomes free, and chair 4’s schedule has nothing to do with what chair 7 is doing. Nobody made the lift smarter about skiers. They stopped scheduling the whole lift as one unit.

The mechanism in one line: static batching schedules a group and pays for its slowest member. Continuous batching schedules a slot and pays only for its own occupant, one step at a time.

04

The mechanism: an order-statistics law, and the allocator it actually needs

Model each reply’s length as an independent draw from the same distribution with mean μ. A static batch of B slots finishes when its longest member does, so:

static utilisation = mean(length) ÷ E[max of B lengths] Useful work over the whole run, divided by the run’s actual length — a ratio that says nothing about which allocator is underneath it, only about the schedule.

For the useful special case of i.i.d. Exponential(μ) lengths — the same memoryless assumption this repo’s queueing lessons already use as their default service-time model — this has a genuinely closed form, and it is worth deriving rather than quoting. Memorylessness does the work: among B independent Exponential(μ) draws, the first to finish arrives after an expected μ/B, and because the exponential distribution forgets how long it has already waited, the remaining B−1 draws are still exactly Exponential(μ) from that instant — so the wait for the next one to finish is Exponential(μ/(B−1)), and so on down to a final Exponential(μ) wait between the last two. The expected maximum is the sum of these B shrinking waits:

E[max of B i.i.d. Exponential(μ)] = μ · (1/B + 1/(B−1) + … + 1/1) = μ · HB HB is the B-th harmonic number. Substitute into the ratio above and μ cancels completely: static utilisation (exponential lengths) = 1 / HB — a function of batch size and nothing else. Asserted against the exact fractions H4 = 25/12 and H8 = 761/280 in continuous-batching.test.mjs.

HB grows without bound as B grows — slowly, like ln B, but without bound — so 1/HB falls as the batch gets bigger. A batch of 2 loses a third of its steps to the straggler problem. A batch of 64 loses close to four-fifths. Handing static batching more slots does not dilute the straggler problem, it makes having one more likely: more independent draws means more chances for one of them to be the unlucky long one, and the whole batch waits on whichever one it is.

Continuous batching sidesteps the derivation entirely, not by being cleverer about scheduling but by changing what has to be true for a slot to be occupied. At every step, a slot with nothing left is refilled from the queue, and E[max] never enters the calculation — there is no “the batch’s longest member” to wait for, only “whichever slot most recently emptied.”

What this actually requires from memory management is narrower than it sounds, and narrower than kv-cache.mdx’s full paged-block scheme. All continuous batching needs is that one sequence’s memory can be freed and handed to a different sequence independently of its neighbours in the batch — a plain contiguous reservation already permits that, as long as each slot’s reservation belongs to one request rather than to the whole batch. Orca, the paper that introduced this scheduling change, did exactly that: a contiguous block sized to each request’s own declared maximum, freed when that request finishes and handed straight to the next one waiting — no paging at all, and still wasting memory by kv-cache.mdx’s own arithmetic (1 − mean/max, charged per slot instead of per batch). kv-cache.mdx’s paged blocks are a separate, later fix to that waste. The two compose — production engines ship both — but neither is a precondition for the other: this lesson’s scheduling change and kv-cache’s allocator change attack two different wastes of the same memory budget that gpu-memory-arithmetic sizes in the first place.

05

The numbers: a batch that gets worse the more slots you give it

Predict Static batching gets 66.7% GPU utilisation at batch size 2. A team doubles the batch to 4, expecting throughput and utilisation to both improve. What happens to utilisation?
It falls to exactly 48% — 1/H₄ = 12/25, because H₄ = 25/12. More slots means more independent draws, and more draws means the expected longest one among them grows — so static utilisation's ceiling falls monotonically with batch size, for any reply-length distribution with real variance. 06

The trade-off: packed tensors, an interleaved step, and a queue that has to stay full

A ragged batch needs a kernel that can be ragged. Once slots hold sequences at different positions in their own generation, a stock attention kernel that expects one clean rectangular tensor no longer applies — Orca’s answer was “selective batching,” applying ordinary batched matrix multiplication to the operations that don’t care about sequence boundaries and handling attention per-sequence for the one operation that does. NVIDIA’s TensorRT-LLM documentation describes the same requirement from the implementation side: in-flight batching needs its input tensors packed, with no padding, and sequences in the prefill phase placed before sequences in the generation phase within that packed tensor. This is not a scheduler bolted onto an existing engine; it is a different shape for the hottest kernel in the system, the same character of cost kv-cache.mdx pays for paged attention’s block-table indirection.

Admitting a new request can spike everyone else’s step. A newly-joined sequence’s first bit of work is prefill — compute-bound, proportional to its whole prompt length — landing in the very same step as everyone else’s decode, which is memory-bound and nearly flat regardless of anyone’s prompt length. A long prompt joining the batch can turn a normally-fast decode step into a slow, compute-heavy one for every sequence sharing it. Production schedulers answer this by chunking a new prompt’s prefill across several steps instead of admitting it whole, or by moving prefill to a separate pool of GPUs entirely — a deployment decision downstream of this lesson that this tier covers on its own rather than re-deriving here.

The near-100% ceiling is a steady-state property of a full queue, not of the algorithm alone. A continuous scheduler that runs out of queued work mid-run ends exactly like a static batch does: whichever slots are still active finish independently while the rest of the card sits idle, because there is nothing left to hand them. This is the same effect the rig’s rounds slider makes visible at low settings, and it is why a production system is engineered to keep demand ahead of capacity rather than to let the queue drain — the guarantee is conditional, not absolute.

None of this makes the underlying decode step any faster. Continuous batching, like batching itself, is a way of not wasting a resource that is already there — it cannot lower a decode step’s cost, only raise the fraction of that cost that produces a token somebody sees. The ceiling on the step itself is llm-inference-phases’s roofline, untouched.

07

In the wild

Orca, the paper this scheduling change comes from. Yu, Jeong, Kim, Kim & Chun, “Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models,” OSDI 2022, names the technique iteration-level scheduling — invoking the execution engine for a single iteration of the model at a time rather than for a whole batch’s full run — paired with selective batching to apply ordinary batched matrix multiplication to the operations that tolerate it and handle attention per-sequence for the one that doesn’t. Their own evaluation reports 36.9× the throughput at the same latency against NVIDIA FasterTransformer, serving a 175B-parameter model.

“Continuous batching,” the name that stuck. Daniel, Shen, Liang & Liaw, “How Continuous Batching Enables 23x Throughput in LLM Inference While Reducing p50 Latency,” Anyscale, June 2023, popularised today’s usual name for Orca’s technique and benchmarked it against the naive static-batch alternative across several open-source serving stacks, including an early HuggingFace text-generation-inference and vLLM — the 23× in the title is that benchmark’s own best case, not a universal constant, and it is the same shape of result this lesson derives from first principles: the gap between a scheduler that waits for a group and one that doesn’t grows with how much a workload’s reply lengths vary.

“In-flight batching,” the other name for it. NVIDIA’s TensorRT-LLM documents the identical mechanism under this name, explicit that it is also known as continuous batching or iteration-level batching, and specific about the implementation cost noted in slot 6: packed, unpadded input tensors, with context-phase (prefill) sequences ordered ahead of generation-phase (decode) sequences in the same packed step.

vLLM and Hugging Face TGI ship it as the default, not an option. kv-cache.mdx already covers vLLM’s other headline contribution, PagedAttention, in detail; the two features shipped together in the same system because they attack two different wastes of the same GPU memory, exactly as slot 4 distinguishes them, and most engineers who have used “vLLM’s speedup” as a single number are really describing the product of both.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Static utilisation is mean(length) ÷ E[max length in the batch]; continuous utilisation is ≈1 given a non-empty queue, and neither formula mentions the mean directly
  • For i.i.d. Exponential(μ) lengths the mean cancels: static utilisation is exactly 1/H_B, the reciprocal of the B-th harmonic number — batch size and nothing else
  • Because E[max] grows with batch size, a bigger static batch has a LOWER utilisation ceiling — more slots means a straggler is more likely, not less
  • Continuous batching needs a slot's memory reassignable independently of its neighbours; Orca's own system did this with plain per-request contiguous reservations, no paging
  • A newly-admitted request's prefill lands in the same step as everyone else's decode, which is why production schedulers pack the step or push prefill to a separate pool
  • Continuous batching's near-100% utilisation is a steady-state property of a non-empty queue; a short burst still ends in a static-like drain tail

    A continuous-batching server's queue briefly empties during a traffic dip. What happens to GPU utilisation at that moment, and why doesn't it contradict the "≈100%" claim made earlier?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It falls, and the fall has the exact same shape as static batching's problem, just at a smaller scale: whichever slots are still running when the queue empties have nothing left to refill them with, so they finish independently while the rest of the card is already idle — a miniature version of "wait for the longest member," except the group waiting is whatever happened to still be in flight rather than a whole declared batch. The "≈100%" claim was always a steady-state statement, conditional on a queue that stays non-empty; it is not a property of the scheduling algorithm in isolation, which is why production systems care so much about keeping utilisation-driving demand ahead of service capacity rather than draining to zero between bursts.

  6. Near transfer

    A ride-pooling dispatcher groups every four waiting riders into one car and the car does not return to the depot until all four have been dropped off, even though most trips are short and one every so often crosses the whole city. A ride-hailing dispatcher instead sends whichever car just dropped its last passenger straight to the next rider waiting. Which policy is analogous to which batching scheme, and what single number predicts the pooling dispatcher's fleet utilisation?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The grouped dispatcher is static batching — one shared departure and return, bounded by whichever of the four riders has the longest trip. The immediate-reassignment dispatcher is continuous batching — a car is never deliberately parked waiting for others, because there is no "others" to wait for once assignment happens per car rather than per group of four. The same formula predicts the pooling dispatcher's utilisation as predicts a static LLM batch: mean(trip length) ÷ E[max of 4 trip lengths] — and if trip lengths are roughly memoryless (a reasonable approximation for city trips with no fixed size), that ratio is exactly 1/H4 = 48%, the same number a batch of 4 gets. Grouping more riders per car to "improve efficiency" makes it worse for the identical reason: more riders means a longer outlier trip is more likely to be in the group, and the whole car waits on it.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a shared resource scheduled at the granularity of a GROUP is bounded by the group's slowest member; the same resource scheduled at the granularity of the INDIVIDUAL is bounded only by the resource's own rate. Name instances outside LLM serving.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A fork-join barrier in parallel computing — barriers-and-semaphores's own topic — is exactly this: a barrier releases only once every one of N threads has arrived, so its wall-clock cost is E[max of N thread-completion times], and that expectation grows with N by the identical order-statistics mechanism, which is the standard argument for why fine-grained parallel work with a shared barrier scales worse than the per-thread speedup alone would suggest. Batch manufacturing versus one-piece flow in lean production: a batch of parts moves to the next station only once every part in the batch has cleared the current one, so a single defective or slow part holds the entire batch hostage, which is the textbook argument for small batch sizes and single-piece flow. Early batch-processing operating systems, where a job queue ran one submitted deck of cards to completion before starting the next, versus a preemptive time-sharing scheduler that reassigns the CPU at a much finer grain without ever waiting for "the job" to finish. A restaurant table for eight that isn't cleared until the last diner finishes their coffee, against counter service that reseats each stool the instant its occupant leaves. In every instance the fix is the same: stop scheduling the group as the unit, and the group's slowest member stops being everyone else's problem.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
