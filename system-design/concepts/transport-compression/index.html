Tier 1 · The network Transport compression explained well nowhere else

Brotli at maximum level makes a response arrive later on any link faster than 5.6 Mbps

Compressing a response costs time to save time, so there is a bandwidth above which it is a straight loss — and it is `(1 − 1/ratio)` over the harmonic sum of compress and decompress throughput. That puts brotli-11 at 5.6 Mbps, gzip-6 at 180, and zstd-1 at 1.8 Gbps. Compressibility barely enters it: the saved fraction varies by 1.2× across every codec here while their throughput varies by 522×. And the level you should pick is not a fact about your content — it is a fact about whether you can cache the result.

01

The compression change that made the API slower and the website faster

A team turns on brotli at level 11 across their CDN. The commit message says reduce bandwidth costs, and it does. Two weeks later:

  • Page loads got faster. Median improved, and the bandwidth bill dropped.
  • API latency got worse. Median response time on their JSON endpoints went up, noticeably, and it is the p50 that moved rather than the tail.

Nothing else changed. Same servers, same network, same code, one config flag — and it moved two numbers in opposite directions.

Here is the API half, in numbers. A 200 kB JSON response over a 100 Mbps link:

time
sent uncompressed16 ms
brotli-11, compressed on the fly226 ms

Fourteen times worse. Not a subtle regression, not a tail effect, not contention — a deterministic 210 ms added to every single response, in exchange for making it smaller. And the website half of the same flag was a genuine improvement. The flag is not wrong; it is being applied to two situations that have opposite right answers, and nothing in the words enable compression distinguishes them.

02

Assume better compression is better, and find where each codec turns against you

The instinct is that compression levels form a quality dial: higher is slower and smaller, and you pick a point on it according to how much CPU you can spare. That framing has the axes right and the conclusion wrong, because it treats the CPU cost as a budget when it is on the critical path of the response. The bytes you removed were going to take time to send; removing them took time too. So there is a bandwidth at which the trade reverses, and it is different for every codec.

Start the rig at gzip −6 and drag bandwidth up. Its line crosses the dashed no compression line at 180 Mbps — above that, gzip-6 is a loss. Now switch to brotli −11: the crossing is not on the chart, because it happened at 5.6 Mbps, before the axis begins. And switch to zstd −1, which keeps paying up to 1.8 Gbps.

Predict brotli at level 11 compresses text about 4.6× and runs at roughly 0.9 MB/s. Above what link bandwidth does compressing a dynamic response with it make that response arrive later than sending it raw?
Compressing at c to ratio r and decompressing at d wins iff 1/c + 1/(b·r) + 1/d < 1/b, i.e. b < (1 − 1/r)/(1/c + 1/d). At 0.9 MB/s and 4.6×, that is 5.6 Mbps — so on any real link brotli-11 on the fly is a straight loss. Option 4 is the other error worth naming: (1 − 1/r) is bounded in [0.65, 0.8] across every codec in use, a 1.2× span, while their throughput spans 522×. The codec decides this, not the content.
Show it without answering

The second control is the one that resolves slot 1. Press pre-compressed and the compression term goes to zero — because a cacheable asset is compressed once and served thousands of times. Every crossing disappears, and brotli-11 becomes the best choice at every bandwidth on the chart. The two halves of the team’s regression are the same inequality read in the two regimes.

03

Flat-packing a wardrobe

You are moving a wardrobe.

Taking it apart makes it much easier to move, and it costs an hour at each end — an hour to disassemble, an hour to put back together. Carrying it whole across one room takes two minutes.

So whether to flat-pack depends on how far it is going, and there is a distance below which taking it apart is a straight loss. That distance is not a fact about the wardrobe. It is a fact about how fast you can move the assembled thing: down a corridor, never bother; onto a lorry going three hundred miles, always.

And notice who reaches the opposite conclusion. The manufacturer flat-packs once, in a factory, for a million units — at which point the hour of preparation costs nothing per unit and the only question left is how small it goes. Same object, same physics, and IKEA and your Saturday afternoon are both right.

That is the whole lesson. The level is a question about whether the preparation can be reused, and it is routinely mistaken for a question about the object.

04

The mechanism — one inequality, and the term everybody drops

Sending S bytes at ratio r over bandwidth b, compressing at c and decompressing at d, costs

S/c  +  S/(b·r)  +  S/d          against          S/b

Divide through by S and rearrange. Compressing wins iff

1/c + 1/(b·r) + 1/d  <  1/b      ⟺      b  <  (1 − 1/r) / (1/c + 1/d)

That is the whole lesson, and two things follow from the shape of it.

Compressibility barely enters. (1 − 1/r) is a bounded factor: 0.67 at a ratio of 3, 0.90 at a ratio of 10. Across every codec in common use it spans 1.2× — while their compression throughput spans 522×. So “our JSON compresses beautifully” moves the answer by a few percent and the codec choice moves it by three hundred times. This is the opposite of how the decision is usually discussed.

The 1/d term is the one everyone drops, and dropping it gives the form the inequality is usually quoted in: b < c·(1 − 1/r). That overstates the break-even by exactly 1 + c/d, and how much that matters depends on the codec in a way the short form cannot express:

codecnaive break-evenreal break-evenoverstated by
brotli −115.6 Mbps5.6 Mbps1.002×
gzip −6197.6 Mbps179.7 Mbps1.100×
gzip −1629.0 Mbps468.4 Mbps1.343×
zstd −12,585 Mbps1,811 Mbps1.427×

The pattern is not random: a compressor slow enough that decompression is negligible is a compressor whose break-even is too low to be useful anyway. Exactly the codecs worth considering are the ones where the short form is wrong — and the term it drops carries a conclusion, because fast decompression is what lets zstd keep paying further up the bandwidth ladder than gzip.

(This lesson’s model got that wrong first. breakEven was written in the popular form, and a numeric oracle that bisected the model’s own time function disagreed by 10% for gzip-6 and 43% for zstd-1. The algebra was what was wrong, and naiveBreakEvenMbps is kept in the model so the error stays visible.)

Pre-compression removes the first term entirely, which is why it changes the answer rather than improving it. With 1/c gone, the inequality can never reverse — compressing is free at request time, so more ratio is strictly better. Almost:

And brotli-11’s on-the-fly column is a catastrophe at every row, 226 ms against a 4.0 ms cached figure for the same codec on the same link: a factor of 56.6, and all of it is one question about caching.

There is also a wrinkle worth having, because it stops the rule from being “cached means maximum level”. Above about 100 Mbps the best pre-compressed codec is zstd-19 at ratio 4.3, not brotli-11 at 4.6 — because zstd decompresses at 1,100 MB/s against brotli’s 400, and past that bandwidth the client’s decompression matters more than the last 7% of ratio. Even in the cached regime it is a trade; it is just a much gentler one.

Small bodies get bigger, and framing is why. The smallest response each codec actually shrinks:

codecframinguseless below
brotli2 B31 B
zstd13 B80–97 B
gzip18 B111 B

Nothing to do with ratio — a 3.6× codec beats a 3.5× one by 3.6× on this axis, purely on container overhead. At 200 bytes gzip-6 achieves only 1.20× against its 3.4× asymptote, because the dictionary has nowhere to build; it reaches 2.0× at 1 kB and 2.9× at 5 kB.

And an already-compressed share collapses the whole thing. A compressor reads every byte and shrinks only the compressible ones, which is Amdahl’s Law with media as the serial fraction. gzip-6 on a 1 MB payload at 100 Mbps:

06

The trade-off — the fast codec is worse at what compression is for, and caching moves a cost rather than removing it

Everything above reads as use zstd for dynamic and brotli-11 for static, done. Each half has a real price.

The fast codec is worse at the thing you turned compression on for. zstd-1 keeps paying to 1.8 Gbps and compresses 3.2× where brotli-11 gets 4.6×. If your constraint is the bandwidth bill rather than latency — egress is metered, and egress-costs prices it — then the codec that wins on time loses on money, by 30% of every byte. The two objectives genuinely disagree and the arithmetic here answers only one of them. A CDN charging for egress and a mobile client on a metered plan both want the slow codec.

Pre-compression moves the cost to build and storage, and to a cache you now have to be right about. The compression still happens; it happens at build time, and it happens for every variant. Compress a bundle in gzip and brotli and zstd and you have three artefacts, three cache entries, and a Vary: Accept-Encoding on every response — which multiplies the cache key space, exactly the effect http-caching-semantics measures. And a pre-compressed artefact is only free if it is hit: at a low hit rate you are paying build-time compression and origin fetches, and when-a-cache-stops-paying is the same accounting one layer up.

Decompression is not free at the client, and it is the term you cannot measure. Every figure above uses a server-class decompression rate. A cheap phone does not have one — and the client is where decompression happens, on the same core as the parse and the render. On the device population client-perceived-latency describes, the 1/d term is the largest one in the inequality and the break-even is lower than any of these tables suggest. The honest statement is that this lesson’s ladder is computed with the client you wish you had.

And the largest trade is that this optimisation competes for attention with a bigger one. Every number here is a multiplier on a payload you chose to send. byte-budgets and pagination are about not sending it, and 3.4× off a response that should have been a tenth the size is the wrong argument to be having. Compression is the last thing to tune, not the first — it just happens to be a config flag, which is why it gets tuned first.

07

In the wild

Brotli was published with a table that says all of this, and it was read as a ratio table. Alakuijala, Farruggia, Ferragina, Kliuchnikov, Obryk, Szabadka and Vandevenne, Brotli: A General-Purpose Data Compressor (ACM Transactions on Information Systems 37(1), 2019) reports compression speed alongside ratio across all eleven levels, and the speed column drops by more than two orders of magnitude from the fast levels to level 11. Everything in this lesson is derivable from that table plus one inequality. What the industry took from the paper was the ratio.

The CDNs split their policy exactly along the cacheable boundary, and say so. Cloudflare, Fastly and Akamai all document compressing cacheable assets at high effort while using a low level or none for pass-through responses — and Cloudflare’s Zstandard support was introduced with throughput, not ratio, as the argument. The configuration surface most of them expose is per content type, which is a proxy for cacheability and mostly a good one; the case it gets wrong is exactly the one in slot 1, a JSON endpoint whose Content-Type looks static and whose responses are not.

Google’s Zopfli exists because of the asymmetric case, and is honest about it. Zopfli produces DEFLATE-compatible output about 5% smaller than gzip-9 and takes roughly 80× longer to do it. It was published explicitly for content compressed once and served many times, and its documentation says not to use it on the fly. It is the clearest available statement of the rule: the same output format, an absurd compression cost, and a correct engineering decision — because 1/c is divided by the number of hits.

The small-body effect is why HTTP/2 needed HPACK rather than gzip. Header blocks are a few hundred bytes, which is inside the range where a general-purpose compressor’s dictionary has nowhere to build and its framing is a material fraction of the output. http2-multiplexing covers what HPACK does instead — and what it costs, which is a cross-stream ordering coupling that a per-message compressor would not have had.

  • Jyrki Alakuijala and Zoltan Szabadka, RFC 7932: Brotli Compressed Data Format, IETF, 2016.
  • Yann Collet and Murray Kucherawy (eds.), RFC 8878: Zstandard Compression and the application/zstd Media Type, IETF, 2021 — and the reference implementation’s published compression/decompression speed table, which is where the asymmetry between the two directions is visible.
  • Peter Deutsch, RFC 1951: DEFLATE Compressed Data Format Specification and RFC 1952: GZIP File Format Specification, IETF, 1996 — the 10-byte header and 8-byte trailer that decide the small-body threshold.
  • Lode Vandevenne and Jyrki Alakuijala, Zopfli Compression Algorithm, Google, 2013 — DEFLATE-compatible output at roughly 80× the compression cost, published for the compress-once case and documented as unsuitable for on-the-fly use.
  • Roy Fielding and Julian Reschke (eds.), RFC 7231: HTTP/1.1 Semantics and Content, IETF, 2014, §3.1.2 and §5.3.4 — Content-Encoding and Accept-Encoding, and the negotiation that makes Vary necessary.
  • Ilya Grigorik, High Performance Browser Networking, O’Reilly 2013 — the chapter on transfer encoding, and the argument for measuring the client’s decompression cost rather than assuming it.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Compressing on the fly beats sending raw only while bandwidth is below (1 − 1/ratio) divided by the harmonic sum of compress and decompress throughput — b < (1 − 1/r)/(1/c + 1/d)
  • The popular form of that inequality, b < c(1 − 1/r), omits decompression and overstates the break-even by exactly 1 + c/d — negligible for brotli-11 at 1.002× and wrong by 1.43× for zstd-1
  • Compressibility barely decides it: (1 − 1/r) is bounded in [0.65, 0.8] across every codec in common use, a 1.2× span, while their compression throughput spans 522×
  • brotli-11 breaks even at 5.6 Mbps, so on any deployed link it makes a dynamic response arrive later — at 200 kB over 100 Mbps it takes 226 ms against 16 ms uncompressed
  • Pre-compressing sends the compression term to zero, which is why the level is a question about whether the artefact is cacheable and not about the content — brotli-11 goes from 226 ms to 4.0 ms, a factor of 56.6
  • Even pre-compressed the highest ratio does not always win: above about 100 Mbps zstd-19 beats brotli-11 despite a lower ratio, because it decompresses at 1,100 MB/s against 400
  • Framing and an unbuilt dictionary make small bodies larger — gzip is useless below 111 bytes and brotli below 31, and the difference is framing (18 bytes against 2), not ratio

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Your CDN offers brotli at maximum level for dynamic API responses. Is that a good idea?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it makes them arrive later. Compressing at throughput c to ratio r and decompressing at d beats sending raw only while b < (1 − 1/r)/(1/c + 1/d), and brotli-11 runs at roughly 0.9 MB/s, so its break-even is 5.6 Mbps — below any link you would deploy behind. Concretely, a 200 kB response over 100 Mbps takes 226 ms with brotli-11 on the fly against 16 ms sent raw. That is not a tuning subtlety, it is the wrong side of an inequality by a factor of eighteen. For a dynamic response the question is which codec is fast enough: at 100 Mbps that is zstd-3 (5.6 ms), and at 1 Gbps zstd-1 (1.1 ms).
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    So brotli-11 is a bad codec?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is the best codec — for anything you can compress once. Pre-compressing sends the 1/c term to zero, and the same 200 kB response goes from 226 ms to 4.0 ms, a factor of 56.6. That is the actual rule, and it is not about content at all: if the result can be stored and reused, use the slowest and best compressor available; if it cannot, use the fastest one that still compresses. Teams argue about levels when the question was whether the artefact is static. A JS bundle, a stylesheet, a font subset, a versioned JSON blob — all pre-compressible, all should be at maximum. A search result should not be compressed at maximum on any link.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Which matters more for the decision: how compressible your content is, or which codec you picked?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The codec, by a very long way, and the arithmetic says why. The break-even is (1 − 1/r)/(1/c + 1/d), and (1 − 1/r) is a bounded factor: at r = 3 it is 0.67 and at r = 10 it is 0.90, so across every codec in common use it spans just 1.2×. Compression throughput across the same list spans 522×. So "our JSON compresses really well" moves the answer by a few percent and "we're using brotli-11 instead of zstd-1" moves it by three hundred times. The one case where compressibility does decide is when it collapses — see the already-compressed-share question below, where the effective ratio approaching 1 takes (1 − 1/r) to zero with it.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You measure your compressor and find it hits 35 MB/s at a 3.4× ratio. You compute the break-even as 35 × (1 − 1/3.4) = 198 Mbps. What is wrong with that?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It omits decompression, which the client also has to do before the response is usable. The correct denominator is the harmonic sum 1/c + 1/d, so the answer is 180 Mbps, not 198 — the popular form overstates by exactly 1 + c/d. For gzip-6 that is a 10% error and easy to shrug off. For zstd-1 it is 1.43× — 2,585 Mbps claimed against 1,811 real — because a compressor fast enough to be interesting is fast enough that decompression is no longer a rounding error. It also hides a conclusion: fast decompression is exactly what lets zstd keep paying further up the bandwidth ladder than gzip, and the naive form cannot express that.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Your gateway compresses every response. Half your traffic is JPEG thumbnails. What is that costing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    CPU with nothing bought, and it drags down the decision for everything else in the same response. The compressor reads every byte but only shrinks the compressible ones, so the effective ratio is Amdahl's Law with the incompressible share playing the serial fraction: at a 60% already-compressed share, gzip-6's effective ratio falls from 3.4× to 1.39× and its break-even collapses from 180 Mbps to 72. At a 100 Mbps link that flips it from worth doing to a straight loss — a 1 MB payload goes from 52 ms to 86 ms against 80 ms raw. This is the whole reason servers ship a Content-Type allow-list rather than compressing everything, and the reason the list is a correctness feature rather than a micro-optimisation.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team enables brotli at level 11 across their CDN for everything, and the change ships as "reduce bandwidth costs". Their p50 page load improves and their p50 API latency gets worse. Explain both halves, and say what you would change.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Both halves are the same inequality read in the two regimes, and the split between them is exactly the split between what the CDN can cache and what it cannot.

    The page load improved because static assets are pre-compressed. A CDN compresses a cacheable asset once and serves the stored result, so the 1/c term is paid on one request out of thousands and is not on the critical path of any of them. In that regime the only thing that matters is ratio, brotli-11 has the best one in common use, and the improvement is real and permanent. Nothing about this half is a mistake.

    The API latency got worse because a dynamic response is compressed on every request. There the 1/c term is on the critical path, brotli-11 runs at about 0.9 MB/s, and its break-even is 5.6 Mbps — so on any real link it is a loss. A 200 kB JSON response over 100 Mbps: 226 ms compressed against 16 ms raw. Note which percentile moved: the p50, not the tail, because this is a *deterministic* cost added to every response rather than a tail event. A change that moves the median is usually not a capacity problem.

    Also check what fraction of the "bandwidth saving" was real. If the CDN is compressing images too, that share is pure CPU: the compressor reads the bytes and returns them the same size. At a 60% media share the effective ratio is 1.39×, not 4.6×, so the bandwidth line on the invoice moved much less than the config change suggested.

    What to change: split the policy on cacheability rather than on content type. Keep brotli-11 for anything with a `Cache-Control: immutable` or a content-hashed URL — see `http-caching-semantics` for which those are. For dynamic responses use the fastest codec that still compresses: at 100 Mbps that is zstd-3 (5.6 ms for the same response, against 16 ms raw, so still a real win). Keep a `Content-Type` allow-list so already-compressed bodies are skipped. And measure the *link* the clients actually have — on a 5 Mbps mobile connection brotli-11 on the fly is genuinely correct, which is why the answer is a policy and not a constant.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a preparation step makes the thing being moved smaller, the preparation costs time on the same clock as the movement, and there is a movement speed above which preparing costs more than it saves — with the whole calculation inverting if the preparation can be done once and reused. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Flat-packing furniture. Disassembling a wardrobe makes it far easier to move and costs an hour at each end; carrying it whole across one room costs two minutes. So there is a distance below which flat-packing is a straight loss, and the "bandwidth" is how fast you can move the assembled object. The inversion is the manufacturer's: they flat-pack once, in a factory, for millions of units, at which point the preparation cost per unit is nil and the only thing left is how small it goes. Same object, same physics, and IKEA and your Saturday reach opposite conclusions for exactly the reason this lesson gives.

    Other instances: freeze-drying food for a trek — worth days of processing for an expedition and absurd for tonight's dinner, and note the decompression term, because rehydrating takes the walker's time too. Compressing a spring to store energy, where the loading work is paid at the same joules the release returns and friction makes the round trip a loss above some cycle rate. And a legal summary: distilling a thousand-page filing to four pages costs a solicitor a day and saves every subsequent reader an afternoon, so it pays if there will be many readers and not if there will be one — the same "can you reuse the preparation" test, with reader count standing in for cache hits.

    The instance that fails the analogy is worth having: a shipping container does not compress anything, it standardises. Standardisation has no preparation-versus-movement trade at all, which is why it won so completely — and it is a useful reminder that "make it smaller" and "make it uniform" are different optimisations with different economics.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
