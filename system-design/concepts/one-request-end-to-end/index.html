Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all One request end to end explained well nowhere else

Your server has 205 milliseconds of buffer in front of it, and one garbage-collection pause spends all of it

A request passes through two kernel queues before any line of your code runs, and both of them drop connections without producing a single application-visible event. At 20,000 new connections a second the modern default accept queue absorbs 204.8 ms of silence — the historical default absorbed 6.4 ms. This is the vocabulary the rest of the tier assumes.

01

The clients are timing out. The server log has nothing in it — not an error, not a line.

A deploy rolls. Thirty seconds later support has forty tickets saying the site would not load, and your monitoring is completely calm: zero 5xx responses, p99 latency unchanged, CPU at 40%, no restarts, no out-of-memory kills. You grep the application log for the exact minute of the complaints. There is nothing there. Not an error you could investigate — nothing, as though those users never arrived.

They did arrive. Here is what happened, with the numbers.

The service is behind a load balancer that opens 20,000 new connections a second across the fleet during the deploy, because every existing keep-alive connection to the replaced instances died at once and every client reconnected. On one instance, the process that accepts those connections stops for 250 milliseconds — a stop-the-world garbage collection, or a synchronous config reload, or a first-request code path that touches the disk. Not a crash. A pause.

During those 250 milliseconds the kernel keeps doing its job. It completes the TCP handshake for every arriving connection, tells each client “you are connected”, and stacks the finished connections up waiting for your process to ask for the next one. It can stack up 4,096 of them, which is the default on any Linux since 5.4. At 20,000 a second, 4,096 connections is

4,096 connections ÷ 20,000 connections per second = 204.8 milliseconds

The pause was 250. From 204.8 ms onward, every new connection is discarded — and it is discarded by the kernel, in a socket your process has never been handed.

904 connections are destroyed. The clients see a connection that opened and then went silent until their own timeout fired. Your process logs nothing, because from its point of view nothing happened: it never accepted those sockets, so there is no request object, no handler invocation, no access-log line, no error, no metric. The only record anywhere is a counter in the kernel called ListenOverflows, which nobody is scraping.

That is the shape of this entire lesson. There is machinery in front of your code that can lose work, and it is not instrumented by anything you wrote. Before you can reason about capacity, queues or timeouts, you need the names of the parts and the order they come in — which is what the rest of this page is.

02

The obvious fix is to raise the backlog. It converts a refusal into a four-second wait.

The fix suggests itself. The queue overflowed, so make the queue bigger. sysctl -w net.core.somaxconn=65535, restart, done — and it is worth doing, because 4,096 is a small number. But it does not do what it looks like it does, and the way it fails is the thing worth understanding before you reach for it.

Raising the backlog helps exactly one case: a pause shorter than the new backlog ÷ λ. 65,535 at 20,000 a second is 3.28 seconds of slack, so it comfortably covers a 250 ms garbage collection. What it does not touch at all is the other case — the one where your accept loop is not paused, it is simply slower than the arrival rate.

Suppose the accept path costs 60 µs per connection, so the loop can complete 16,667 accepts a second against 20,000 arriving. The load offered to the acceptor is ρ = 1.2. Here is the same overload at five different backlogs, with the loss rate and the time a connection spends established and unattended:

BacklogConnections droppedMean unattended wait
12816.667%7.44 ms
51116.667%30.42 ms
1,02416.667%61.20 ms
4,09616.667%245.52 ms
65,53516.667%3,931.86 ms

A 512-fold increase in the buffer changed the loss by nothing and multiplied the wait by 528×. The reason is arithmetic rather than tuning: a queue is a device for absorbing a temporary mismatch between arrival and service. When the mismatch is permanent, the loss rate settles at 1 − 1/ρ — here 1 − 1/1.2 = 16.667% — and the backlog cancels out of the answer entirely. What the extra 61,439 slots bought was four seconds of a connection sitting there established, which for any client with a 2-second connect timeout is worse than being refused at once: the client has given up and gone away, and your server is about to accept its socket and do the work anyway.

Predict 20,000 new connections a second, an accept loop that can only manage 16,667 of them (so ρ = 1.2), and somaxconn at 128. You raise somaxconn to 65,535 — 512× deeper. What happens to the share of connections dropped?
Above the acceptor's capacity the queue fills permanently, so the loss rate converges on 1 − 1/ρ, which at ρ = 1.2 is 16.667% — and the model returns exactly 16.667% at a backlog of 128 and exactly 16.667% at 65,535. The backlog is not in that expression. What the deeper queue actually changed was the wait: 7.44 ms to 3,931.86 ms. A buffer converts loss into latency, and when the overload is permanent there is no amount of buffer that converts it into throughput.
Show it without answering

Three things to do to it before reading on.

  • Set the backlog to 128 and leave everything else alone. The slack collapses from 204.8 ms to 6.4 ms, and 872 connections die in a 50 ms pause. That was the Linux default for the whole of its history until 5.4 in 2019, and it is still the effective bound whenever a framework passes a small backlog argument to listen() — the kernel uses min(somaxconn, backlog), so your sysctl loses to the library’s hard-coded 128.
  • Drag the pause down until the crimson wedge disappears, then look at the drain time. A 50 ms pause at a 4,096 backlog loses nothing and still leaves 1,000 connections queued, taking 200 ms to clear. A pause of exactly 204.8 ms — the last one that loses nothing at all — fills the queue to 4,096 and takes 819 ms to clear it, four times the pause that caused it. Nobody was refused; four thousand people waited most of a second for a server that had not noticed them yet, and no server-side latency metric recorded any of it.
  • Drag the handler’s work from 1 µs to 10 ms. At 1 µs your code is under 2% of the request. At 10 ms it is 99%. The fixed part never changes size — it is 69 µs at every position of that slider, which is why “the framework overhead is 60% of my request” is a statement about how little your handler does, not about the framework.
03

The intuition: two waiting rooms and a receptionist who stepped away

Forget packets for a moment. Think about a doctor’s surgery.

Someone phones to make an appointment. The receptionist answers, takes their name, and says “come in”. The caller is now in a provisional state: they have been promised something, and the surgery is holding a scrap of paper with their name on it, but they have not turned up yet. The surgery only holds that scrap for a while — if the person never arrives, the paper goes in the bin. That is the SYN queue: half-finished arrangements, held for as long as it takes the other side to complete them, and the other side controls how long that is.

Then the person walks in and sits down in the waiting room. They are definitely here now. As far as they are concerned they are being seen by the surgery — they arrived, they are inside, the process has begun. But nobody has spoken to them. The receptionist has not looked up, has not taken their details, does not know they exist. That is the accept queue: people who have completely and successfully arrived, and whom nobody has greeted.

Now the receptionist steps away for four minutes. People keep walking in, because the front door still works — the door is the kernel and it is not the thing that paused. The waiting room has sixteen chairs. Once those sixteen are full, the seventeenth person who walks in is turned around at the door and sent home.

Here is the whole lesson. When the receptionist comes back and asks “how was this morning?”, her notes say she saw everybody promptly and nobody complained. She is not lying. She never met the people who were turned away at the door, so they are in nobody’s notes. The only person who knows how many there were is the person on the door, and nobody ever asks them.

Two consequences follow directly, and both are quantified in the next slot. More chairs do not make the receptionist faster — if she is permanently slower than the rate people arrive, the room is permanently full and the same fraction gets turned away whether there are sixteen chairs or sixteen hundred. And filling the chairs is not free even when nobody is turned away, because everybody in one is a person who arrived and is waiting, and the surgery’s own records show none of that time.

04

The mechanism: the named stages, and Little's Law twice

Here is the actual path, in order, for one request. The two queues are the parts nothing in your process can see; the shaded band is the interval during which the client believes it has a working connection and your code has not been told anything exists.

Queue one: the SYN queue, and why its size is not your decision

A SYN arrives. The kernel replies SYN-ACK and creates a half-open entry — a small structure recording that a handshake is in progress. The entry lives until the client’s ACK completes the handshake, which takes one round trip. That is a residence time, so the depth is littles-law directly:

half-open entries = new connections per second × round-trip time

20,000 a second at a 30 ms round trip is 600 entries. The bound is net.ipv4.tcp_max_syn_backlog, typically 4,096 or 1,024 depending on the machine’s memory.

For honest clients that is comfortable — you would need 136,533 connections a second before 600 became 4,096. The problem is that W is set by the client, not by you. If the ACK never comes, the kernel does not discard the entry; it retransmits the SYN-ACK on a doubling schedule and holds the entry through the whole of it. With tcp_synack_retries at its default of 5 that is 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 = 63 seconds.

Run Little’s Law backwards with that W and you get the reason SYN cookies exist:

4,096 entries ÷ 63 seconds = 65 unacknowledged SYNs per second

Sixty-five packets a second — a few kilobits — keeps a 4,096-entry SYN queue permanently full, at which point legitimate handshakes cannot start. At the older 1,024 the figure is 16 a second.

That asymmetry — 65 against 136,533, a factor of over 2,000 — is why this queue has a special mechanism rather than just a bigger number. net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies encodes the connection state into the initial sequence number of the SYN-ACK and keeps no entry at all, reconstructing it from the client’s ACK. It is not free: the encoding has no room for the TCP options negotiated in the SYN, so window scaling, selective acknowledgement and timestamps are degraded for cookie-established connections. Which is exactly why it is armed as an overflow behaviour rather than used all the time.

Queue two: the accept queue, and the state that has no name in your code

When the ACK lands the connection is ESTABLISHED. This is the moment worth being pedantic about, because it is where most people’s mental model is wrong. The handshake is complete. The client is entitled to send data and usually does — with TCP Fast Open or a fast client it may already have. Nothing in your process knows. The kernel moves the socket onto the listening socket’s accept queue, whose depth is bounded by min(net.core.somaxconn, the backlog argument to listen()), and there it sits until your code calls accept().

That queue’s depth is Little’s Law again, with a different W:

established-but-unaccepted = new connections per second × time to get accepted

Same law as the SYN queue, different residence time. Here W is under your control — it is how promptly your process loops back round to accept() — which is the good news and the whole trap.

Steady state is a bounded single-server queue — queueing’s subject with a finite buffer bolted on — and it has exactly two regimes, which is the finding this lesson exists for. Let ρ be the offered accept load, arrivals divided by the rate your loop can accept, and let K be the backlog.

Below ρ = 1 it is a latency device. It absorbs everything. The most loss it can produce while the acceptor is still keeping up is 1/(K+2), reached exactly at ρ = 1 — for a 4,096 backlog that is 0.0244%, and at ρ = 0.98 the modelled loss is 2.26 × 10⁻³⁶%, which is to say zero. But it is not free: at ρ = 0.98 the mean depth is 49 connections and the mean unattended wait is 2.45 ms, none of which appears in a server-side latency metric.

At and above ρ = 1 it becomes a loss device, and the loss rate is 1 − 1/ρ regardless of K. There is no gradual ramp between the two:

Read the first column against the second. A 1% overload of the accept loop produces a 1% connection loss, and a 2% overload produces essentially nothing. There is no comfortable middle in which dashboards degrade gently and somebody notices; the mode changes at the capacity of the accept loop, and it changes from “slow” to “refused”. That is the threshold, and expressing it as an accept latency at 20,000 connections a second: 49 µs per accept is fine, 50.51 µs loses 1% of your users.

What overflow actually does, which depends on one sysctl

When the accept queue is full the kernel’s response is governed by net.ipv4.tcp_abort_on_overflow, and both settings are bad in a different direction.

  • 0 (the default) — drop the ACK silently. The connection stays half-open on the server and the client, which has already moved to ESTABLISHED, sees nothing at all. It sends its request into a connection the server does not consider open, gets no response, and eventually hits its own timeout. The server-side evidence is one increment of ListenOverflows and one of ListenDrops. From the client’s side this is indistinguishable from the network having eaten the packet, which is why it gets blamed on the network.
  • 1 — send a RST. The client gets ECONNRESET immediately, which is honest and fast, and which also destroys the connection’s chance of being saved by the client’s SYN retransmission a second later. The kernel documentation’s own advice is to leave it at 0 unless you are certain the overload is permanent rather than a burst — because with 0, a burst that clears in 200 ms is recovered by retransmission and nobody notices.

Note what is absent from both branches: an application-visible event. accept() does not return an error. No handler runs. Nothing in your process is in a position to count it. The counters are kernel-side (nstat -az | grep -i listen, ss -lnt for Recv-Q on a listening socket) and if you are not collecting them, this failure mode is invisible by construction.

And then the stages, once you finally have the socket

Only now does the request exist as far as your code is concerned. Here is the whole per-request path for a small fast endpoint — a few hundred bytes in, about 2 KB of JSON out, on an already-established connection, so no handshake and no TLS. Three readings of that table, in increasing order of usefulness.

The naive one: your code is 36.7% of the request. True, and not very actionable, because it is a statement about this particular handler.

The better one: 31 µs — 28.4% — is already spent when your framework logs “request received”. Everything down to and including read() happened before any timer you own started. A handler-entry timer reporting 40 µs is not lying; it is measuring the fifth of ten stages. This is the same measurement trap littles-law catches from the other side, and the same answer applies: prefer the quantity that is counted over the quantity that is timed, because a count of unaccepted sockets cannot be moved by where somebody put the start = now().

The best one: 69 µs of it is a fixed cost, not a percentage. Halve your handler from 40 µs to 20 µs and the request goes from 109 µs to 89 µs — an 18% improvement, not 50%. Make the handler infinitely fast and you get 69 µs. Which means the useful threshold is:

your handler is the majority of the request only above 69 µs of work

Below that you are mostly paying for the interface to the kernel, and the lever is batching syscalls (io_uring, sendmmsg, larger reads) rather than optimising your logic.

At 5 µs of handler work the request is 74 µs and your code is 6.8% of it, so one core is doing 13,514 requests a second and 93% of that core is the machinery. At 1 ms of handler work the request is 1,069 µs, your code is 93.5%, one core does 935 a second, and the 69 µs has stopped mattering at all. Both are ordinary services. Which one you are decides whether the next thing to read is single-machine-ceiling or your own profiler.

What one connection costs while it does nothing

The last piece of the anatomy, because it is what caps connection counts long before CPU does. An established socket has two costs and they differ by a factor of 49.

The structures are not tunable and are cheap. The buffers are tunable and are 98% of the cost, and the number to plan against is the ceiling rather than the floor, because the case that runs a box out of memory is not the idle one — it is a slow client that has stopped reading while your server has written a full response into the send buffer. A million idle WebSocket connections is a memory-feasible idea. A million connections each holding a response is 140 GiB and is not.

This is also the honest reason the C10K problem was ever hard, and the part that is not about threads: even with a perfect event loop, a hundred thousand connections with default buffers is 14 GiB of kernel memory before your application has allocated anything. Whether you serve them with threads or an event loop is a separate question, and a later one.

05

The numbers: when does any of this matter

The two queues matter at completely different scales, and the deciding variable is not requests per second — it is new connections per second. A service doing 200,000 requests a second over persistent connections opens almost none; a service doing 2,000 requests a second from mobile clients on flaky networks with no keep-alive opens 2,000.

So the table that locates you is accept-queue slack — backlog ÷ λ, in milliseconds, which is how long your process may go without calling accept():

Now put a real pause next to it. A young-generation collection is 1–10 ms; a full collection on a several-gigabyte heap is 100 ms to several seconds — see gc-pauses. A synchronous DNS lookup on the accept path is 20–200 ms. A container throttled by its CFS quota can be descheduled for a full 100 ms period. A synchronous log flush to a busy disk is 10–50 ms.

Read the two together and the conclusions are uncomfortable and specific.

  • Under about 1,000 new connections a second, none of this is your problem. Even the historical 128 gives you 128 ms of slack, and you will hit some other ceiling first. This is most internal services and most applications behind a proxy that holds persistent connections.
  • From a few thousand new connections a second, the default is one ordinary pause deep. At 20,000 a second the modern 4,096 is 204.8 ms — smaller than a single full GC and smaller than one CFS throttling period. This is where the incident in slot 1 lives, and it is a deploy-shaped problem because a deploy is what synchronises every client into reconnecting at once.
  • At any rate, an accept loop that is 1% slower than arrivals loses 1% of connections. That is independent of scale and independent of the backlog. It is the reason the accept loop should do nothing except accept — no logging, no TLS handshake, no lookups, no allocation you could have pooled.

For the SYN queue the number to know is smaller and does not scale with your traffic at all: 65 unacknowledged SYNs a second fills a 4,096-entry queue, 16 a second fills a 1,024-entry one. There is no legitimate traffic level at which you need to tune this. Either enable tcp_syncookies — it is on by default on essentially every modern distribution — or accept that a single host with a kilobit of upstream can stop new connections to your server.

06

The trade-off: every one of these buffers converts a loss into a latency, and you cannot have neither

There is one trade running through this whole page and it is worth stating in the general form, because it is the same trade in the NIC ring, the SYN queue, the accept queue, the socket buffers and every application queue you will ever add: a buffer converts refusal into delay. That is the only thing it does. It never creates capacity.

Which means the choice in front of you is never “drop or don’t drop”. It is “drop now, or serve late”, and the honest question is which one your client prefers — a question about the client’s timeout, not about your server.

The specific costs, each with its number.

A deeper accept queue buys latency you cannot see and cannot shed. The ρ = 1.2 table in slot 2 is the whole argument: 128 to 65,535 changed the loss by zero and the unattended wait from 7.44 ms to 3,931.86 ms. Worse, that wait is behind your admission control. Load shedding, rate limiting, circuit breaking and concurrency limits all operate on requests your process has accepted; a connection rotting in the accept queue has not been accepted, so it is invisible to all of them and cannot be rejected cheaply. A deep backlog is a queue with no backpressure and no load-shedding — you have built the one queue in your system you cannot manage. The defensible position is a backlog sized to your worst tolerable pause and no larger, plus an actual admission controller behind it.

A shallower accept queue turns ordinary jitter into user-visible failures. The opposite error, and the reason the kernel default moved from 128 to 4,096: at 128 a single 7 ms hiccup at 20,000 connections a second starts dropping, and 7 ms hiccups are not a bug you can eliminate. There is a genuine floor here and it is set by the pauses you actually have, which is an argument for measuring your pause distribution before choosing a number.

SYN cookies buy availability with degraded connections. They eliminate the queue by keeping no state, which is the correct trade under attack, and the cost is that the TCP options negotiated in the SYN do not fit in the cookie. Window scaling, SACK and timestamps come back reduced or absent, so a cookie-established connection has worse throughput and worse loss recovery for its whole lifetime — see bandwidth-delay-product for what losing window scaling does to a long fat link. Correct as an overflow behaviour, wrong as a steady state.

tcp_abort_on_overflow=1 buys a clear error and loses the retransmission that would have saved you. With the default 0, a burst that clears within the client’s SYN retransmission window recovers by itself and nobody ever knows. With 1 the client gets an immediate ECONNRESET, which is much easier to debug and permanently destroys connections that a 200 ms wait would have rescued. It is the right setting only when you have decided the overload is permanent — which is a policy decision, not a tuning one.

Bigger socket buffers buy throughput and cost you connection count. tcp_rmem and tcp_wmem are what make a high-bandwidth-delay-product link go fast, and they are also 98% of what a connection costs: the same 16 GiB holds 5,592,405 idle sockets or 114,130 with both default buffers committed. If you serve many slow connections, small buffers plus backpressure beats large buffers plus an out-of-memory kill. If you serve a few fat transfers, the reverse. There is no setting that is right for both, which is why this is a per-service decision and not a global one.

And the honest verdict, which is the reason this lesson is early in the tier rather than late: almost every incident of the form “the server was fine and the client saw errors” is one of these two queues, and neither of them appears in an application metric. The server genuinely was fine. The instrumentation was measuring the requests that survived the buffer, and saying nothing whatever about the ones that did not. If you take one operational action from this page, it is to scrape ListenOverflows, ListenDrops, TCPReqQFullDrop and the listening socket’s Recv-Q, because they are the only witnesses that exist.

07

In the wild

The C10K problem was the first time this path was written down as a whole. Dan Kegel’s The C10K Problem (1999, revised through the 2000s) is the document that made a generation of engineers enumerate the stages — the poll and select ceilings, the per-connection memory, the accept path, the thundering herd on a shared listening socket — rather than treating a server as a box that answers requests. It is worth reading now for the framing more than the API advice: the arithmetic aged much better than the syscalls did.

Linux raised the default somaxconn from 128 to 4,096 in kernel 5.4 (2019). The commit message is blunter than most documentation about this: the old value was chosen when machines and networks were much slower and had become a source of hard-to-diagnose connection failures. Thirty-two times the buffer, and the reason it took twenty years is that the failure it prevents produces no server-side error — which is this lesson’s thesis, confirmed by how long it took to fix.

Every large-scale HTTP server’s tuning guide is a walk down this path, in this order. nginx’s listen ... backlog=N, somaxconn, tcp_max_syn_backlog, tcp_syncookies, worker connections, tcp_rmem and tcp_wmem are not a grab-bag; they are one queue per stage, and they are the same stages as the table in slot 4. If a tuning guide’s list has ever looked arbitrary, it is because the path was never drawn.

SYN cookies were invented in 1996 for exactly the arithmetic above. Daniel J. Bernstein’s design, in response to the SYN flooding attacks of that year, eliminates the half-open state entirely by encoding it into the sequence number. The reason it is still an overflow mechanism rather than the default behaviour is the option-loss cost in slot 6 — a good example of a mitigation that is correct precisely because it is conditional.

Brendan Gregg’s network analysis method walks the same stack downward. Systems Performance (2nd ed., 2020) works from the application through the socket, TCP, IP, the driver ring and the wire, with the counter to check at each layer, and makes the same general point this page makes about the accept queue: each layer drops for its own reasons and counts its own drops, so a method that looks only at the application will keep concluding the problem is elsewhere.

Netflix, and everyone else, found it during deploys. The pattern in slot 1 — connection errors concentrated in the seconds after an instance is replaced, with clean server-side metrics — recurs in public postmortems across the industry, and the mechanism is always the same: replacing an instance synchronises every client into reconnecting at once, which converts a modest steady-state connection rate into a large instantaneous one. thundering-herd is the general form of that synchronisation, and this page is what it lands on.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • There are two kernel queues before your code, not one: the SYN queue holds half-open connections for one round trip, the accept queue holds fully established ones until your process calls accept()
  • Accept-queue depth is Little's Law with W = the time to get accepted, and the pause the queue survives is backlog ÷ λ
  • A full accept queue drops or resets the connection, and produces no application-visible event at all — the server never accepted it, so there is no request to log
  • Below accept capacity the queue is a latency device whose worst loss is 1/(K+2); above capacity it is a loss device whose rate is 1 − 1/ρ and does not depend on the backlog
  • A request's stages are NIC, softirq, socket buffer, accept, read, parse, handler, serialise, write, TCP output — and everything that is not the handler is a fixed cost in microseconds, not a percentage
  • A connection costs about 3 KB of kernel structures while idle and up to about 147 KB with both default buffers committed, so buffers rather than sockets set the connection ceiling

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Connections are arriving at 20,000 a second and your accept loop is 20% slower than that. You raise somaxconn from 128 to 65,535 — a 512-fold increase. What did that buy, and what did it not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It bought nothing at all in loss and a great deal in waiting. Above capacity the loss rate converges on 1 − 1/ρ, which at ρ = 1.2 is 16.667%, and the model gives exactly 16.667% at a backlog of 128 and exactly 16.667% at 65,535. What changed is the time a connection sits established and unattended: 7.44 ms at 128, 3,931.86 ms at 65,535 — a 528× increase. So a deeper queue converts refused connections into connections that are accepted four seconds late, which is worse than refusal for anything with a client-side timeout. A bigger buffer buys time, never throughput.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Your service is healthy on every dashboard — no errors, flat latency, CPU at 40% — and clients report connection timeouts during deploys. Where do you look, and what number do you compute first?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The accept queue, because it is the only place a connection can die without your process ever learning it existed: the kernel completed the handshake, the client believes it is connected, and the socket was never handed to you, so there is nothing for your framework to log or count. The number to compute is backlog ÷ λ — how many seconds of not-calling-accept() the queue absorbs. At 20,000 connections a second that is 6.4 ms at the historical somaxconn of 128 and 204.8 ms at the modern 4,096. Then check ss -lnt for a non-zero Recv-Q on the listening socket and the ListenOverflows counter in nstat. Those two are kernel-side and are the only evidence that exists.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why is a half-open connection's cost under the client's control rather than yours, and what does that imply about the size of a SYN flood?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the residence time of a SYN-queue entry is however long the kernel waits for the client's ACK. For an honest client that is one round trip, so depth is λ × RTT — 20,000 a second at 30 ms is 600 entries. For a client that never ACKs, the kernel retransmits the SYN-ACK on a doubling schedule and holds the entry for the whole of it: with tcp_synack_retries = 5 that is 1+2+4+8+16+32 = 63 seconds. Little's Law run backwards then says the rate needed to keep a 4,096-deep SYN queue permanently full is 4096 ÷ 63 = 65 packets per second. An honest client base would need 136,533 a second to do the same. That five-orders-of-magnitude gap is the entire reason SYN cookies exist.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A profiler says your handler takes 40 µs. What is the whole request costing the machine, and what happens to that figure if you make the handler twice as fast?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About 109 µs, of which your handler is 36.7%. The other 69 µs is the kernel receive path, two syscall boundaries, a context switch, the framework's parse and route, serialisation, and the kernel transmit path. Halving the handler to 20 µs takes the request to 89 µs — an 18% improvement, not a 50% one — because the 69 µs is a fixed cost in microseconds, not a proportion. The corollary is the useful one: your handler only becomes the majority of the request once it does more than 69 µs of work, and below that threshold you are mostly paying for the syscall interface rather than for your own logic.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Your framework logs "request received" and starts its timer there. Name the work that has already happened, and say roughly how much of the request it was.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The NIC has DMAed the frame and raised an interrupt, a softirq has run the driver poll, skb allocation, IP receive, netfilter and TCP input, the payload has been queued on the socket, the blocked task has been woken, a context switch has run, and read() has copied the bytes into user space. That is 31 µs of the 109 µs request — 28.4% — and none of it is inside any timer you own. It is also why littles-law's backwards reading matters here: a count of established-but-unaccepted sockets is kernel-side and cannot be moved by where you put the start = now().
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Someone proposes holding a million idle connections on one 16 GiB box. Is that memory-feasible, and what is the question you have to ask before answering?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Idle, yes: the socket structures are about 3 KB each, so a million of them is 2,930 MiB and a 16 GiB box would hold 5,592,405. But "idle" is doing all the work in that sentence. With both default buffers committed — tcp_rmem's 131,072 and tcp_wmem's 16,384 — a connection is about 147 KB, which is 49× more, and the same box holds 114,130. So the question is not how many connections but how many will have bytes in flight at once, and the honest planning figure is the buffer ceiling rather than the idle floor. It is the buffers, never the sockets, that set the ceiling.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A machine is dropping packets and every application metric is clean. This time the accept queue is empty and the CPU is at 30%, but ethtool -S shows a rising rx_dropped and /proc/net/softnet_stat shows a rising second column. What is the mechanism, and why is the shape of it familiar?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is the same shape one layer down: a bounded buffer in front of a consumer that stopped consuming for a moment. The NIC DMAs frames into a fixed-size receive ring; the NET_RX softirq drains it, but only for a budget of packets per invocation and only when a core is available to run it. If the softirq is delayed — a long-running kernel section, a core pinned by a busy poll loop, an interrupt landing on a core doing something else — the ring fills at line rate and the NIC overwrites or discards. The ring is measured in thousands of descriptors, so at 10 Gbps the tolerance is microseconds rather than the accept queue's milliseconds. And as with the accept queue, the loss is counted only on the buffer's own side: rx_dropped and the softnet backlog counter, never in an application metric, because the packet never became a request. The general rule the two share is that every bounded buffer in the path has its own overflow counter, and none of them is in your process.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a bounded buffer sits in front of a server, and when it overflows the work is destroyed silently — recorded, if at all, only on the buffer's side, so the server's own instrumentation reports perfect health while demand is being thrown away. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Ambulance diversion at a hospital. When the emergency department is full it goes "on divert" and ambulances are sent elsewhere. Every metric inside the department — treatment time, mortality, occupancy — looks fine or improves, because the patients who were turned away are not in the denominator. The diversion is logged by the ambulance service, not the hospital. Trunk lines at a call centre. If every line is busy the caller gets a busy signal and never reaches the queue, so the ACD reports excellent answer times while callers are being refused; the count lives with the carrier. Airport holding and diversion. A full holding stack diverts aircraft to another field, and the destination's on-time statistics improve as a result. A restaurant's phone. Nobody records the reservations that rang out. In every case the pattern is identical: the entity that discards the work and the entity that reports on the work are different entities, so measuring only the server tells you about the requests that survived the buffer and nothing about the ones that did not. The operational rule transfers exactly — instrument the buffer, not just the server.
    Did you have that?
