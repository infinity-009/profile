Tier 10 · AI-era systems Prefill/decode disaggregation explained well nowhere else

The disaggregated pool needs 16% more GPUs and is 4 to 7 times faster on the number users actually feel

llm-inference-phases established that prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound in the same request, and stopped there. This lesson asks the deployment question that observation forces: if both phases run on one shared pool of GPUs — the naive thing to build, and the only thing continuous-in-flight-batching's own scheduler needs to make work at all — a newly arrived prompt's prefill queues behind every decode iteration already in flight, because the scheduler protects tokens already streaming to a connected user. Splitting the phases onto two independently-sized pools removes that queue entirely, at the cost of a GPU-equivalent that neither pool would need alone, and a KV-cache transfer over the network between them.

Read first — llm-inference-phasescontinuous-in-flight-batching

01

Same GPUs, two workloads, one scheduler forced to pick a priority

llm-inference-phases established the fact and stopped at the observation: prefill is compute-bound, decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, in the same request.

That distance is why the two phases want different machines, and it is the reason llm-inference-phases ends where it does. It never asked what a real serving engine does when both phases share one pool of GPUs — which is the only thing continuous-in-flight-batching’s iteration-level scheduler needs in order to work at all. That scheduler protects tokens already streaming to connected users, which means a newly arrived prompt’s prefill is low priority against decode iterations already in flight. Non-preemptive priority queueing has a term a same-class queue does not: (1 − ρ_high) in the wait-time denominator, where ρ_high is decode’s own utilisation — and operators push decode utilisation up on purpose, because idle GPU-seconds are the thing costing money.

02

The belief this breaks: 'we'll deal with tail latency by adding capacity later'

The unified-pool design is not naive about capacity — it is naive about which capacity fixes tail latency. Adding more unified GPUs lowers ρ overall, but as long as decode is still run near its own cost-efficient target, prefill’s wait time is still gated by decode’s utilisation, not the pool’s total size. A dedicated prefill pool sidesteps the problem structurally rather than by brute force: its wait time is the plain M/D/1 formula, ρ/(2(1−ρ))·E[S], with no term that depends on any other workload’s utilisation at all.

priorityWaitMs = rhoLow · sLowMs² / (2(1 − rhoHigh)) + rhoHigh · sHighMs² / (2(1 − rhoHigh)(1 − rhoHigh − rhoLow))

Cobham’s (1954) non-preemptive priority M/G/1 formula, unchanged. The (1 − rhoHigh) factor appears in both terms’ denominators — so low-priority (prefill) wait depends on high-priority (decode) utilisation twice over, and diverges as rhoHigh → 1 regardless of how light prefill’s own load is.

03

The intuition: a shared kitchen line versus two dedicated stations

A restaurant kitchen can run appetisers and entrées off one shared line of cooks, or split them into two dedicated stations. The shared line needs fewer total cooks, because slack time on one dish absorbs demand for the other. But the moment the entrée station is run near its own cost-efficient capacity — cooks kept busy, not idle — a new appetiser order queues behind whatever entrée is already on the pan, because the kitchen’s ticket rail is built to protect a dish already cooking over one that has not started. Push entrée load high enough and appetiser wait does not degrade gracefully; it compounds, because every entrée ahead of it in the queue is itself protected from its successor. Two dedicated stations remove the coupling entirely: appetiser wait depends only on appetiser volume, forever, no matter how busy entrées get.

04

The mechanism: two closed forms, and the one term that only appears in the shared case

src/sim/disagg.mjs prices both architectures from the same primitives inference.mjs already established — ttft, tpot, decodeThroughput — reusing them unchanged. Two queueing formulas do the rest, both textbook:

M/D/1 mean wait = ρ / (2(1 − ρ)) · E[S]

A dedicated queue of same-sized jobs (Pollaczek-Khinchine at zero service variance). This is prefill’s own wait time in the disaggregated design — no other workload’s utilisation appears anywhere in it.

p99 ≈ mean × ln(100)

Both queues are asymptotically exponential-tailed in heavy traffic (Kingman 1962) — an order-of-magnitude device for a percentile, not a certified one, used identically for both architectures so the comparison stays apples-to-apples.

gpuEquivalents converts a traffic rate into GPU-equivalents of prefill and decode demand from inference.mjs’s own per-GPU ceilings — nothing new derived here. disaggregatedPool sizes each pool independently at its own target utilisation; unifiedPool sizes one pool at decode’s target and derives what share of it prefill’s demand actually consumes (prefillRho), which is the input priorityWaitMs needs. kvTransferMs prices the one cost disaggregation alone pays: prompt tokens × bytes-per-token (BF16 KV, reused from kv-cache unchanged) ÷ network bandwidth, plus a fixed per-transfer overhead.

06

The trade-off: when disaggregation stops paying for itself

Disaggregation’s whole case rests on decode being run hot. At low decode utilisation (70% in the table above), the unified design’s p99 TTFT penalty shrinks to about 1.7× — real, but far from the 4-7× seen at realistic 90% targets. An operator willing to run decode at low utilisation gets most of disaggregation’s latency benefit for free, at the cost of the GPU-hour saving decode’s own low utilisation gives up.

The GPU-count gap is smaller and more stable than the latency gap. Across all three traffic shapes, disaggregation costs 13-23% more GPUs — a real, bounded, budgetable number. The latency gap is not bounded the same way: it grows without limit as decode heat approaches 100%, which is exactly the region a cost-conscious operator is drawn toward.

The network transfer is the cost most naive “just split it” arguments forget to count, and the one this lesson is least worried about. It is real money and real milliseconds, but at the traffic volumes where disaggregation’s latency advantage matters most, it is a minority term next to the queueing cost it replaces.

07

In the wild

DistServe (Zhong et al., 2024) is the paper that names this exact architecture and reports prefill/decode interference as the dominant cause of SLO violations in unified serving, motivating physically separate GPU pools connected by a dedicated KV-cache transfer path — the shape disagg.mjs prices.

Splitwise (Patel et al., ISCA 2024, Microsoft Azure) reports the same split in production terms: prefill-optimised and decode-optimised machine pools, chosen because the two phases’ resource profiles (compute-bound versus memory-bandwidth-bound) make one machine shape wrong for both — the same fact llm-inference-phases already established, taken to its deployment conclusion.

Mooncake (Qin et al., 2024, Moonshot AI/Kimi) disaggregates further, adding a dedicated KV-cache-centric scheduler and reporting the transfer cost this lesson prices as kvTransferMs as a first-class scheduling input rather than an afterthought.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A unified pool's scheduler treats a newly arrived prompt's prefill as low priority against decode iterations already streaming to connected users, and non-preemptive priority queueing has a (1 − ρ_high) term in its wait-time denominator that a same-class queue does not
  • That term is driven by decode's utilisation, not prefill's — so pushing decode toward saturation for cost reasons inflates prefill's wait time even though prefill's own load never changed
  • A dedicated prefill pool never carries that term, because nothing above it in priority exists; its wait time is the plain M/D/1 formula, ρ/(2(1−ρ))·E[S]
  • Disaggregation costs a real, non-zero KV-cache transfer over the network between pools — real and measurable, but small next to the queueing term it removes at realistic decode heat
  • Across three traffic shapes (chat, RAG, agent) a unified pool is always 15-30% cheaper in raw GPU count and always 4-7x slower on p99 time-to-first-token, at the same 90% decode utilisation target
  • The two costs are not symmetric with load: GPU count difference stays roughly proportional, while the latency ratio can grow without bound as decode heat approaches 100%, because (1 − ρ_high) approaches zero in the denominator

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A unified pool and a disaggregated pool are sized for the identical RAG traffic (8,192-token prompts, 20 requests/sec). The unified pool needs fewer total GPUs. Why does it still lose, and by how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because GPU count and tail latency are not the same axis, and the unified pool's saving comes entirely from sharing headroom that its own scheduling policy then spends against prefill. At this traffic, disaggregation needs 104 GPUs (39 prefill, 65 decode) for a p99 TTFT of about 5.1 seconds; the unified pool needs only 90 — 13% fewer — but its p99 TTFT is about 20.9 seconds, roughly 4.1× worse. The saving is real: sharing one pool means neither workload pays for the other's idle capacity. The cost is also real and lands on exactly the number a user notices first, because the unified scheduler's non-preemptive priority queue makes every new prompt wait behind whatever decode iterations are already running, and decode is deliberately run hot (90% utilisation) to keep its own GPU-hour cost down.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    `priorityWaitMs` has a `(1 − ρ_high)` term in its denominator where a plain M/D/1 queue has `(1 − ρ)`. Concretely, what happens to a unified pool's prefill wait time as the operator pushes decode utilisation from 80% toward 99%, holding prefill's own load fixed?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It grows without bound, and does so entirely independent of anything happening to prefill. `rhoHigh` in that denominator is decode's own utilisation, so as it approaches 1 the term (1 − rhoHigh) approaches 0 and the whole expression diverges — the function throws rather than returning a number once the unified pool crosses that boundary (`ttftUnified`'s `stable: false` case). This is the one failure mode a dedicated prefill pool structurally cannot have: its own wait time depends only on rhoPrefill, the M/D/1 formula's own utilisation term, and nothing decode does to its own knob can move it, because the two pools never share a queue.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Name the one cost this lesson counts that a naive "just split them" argument tends to wave away, and the concrete number for it on RAG traffic over InfiniBand NDR.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The KV-cache transfer: after prefill finishes, its output KV cache — real bytes, in `kvTransferMs`'s accounting — must cross the network to whichever decode GPU picks up the request, and that transfer is not instantaneous. At 8,192 prompt tokens over InfiniBand NDR (50 GB/s), it costs about 55 ms, against a p99 TTFT of roughly 5,132 ms — a little over 1% of the total, real and worth stating exactly rather than assuming zero, but nowhere near the dominant term at this traffic. Switch to RoCE v2 (12.5 GB/s, a quarter the bandwidth) and the transfer alone rises to about 219 ms — still a minority of the total at this load, but the gap narrows as traffic (and therefore the unified pool's queueing penalty) drops.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    A hospital runs one shared pool of doctors who both triage new arrivals AND continue treating admitted patients, versus a design with a dedicated triage team and a separate ward team. The shared-pool hospital needs fewer total doctors for the same patient volume, but new-arrival wait times are far worse whenever the ward team is running near capacity. What is this lesson's equivalent of "the ward team running near capacity," and why does splitting the staff fix the new-arrival wait specifically?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The ward team running near capacity is this lesson's decode utilisation pushed toward its cost-efficient target — deliberately run hot because idle doctors (or idle GPUs) are the thing costing money when nobody needs them. In the shared-pool hospital, triage is structurally lower priority than a patient already mid-treatment, so as the ward team's load rises, triage's wait time inflates for a reason that has nothing to do with how many new arrivals there are — exactly the (1 − ρ_high) mechanism in `priorityWaitMs`. Splitting the staff fixes it because triage's wait time then depends only on the triage team's own load, never on how hard the ward team is being run — the same reason a dedicated prefill pool's wait time never sees decode's utilisation at all.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: sharing one resource pool between a latency-sensitive, low-priority workload and a throughput-sensitive, high-priority workload saves total capacity, but a non-preemptive priority discipline makes the low-priority workload's wait time a function of the high-priority workload's utilisation — and pushing that utilisation up for cost reasons can make the low-priority wait grow without bound, a failure mode a dedicated pool for the low-priority workload cannot have. Name an instance outside GPU serving.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A hospital emergency department's imaging equipment, shared between routine outpatient scans (schedulable, lower priority) and trauma-team scans (must run immediately). As trauma volume is pushed toward the machine's capacity to justify its cost, routine scan wait times do not degrade gracefully — they blow up, because a trauma scan always preempts a queued routine one, and the routine queue's wait time is a function of trauma's utilisation, not its own. A hospital that instead buys a second, dedicated routine-only scanner removes that coupling entirely: routine wait time becomes a function of routine volume alone. Other instances: a shared print server where "rush" jobs jump every queued job, so non-rush wait times spike precisely when rush volume is highest; and a single security checkpoint serving both a VIP fast lane's spillover and the general line, where pushing VIP-lane usage up for revenue degrades general-line wait unpredictably, versus two physically separate lines where general wait depends only on general volume.
    Did you have that?
