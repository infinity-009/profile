Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Stop-the-world pauses explained well nowhere else

A 200 ms pause on 2% of requests gives a 5 ms service a 203 ms p99

A garbage collector, a hypervisor stealing a vCPU, a cgroup CPU quota running out mid-slice and a writeback storm all do the same thing: the process stops for a while and does not know it stopped. That produces a latency bug with a cliff in it — cross 1% of requests and your p99 is the pause length, whatever your code does — and a correctness bug that is worse, because a paused process is indistinguishable from a crashed one and its peers will give its lease away while it still believes it holds it.

Read first — percentilestail-at-scale

01

The service is 5 milliseconds and the p99 is 203

You run a service whose requests take 5 milliseconds on average. The distribution is the ordinary one for a queueing service — exponential — so its p50 is 3.47 ms and its p99 is 23.03 ms. That is a healthy service and there is nothing in it to optimise.

Its garbage collector reports 98% throughput. In collector language that means the application ran for 98% of the wall clock and the collector had the other 2%: concretely, one 200 millisecond stop-the-world pause every 10 seconds. Everybody who reads that line reads it as “2% overhead”, files it as acceptable, and moves on.

The p99 is now 203.47 ms. Not 23. Two hundred and three.

The p50 barely moved — 3.47 ms to 3.57 ms — so the dashboard’s average response time looks untouched. The p999 is 214.98 ms. The ratio between the p99 and the p50 is 57×.

And here is the part that makes this different from every other latency problem: making the service faster cannot fix it. The p99 decomposes into 200 ms of pause and 3.47 ms of request. If you made the request path take literally zero time, the p99 would be exactly 200 ms — a 1.7% improvement for infinite effort.

Nothing here is a garbage-collection tuning problem in particular. A hypervisor descheduling your vCPU to run somebody else’s, a cgroup CPU quota exhausted 35 ms into a 100 ms period, a page-cache writeback storm, an fsync behind a full device queue, a laptop lid closing: each one stops the process for a while, and — this is the whole mechanism — the process cannot observe that it stopped. From inside, one instruction retired and then the next one did.

That single property produces two failures. The first is the number above. The second is worse, and this page is mostly about the second.

02

Optimise the fast path. Watch the number not move.

The mental model almost everyone starts with is that a pause affecting 2% of requests contributes about 2% of the problem — that it is a small, proportional tax on latency, and the p99 must therefore live somewhere in the fast body of the distribution where the profiler can find it.

It does not, and the reason is arithmetic about sorted lists rather than about averages. A percentile is a position. The stalled requests form a block sitting entirely above the pause length, because every one of them took at least 200 ms. The p99 asks where the slowest 1% begins. If the stalled block is smaller than 1%, that boundary is inside the fast body. If the stalled block is larger than 1%, the boundary is inside the block — and the answer is the pause.

So the p99 as a function of the affected fraction is not a slope. It is a cliff, and the edge is at 1%.

Predict A 5 ms exponential service (p99 = 23 ms) with one 200 ms stop-the-world pause every 10 seconds, so 2% of requests absorb the pause. What is the p99?
2% of requests took at least 200 ms, so the slowest 1% is entirely inside that group and the p99 is 203.47 ms. The first option is the belief this slot exists to break: it treats the p99 as something the mean drags along, when a percentile is a position in a sorted list and the stalled requests are a block above the pause. The fourth is wrong for a different reason worth knowing — the backlog does drain, and at any utilisation below 1 the queue recovers between pauses; the damage is bounded by the pause, not compounding across them.
Show it without answering

Three things to take from it, and the third is the one that generalises.

The service slider is nearly inert above the cliff. Take the service mean from 5 ms to 1 ms — a fivefold speedup of everything a profiler can see — and the p99 goes from 203.47 ms to 200.69 ms.

The cliff edge is a ceiling, not an estimate. 1% is the most forgiving this threshold ever gets. A slower service crosses at a lower fraction, because its own tail already reaches past the pause: at a 60 ms mean service the threshold is zero, since that service’s clean p99 of 276 ms already exceeds a 200 ms pause. Speeding the fast path up raises the threshold and can never raise it above 1%.

Fan-out walks you off the cliff for free. Set affected to 0.05% — a 200 ms pause every 400 seconds, which is a well-tuned runtime — and one call has a p99 of 23.28 ms. The pause is invisible. Now fan out: at k = 20 the p99 is 64.72 ms and at k = 21 it is 200.22 ms. One more backend, 3.1×. Nothing changed about the runtime.

03

The intuition: the clerk who freezes, and the till that gets reassigned

One counter, one clerk, thirty seconds a customer. Every ten minutes the clerk freezes solid for twenty seconds — eyes open, hands still — and then carries on from exactly where they were, with no sense that anything happened.

The first consequence is the queue, and it is the obvious one. Everyone standing there during the freeze waits, and so does everyone who joins while the backlog clears. If you asked “how long does the unluckiest one customer in a hundred wait”, the answer is not thirty-one seconds. It is twenty seconds plus service, because more than one customer in a hundred was in that freeze.

The second consequence is not about waiting at all. The manager, watching from the office, cannot tell a frozen clerk from a clerk who has gone home. Both look like a person who is not serving customers. So after fifteen seconds the manager does the sensible thing: reassigns the till to somebody else, who starts taking payments.

Then the clerk unfreezes. They are holding a customer’s cash, mid-transaction, and they finish it — into a till that now belongs to somebody else, on top of somebody else’s work. They did nothing wrong. The manager did nothing wrong. Nobody in the building has any way to know it happened.

A stopped process and a dead process produce the same observation: silence. Everything a system does about “is it dead?” is a guess, and the guess is correct except during exactly the interval in which the answer matters.

The fix is not a longer wait before reassigning the till. The freeze has no maximum length, so there is no wait that is long enough. The fix is that the till checks: it knows which clerk it currently belongs to, and it refuses a transaction from anyone else. That is a fencing-tokens check, and slot 6 is about why it is the only thing here that is not a bet.

04

The mechanism: one cause, two consequences, and where f comes from

Where f comes from — you already have it

The affected fraction is not a parameter you invent. Two cases, and they differ by a factor nobody expects.

A request blocked on something that pauses — a fan-out leaf, an RPC to a peer, a lock holder — is hit if the pause overlaps its lifetime. For Poisson arrivals and requests short compared with the pause, that is simply the fraction of wall-clock time the thing spends paused:

f = P / T

The pause duty cycle. It is the complement of the GC log’s throughput figure, so 98% throughput is f = 2%. The same quantity is throttled_time ÷ elapsed for a cgroup, and steal ÷ elapsed for a hypervisor. In every case it is already being measured and already being read as “overhead”.

A request queued behind a paused server is a larger count. The pause injects P of pure backlog; clearing it takes a busy period of mean length P/(1−ρ), and every arrival inside that busy period is late:

f = (P / T) ÷ (1 − ρ)

4% at ρ = 0.5, and 20% at ρ = 0.9. Measured on a simulated queue with a real stall injected: 4.17% at ρ = 0.5. This is the queueing lesson’s 1/(1−ρ) arriving through a different door — running hot does not merely add queueing delay, it widens every pause’s blast radius.

The quantile, in closed form

Model it as the mixture it is: a service time X, and with probability f an added stall of P. The distribution function is

F(x) = (1−f)·Fx(x) + f·Fx(x−P)

and the whole lesson is in the second term contributing nothing below P. For exponential service with mean m the inverse is algebraic, in two branches split at F(P):

below the stall  x = −m·ln(1 − q/(1−f))  ·  above it  x = m·ln( ((1−f) + f·eP/m) / (1−q) )

The second form is the interesting one. Whenever the stall is many service times long the bracket is dominated by f·e^(P/m), and it collapses to x ≈ P + m·ln(f/(1−q)) — the quantile is the pause, plus a correction the size of one service time. That is the algebraic statement of “the fast path is not the problem”.

Setting the quantile equal to P and solving for f gives the cliff:

the q-quantile is at least P  ⟺  f ≥ 1 − q / Fx(P)  ≈  1 − q

1% for a p99, 0.1% for a p999. And 1 − q is a ceiling on the threshold rather than an estimate of it: Fx(P) is below 1 for a slow service, which pushes the threshold lower. The dependence on how fast your code is has dropped out of the answer entirely, in the one direction that is no help to you.

And under fan-out it compounds

A user request needing all k replies waits for the slowest, so P(user latency at most x) is F(x)^k and the q-quantile of the user request is the q^(1/k)-quantile of one call. tail-at-scale derives that result; what is added here is that the per-call slow probability now has a name and a cause. It is not a mysterious 1%. It is the duty cycle of your runtime, and it compounds:

P(at least one of k is inside a pause) = 1 − (1−f)k  ·  the cliff arrives at  k ≥ ln(q) / ln(1−f)

At f = 2%: 18.29% at k = 10, 63.58% at k = 50 — which is the Tail at Scale headline figure, arrived at from the pause side. At f = 0.05% the threshold k is 21.

The second consequence, which is not about latency

Now the half that matters. A lease, a lock, a leader election and a heartbeat all rest on the same inference: this peer has not spoken, therefore it is gone. A paused process emits exactly what a crashed one emits, so that inference is wrong precisely when it is expensive, and the paused process cannot help — it has no way to learn that time passed, because the clock it would ask was not being read during the interval it needs to ask about.

So compute P(a pause outlives the lease) — the survival function of the pause length at the lease TTL. At a 200 ms mean pause and a 10-second lease, on a power-law tail:

P(pause > lease) = 1 × 10−4  ·  pauses per year = 3,153,600  ·  expected revocations = 315

One in ten thousand, which is the form in which everybody dismisses it, and 315 a year, which is the form in which nobody does. The first one arrives after 27.8 hours. Note this is a different quantity from the corruption rate in fencing-tokens, which averages over the lease remaining and comes out around E[pause]/L — some two hundred times larger at these settings. This one is the one that inverts.

Read the last two columns together, because that pair is the argument. To make lease-loss-under-pause a once-a-century event you need a lease of half an hour, which means half of that — fifteen minutes — of everything queued behind a holder that genuinely died. And the number is a guess: the same measured mean pause says 3.9 seconds if the tail is exponential and 50 minutes if it is lognormal, and you cannot tell which you have, because the instrument that would tell you is the process that stops.

That is why the fix is not a bigger timeout. A pause can exceed any finite timeout, so every choice of lease is a bet on a distribution you cannot see, and the only question is what odds you accepted without noticing. fencing-tokens replaces the bet with an integer comparison at the resource: the resource keeps the highest token it has accepted and refuses anything lower, which makes the corruption rate exactly zero at every lease, every tail and every rate. Then you set the lease by recovery speed alone, which is the only axis left, and it argues for a shorter one.

06

The trade-off: what the fixes cost, and the part that cannot be computed

Four levers, and they are not interchangeable.

Shorten the pause. A concurrent collector — ZGC, Shenandoah, Go’s — trades throughput and footprint for pause length, and it moves the floor, which is the only thing that helps once you are above the cliff. It also helps the correctness half enormously, because a shorter mean pause moves the whole survival curve. What it does not do is reach zero, and the correctness argument only needs one pause.

Reduce f. Pausing half as often halves the duty cycle, and below the cliff that is the whole game. Above the cliff, as the table shows, an eighteenfold reduction in f buys 7% of the p99. Know which side you are on before you spend a quarter on it.

Reduce the fan-out. k is in the exponent, so this is usually the cheapest lever available and it is a design change rather than a performance grind — batch, denormalise, cache an aggregate, return partial results. This is tail-at-scale’s conclusion and it holds here for a sharper reason: reducing k is the only lever that works on a runtime you do not control.

Run cooler. Because the blast radius is f/(1−ρ), the utilisation you chose is also a pause-severity setting. Going from ρ = 0.9 to ρ = 0.5 cuts the affected fraction fivefold. That is the queueing lesson’s bill arriving a second time, and it is the same currency: idle capacity, which is money.

And the one that is not a lever at all: you cannot fix this by measuring more carefully. A load generator that waits for a response before sending the next request stops sending during the pause, so the requests the pause would have delayed were never issued and never appear in the histogram. Gil Tene named this coordinated omission, and its effect is to make f look smaller than it is — which, given that the entire behaviour of the p99 turns on whether f is above or below 1%, means the measurement error lands exactly on the quantity that decides the answer.

What cannot be computed

Three things, stated plainly, because the honest boundary of this model is part of it.

The tail. Every figure in the correctness table is a function of the pause distribution’s shape past the lease, and that region contains almost no samples by construction. Four orders of magnitude separate the exponential row from the lognormal one at identical means. This is not a modelling shortcut that better tooling removes; the events are rare because they are rare.

The closed form overstates the queued p99, and by how much is measured rather than derived. The formula inserts the whole pause into every affected request, which is what happens to a request in flight when the world stops, or to a caller blocked on an RPC to a process that stops. A request that arrives while the stall is already in progress absorbs only the residual. Simulated on the kernel at ρ = 0.5 with a real stall injected: the measured p99 is 172.7 ms against the formula’s 206.9 ms, so the formula is 20% pessimistic there — while at the p999 it reads 218.4 ms against a measured 220.6 ms, agreeing to 1%, because the p999 is deep enough into the affected population that the full pause plus drain is what actually happened. Two errors in opposite directions, which is exactly the situation in which a formula should be labelled rather than trusted. The direction that matters is unaffected either way: measured against the same queue with the stalls switched off, the p99 goes from 46.4 ms to 172.7 ms, which is 3.7×.

Whether your pauses are independent across machines. Every fan-out number above assumes k independent draws. Fleet-wide synchronised events — a deploy, a config push, a cron-triggered compaction, ten thousand JVMs started at the same instant and reaching their first full GC together — break that assumption in the expensive direction, and there is no way to compute the correlation from per-machine metrics. correlated-failure is that argument; treat every figure on this page as a lower bound on the cost.

07

In the wild

HBase calls it the Juliet pause, after the tomb scene, and the name is the clearest statement of the mechanism in the industry. A RegionServer’s JVM pauses for longer than its ZooKeeper session timeout; ZooKeeper expires the session, the master concludes the server is dead and reassigns its regions to another server, which starts serving them. Then the RegionServer wakes up believing it still owns those regions. HBase’s operational guidance has long treated this as a tuning problem — raise the session timeout, tune the collector — and the durable fix is the one in slot 5: the write path checks a generation, so a resurrected server’s writes are refused rather than being merely rare.

Kafka fences a paused consumer and a paused leader, in two different ways. A consumer that does not call poll within max.poll.interval.ms — a long pause counts — is removed from its group and its partitions are rebalanced away, and the group’s generation id is what stops the returning consumer from committing offsets it no longer owns. On the broker side the leader epoch exists specifically so that a stale leader’s appends are rejected instead of diverging the log. Both are fencing tokens, under other names, and both were added because the pause happened.

Kubernetes does this to you at two levels at once. A node whose kubelet stops reporting for longer than the node-monitor grace period is marked NotReady and its pods are evicted — a lease revocation, triggered by silence, with the same inference in it. Separately and more commonly, a container with a CPU limit is throttled by the CFS bandwidth controller: once the quota for the current 100 ms period is spent, every thread is descheduled until the next period begins. That is a stop-the-world pause with P up to 100 ms, arriving at up to ten times a second, and its duty cycle is sitting in throttled_time in the cgroup’s cpu.stat. It produces exactly the p99 in slot 1 in a runtime with no garbage collector at all, which is the strongest evidence that this is not a GC lesson.

Dean and Barroso list it first. The Tail at Scale (CACM 56(2), 2013) names background daemons, shared resource contention, maintenance activities and garbage collection as the sources of component variability that fan-out amplifies. This page is the arithmetic for one of those entries: the 1% slow rate that paper multiplies is, very often, a duty cycle you could have read off a log.

Gil Tene’s “How NOT to Measure Latency” is the measurement half, and HdrHistogram exists partly because of it. Two results carry over directly: coordinated omission systematically hides pauses from the histogram, and reporting a mean or a p99 of a service whose pauses are correlated with load tells you very little about what a user experienced. If your monitoring says the p99 is fine and users disagree, f is the first thing to compute and the histogram is the last thing to trust.

And Kleppmann’s process-pause section is the canonical treatment of the correctness half — a JVM stopped for minutes, a virtual machine suspended and resumed, an operator with SIGSTOP, a laptop lid closing mid-transaction. Its conclusion is this page’s: a node cannot know how long it was gone, so safety cannot rest on any node’s belief about elapsed time.

Model and numbers — every figure on this page is computed by src/sim/pauses.mjs and asserted in src/sim/pauses.test.mjs (22 tests). The closed-form mixture CDF and quantile are graded against a generative Monte Carlo that draws service times from the kernel’s own dist.exp and adds the stall with probability f, so it knows nothing of the algebra; the cliff is confirmed by two runs of 2,000,000 samples either side of 1%; the fan-out quantile against a Monte Carlo of the slowest of k; the f/(1−ρ) blast radius against a discrete-event simulation of a real FIFO queue on the kernel with a stall injected, which counts requests rather than deriving busy periods; and P(pause > lease) against a Monte Carlo of the pause samples, which is a separately written expression from the survival function it grades. Degenerate cases — f = 0, f = 1, and a zero-cost service — are checked against answers written by hand.

  • For requests queued behind the paused server the affected fraction is the duty cycle divided by (1−ρ), because the injected backlog has to drain
  • The q-quantile of the mixture is at least the pause length as soon as the affected fraction reaches 1−q, so f ≥ 1% pins the p99 at P and f ≥ 0.1% pins the p999
  • 1−q is a ceiling on that threshold, not an estimate: a slower service crosses at even lower f, and speeding the fast path up can never push the threshold above 1−q
  • Above the cliff the fast path is 1.7% of the p99, so optimising all of the unaffected 98% cannot move the number
  • Under fan-out the per-call pause probability compounds to 1−(1−f)^k, so the cliff arrives at k ≥ ln(q)/ln(1−f) — 21 backends at a 0.05% pause rate
  • A paused process emits exactly what a crashed one emits, silence, so its lease is revoked while it still believes it holds it
  • P(a pause outlives the lease) is small per pause and a certainty per year, and inverting it for a tolerable rate demands a lease of tens of minutes
  • No finite timeout closes it, because the pause length has no bound — the fix is a fencing token at the resource, not a bigger timeout

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A service has a 5 ms mean response time and a 23 ms p99. Its GC log reports 98% throughput — one 200 ms pause every 10 seconds. What is the p99 now, and why is profiling the request path the wrong next step?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    203.5 ms. 98% throughput means a 2% pause duty cycle, so f = 2% of requests absorb the stall — and 2% is larger than the 1% that sits above the p99, so the entire p99 has moved into the stalled population. Profiling is the wrong step because the p99 decomposes into 200 ms of pause and 3.47 ms of request: making the request path infinitely fast leaves the p99 at exactly 200 ms, a 1.7% improvement. The number to attack is the pause, or the fraction, or the fan-out — never the code that runs between pauses.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why is the p99 as a function of the affected fraction a cliff rather than a slope, and where is the edge?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a quantile is a position in a sorted list, and the stalled requests form a block sitting entirely above the pause length. The p99 asks for the boundary of the slowest 1%. While the stalled block is smaller than 1% the boundary lies inside the fast body; the moment the block is larger than 1% the boundary lies inside the block, and the answer jumps by the whole pause. So the edge is at f = 1−q: 1% for a p99, 0.1% for a p999. Measured either side: f = 0.9% gives 34.5 ms, f = 1.1% gives 200.5 ms — 5.8× from two tenths of a percentage point.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your runtime pauses 200 ms every 400 s — a 0.05% duty cycle, and a single call has a 23 ms p99. You fan a user request out to 21 backends instead of 20. What happens?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The p99 goes from 64.7 ms to 200.2 ms, 3.1×, from one extra backend. The user waits for the slowest of k, so the pause probability compounds as 1 − (1−f)^k: at k = 20 that is 0.995% and at k = 21 it is 1.045%. Twenty-one is where it crosses 1% and the fan-out's p99 falls into the stalled block. The general form is k ≥ ln(q)/ln(1−f), and it means a pause rate that is genuinely invisible in a single service has a fan-out at which it becomes the whole p99.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    The same pause is running on a server that is 50% utilised, and requests queue behind it rather than each absorbing it in flight. Does that make the blast radius bigger or smaller?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Bigger, by 1/(1−ρ). The pause injects 200 ms of pure backlog; clearing it takes a busy period of mean length P/(1−ρ), and every arrival inside that busy period is late. So the affected fraction is the duty cycle divided by (1−ρ) — 4% at ρ = 0.5, 20% at ρ = 0.9. Measured on a simulated queue: 4.17% at ρ = 0.5. This is the queueing lesson's 1/(1−ρ) arriving through a different door, and it means the same collector is twice as damaging on a server you decided to run hot.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Why is a paused process a correctness problem and not only a latency problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a paused process emits exactly what a crashed one emits: silence, and no observer can tell them apart. So the peers do the correct thing — the lease expires, they grant it to somebody else, that holder does the work. Then the paused process resumes, having observed no passage of time, still holding a variable that says it owns the lease, and completes a write it is no longer entitled to make. Every component behaved to specification and the result is a value older than the value it replaced. The pause is not detectable from inside; that is the whole difficulty.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Your pauses average 200 ms and you hold a 10-second lease. Talk somebody out of "just make the lease longer".

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Per pause it is reassuring — 1 in 10,000 on a power-law tail. But at one pause every 10 seconds that is 3,153,600 pauses a year, so 315 lease revocations under pause per year, the first after 27.8 hours. Now invert it: for one every hundred years you need a lease of 29.6 minutes, which costs 14.8 minutes of everything blocked behind a holder that really did crash. And that figure is a guess, because it is set entirely by the tail: the same 200 ms mean gives 3.9 seconds on an exponential tail and 50 minutes on a lognormal one. No finite lease works, because the pause length has no bound. The fix is a fencing-tokens check at the resource.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team runs a Node service — single-threaded, no garbage-collection pauses worth mentioning — in Kubernetes with a CPU limit of 500 millicores. The container's cgroup reports throttling for 35 ms in each 100 ms period. The p99 has quadrupled and no profile explains it. What is happening, what is f, and what is the one-line fix?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is the same mechanism with a different cause. The CFS quota is exhausted 35 ms into each 100 ms period, so the process is descheduled for the remaining 65 ms and cannot observe it — a stop-the-world pause with P = 65 ms arriving every 100 ms. That is a duty cycle of 65%, which is far past the 1% cliff, so the p99 is the throttle, and it is also past the p50 cliff at 50%: the median request is now a throttled one. No profiler will show it, because from inside the process no time passed.

    The one-line fix is to remove the CPU limit (keep the request, which is the scheduling weight) or raise it above the service's actual demand. And the general lesson is the one this page is built on: the pause is not always a garbage collector, and the quantity to look for is never a slow function — it is throttled_time, or the GC log's throughput figure, or the hypervisor's steal percentage. Each of those is f, and f is the only number that decides whether your p99 is your code.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a participant is absent for an interval it cannot perceive, so it re-enters the system holding an authority the system has already reassigned — and because the absence has no upper bound, no waiting period fixes it; only a check at the point of effect does. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The abstraction is unobservable absence plus reassigned authority, and the tell is that the returning participant is acting in good faith on stale information it has no way to know is stale.

    An anaesthetised surgeon's patient. Closer to the point: a controller in an air traffic tower who blacks out for ninety seconds. The handover procedure is correct, a colleague takes the sector, and the danger is entirely in the moment the first controller comes round and issues an instruction — which is why the fix is not a longer blackout tolerance but a check at the aircraft: an instruction is only valid from the controller currently on frequency.

    A ship's captain unconscious below decks while the first mate assumes command under a standing order. An executor of a will who is out of contact when the court appoints a replacement, and then executes a sale. A power of attorney that has been revoked while the attorney was in the air. A cheque written before a joint account was frozen and presented after. In every case a waiting period is the wrong instrument, because the absence has no maximum, and the working instrument is the same one: the bank, the aircraft, the registry — the place where the action takes effect — refuses anything that does not carry the current generation of authority.

    And notice which half people always try to fix. The instinct is to make the absence shorter or the tolerance longer, and both are bets against a distribution with no bound. The check at the point of effect is not a bet.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
