Tier 8 · Architecture and patterns When to split explained well nowhere else

Nine services at three nines each is down seventy-eight hours a year

The decision to split one process into several is made almost entirely with adjectives, and it goes wrong for a structural reason: the costs of splitting are measurable and the benefits mostly are not. So the costs get computed, the benefits get asserted, and whoever speaks last wins. This lesson computes the costs precisely — availability, latency, amplification — and then states the benefits honestly, which means admitting that exactly one of the four usual ones produces a number.

Read first — latency-numberspercentilesslos

01

The proposal, and the number nobody put on it

A team has one service that does nine things. They propose splitting it into nine services, one per thing. Each new service will be three nines — 99.9%, which is genuinely good and better than most services manage. A request needs all nine.

The engineering discussion that follows will be about boundaries, ownership, repository layout and whether to use a message bus. It will not contain this:

0.999⁹ = 99.104%. That is 8,964 failed requests per million, and 4,712 minutes — just over 78 hours — of downtime a year. The service they are replacing was down for 8 hours 45 minutes.

Nobody hid this. It is invisible for a mundane reason: 0.999 and 0.99104 look the same. Written as a fraction the cost of the entire decomposition disappears into the fourth decimal place, and every dashboard, every SLO document and every architecture review renders it as a fraction.

The second number, which is the one that ends the conversation: to hold three nines across nine services, each service must be 99.98888% — 58 minutes of downtime a year, each, including deploys.

02

The obvious fix — make each service reliable — and what it demands

The answer to slot 1 is always the same, and it is not unreasonable: we’ll invest in reliability, so each service will be better than three nines. Turn it into a number and it stops being an investment and becomes an arithmetic impossibility.

Three-nines services cannot compose into a three-nines system at all. The largest chain that meets a target is k ≤ ln(target)/ln(a), which at a = target = 0.999 is exactly 1. You get one service. The second one has already spent the whole budget, and there is no amount of reliability engineering that changes the shape of the product.

Predict Twenty services, and the whole request must hit four nines (99.99%). How reliable must each service be, expressed as downtime per year?
Availability composes as a product, so the requirement per service is target^(1/k) = 0.9999^(1/20) = 99.9995%, which is 2.63 minutes a year. Not 52.6/20 — the reciprocal power, not a division. And nothing cancels: there is no mechanism by which one service being down offsets another being down, because the request needed both. 2.6 minutes a year is less than one bad deploy, so a twenty-service four-nines request is not a reliability programme, it is a claim that must be withdrawn or an SLO that must be lowered.
Show it without answering

The lever the rig exposes is the one absent from almost every real decomposition. An optional dependency does not enter the product at all. Twenty services with eight of them optional composes as a chain of twelve: 98.807% instead of 98.019%, which is a 40% reduction in failed requests bought with no latency cost, no extra hardware, and no reliability investment. It is the only lever in this lesson that is free.

Almost nobody makes new dependencies optional when they split, which is why a^k with nothing optional is the formula that describes real deployments rather than a pessimistic bound.

03

The intuition: nine signatures

A document needs nine approvals. Each approver is away from their desk one working day in a thousand — genuinely reliable people.

The document is blocked nine days in a thousand.

Now watch the two responses. The first is we will hold approvers to a higher standard. Work out what that demands: for the document to be available 999 days in 1,000, each approver must be away less than one day in nine thousand — about thirty-five years of unbroken attendance. That is not a standard, it is a refusal to accept the arithmetic.

The second response is the one that works, and it is not about approvers at all: decide that six of the nine signatures are advisory. The document proceeds without them; they are recorded as notes. The chain is now three, the document is blocked three days in a thousand, and nobody’s behaviour changed.

Two things carry over exactly. Making a participant better fights a product and loses. Making a participant optional removes a term, and removing a term is free. And notice which of the two is a technical decision — neither. The second is a decision about what the document is for, which is why it never gets made in the architecture review either.

04

The mechanism: three costs, and the composition rule for each

Availability: a product, and it does not care about your topology

A request needing all k services succeeds a^k of the time. This holds whether the calls are serial or parallel, which is worth stating because the confusion is common and consequential: parallelism buys latency, never reliability. Issuing eleven calls simultaneously does not change which eleven must succeed. tail-at-scale is the parallel-fanout result for slowness; this is the same multiplication for failure, and unlike the latency result it needs no topology at all.

Three forms, and the second is the one to carry into a meeting:

availability     a^k
required per     a = target^(1/k)          ← the useful direction
largest chain    k ≤ ln(target)/ln(a)      ← one number to remember

Call amplification: the latency bill is the depth, the availability bill is the node count

A request that fans out to b children per level for depth levels touches 1 + b + b² + … services. At b = 4, depth = 3 that is 85 services and three hops on the critical path.

Those two costs scale differently, and that difference is the entire reason fan-out feels cheap:

  • Critical path: 3 hops × 0.5 ms = 1.5 ms. This is what the user experiences.
  • Aggregate network time: 84 calls × 0.5 ms = 42 ms, which is 28× the critical path. This is a capacity cost, paid by the fleet, invisible in any latency graph.
  • Availability: 0.999^85 = 91.85%.

Compare a four-service chain of the same depth. Identical critical path, and 7.75 percentage points more availability. You experience the depth and you pay for the nodes.

Latency: means add, and variances add — so quantiles do neither

This is the composition that gets done wrong most often, in both directions.

A network call costs about 5,000× the same call in-process — one datacentre round trip against one main-memory reference. But the ratio that decides anything is the hop’s CPU as a fraction of the request’s own work: 0.56% of an 18 ms request and 20% of a 500 µs one, a 36-fold difference for an identical hop. Splitting out a component that barely computes is the expensive case.

For the mean, the decomposition is exact and unremarkable:

At the p99 the same arithmetic does not work, and the two shortcuts people substitute are wrong in opposite directions. With eight hops at a realistic hop variance:

valueerror
worst single hop p99 — “our slowest dependency is 9 ms”9.14 msunderstates by 3.7×
the truth, measured34.0 ms—
sum of the hop p99s — “9.1 × 8”73.1 msoverstates by 2.15×

The reason is that means add linearly and variances add linearly, so the standard deviation — and with it the distance from mean to p99 — grows like √k while the sum of the p99s grows like k. The gap therefore widens with the chain: the sum overstates by 8.6% at two hops, 2.15× at eight, and 4.56× at thirty-two.

Of the two errors, quoting the sum is the more dangerous, because it makes splitting look worse than it is and gets a correct decision rejected for a wrong reason.

And “sum the p99s” is not a conservative bound. It is not a bound.

This one is stated as fact in a great deal of design writing, and the counterexample is two lines.

Take a hop that is 0 ms with probability 99.2% and 100 ms otherwise. Its own p99 is 0, because the spike lives inside the top 1%. So the sum of two such p99s is 0. But two of those hops in series spike 1 − 0.992² = 1.59% of the time, which is above 1% — so the chain’s p99 is 100 ms.

The sum of the p99s is below the p99 of the sum. Any distribution with a rare enough spike does this, and rare enough spikes are precisely what a shared database, a GC pause or a cold cache produce. There is no safe direction to be sloppy in; there is only measuring the composition.

05

The numbers: at what scale does each cost bite

The three costs arrive at different scales, which is why a decomposition that is fine at three services becomes untenable at thirty without anything visibly changing.

services in the requestavailability at 3 nines eachdown per yearp99 added by hops

399.700%26.3h16.8 ms
999.104%78.5h34.0 ms

4096.077%344h70.9 ms

Read the two columns against each other. Availability degrades roughly linearly in k — each service adds about the same 1,000 failures per million. Latency degrades like √k — going from 9 to 40 services only about doubles the added p99, from 34.0 ms to 70.9 ms. So the binding constraint at small k is latency and at large k it is availability, and a team that optimised the hops will be surprised by which wall they hit.

One more scale effect, from the amplification result: at 40 services in a chain the aggregate network cost equals the critical-path cost, and at 85 services in a tree the aggregate is 28× the critical path. Fleet cost and user-visible latency diverge as the shape widens, which means a latency graph stops being a proxy for a bill.

06

The trade-off: the costs have numbers and the benefits mostly do not

Everything above is a cost, and it would be dishonest to leave the lesson there — plenty of decompositions are correct. But the asymmetry is real and it is the thing to take away:

benefit usually offeredis there a number?what can actually be said
independent scalingyesA break-even capacity ratio, below
independent deployabilitynoMeasurable after the split. Before it, a claim about what the release process would become
team autonomynoConway’s law says the architecture matches the communication structure regardless. Whether a boundary reduces coordination is an organisational prediction
blast-radius isolationno, and it is often negativeOnly real if the new dependency is optional. If it is required, splitting enlarges the blast radius by exactly a^k

One of four. That asymmetry, and not any individual number, is why this decision goes wrong so reliably: the costs get computed, the benefits get asserted, and a figure compared against an argument goes the way of whoever speaks last. The fix is not to pretend the arguments are numbers. It is to write them down as arguments, so that both sides of the comparison are visible and the decision is made knowingly.

Now the one that does have a number, and it came out lower than expected. A deployable scales as a unit, so its replica count is set by its hottest component — and every replica carries the resident footprint of everything else in the binary. If the hot component needs r times the replicas of the cold one, keeping them together buys r copies of the cold component’s footprint and uses one.

Splitting pays on capacity above a break-even ratio of (1+h)/(1 − h·f_hot/f_cold), where h is the hop’s CPU overhead as a fraction of the request’s work. With symmetric footprints that is (1+h)/(1−h):

  • at a 10% hop overhead, splitting pays from a capacity ratio of 1.22 upward
  • at a 1% hop overhead, from 1.02
  • at a 50% hop overhead, from 3.0
  • and when h ≥ f_cold/f_hot — the hop costs more compute than the component does — the break-even is infinite. No capacity ratio ever justifies that split. Which is the distributed monolith, arrived at from the capacity side rather than the change side. See the-distributed-monolith.

At a ratio of 10 and a 10% hop, splitting costs 6.05 replica-units against 10 together — a 39.5% saving, because the monolith was carrying 4.5 copies of the cold component nobody used. On capacity grounds the bar for splitting is low, and that is a genuine finding rather than a concession: two components that differ by more than about 1.2× in what they need are already worth separating.

So the honest summary is not “don’t split”. It is: the availability cost is exact and usually ignored, the latency cost is computable and usually mis-computed, the capacity benefit is exact and usually under-claimed, and the three benefits people lead with cannot be computed at all. Say which kind of claim you are making.

One assumption underpins every figure above and cuts the same way each time: independence. Real hops share causes — one database, one network fabric, one bad deploy, one synchronised garbage collection — and positive correlation moves a chain’s p99 up, toward the sum of the per-hop p99s, and moves a^k down, because the failures stop being independent events. Every number here is therefore a lower bound on the cost of splitting, never an upper one.

07

In the wild

The a^k arithmetic is standard and routinely skipped. Google’s Site Reliability Engineering (2016) makes the point that a service’s availability target is bounded by its dependencies’ and that the budget must be allocated across them rather than promised independently by each. The allocation is the target^(1/k) calculation above, and the reason it is skipped is the encoding problem in slot 1 — in fractional form the cost is not visible.

Dean and Barroso’s The Tail at Scale (CACM, 2013) is the companion result for latency under fan-out and is worth reading alongside this: it establishes that at high fan-out the slowest component decides the user’s latency, which is the maximum rather than a product. Holding both results at once is the whole skill — fan-out composes latency as a maximum and availability as a product, and mixing up which rule applies is the single most common error in this area.

Amazon’s service-oriented mandate (circa 2002) is the best-documented large decomposition, and the part usually left out of the retelling is that it came with the operational apparatus to survive a^k: hard service boundaries, but also the expectation that every team runs what it builds. The mandate is frequently cited as evidence that splitting works; it is better read as evidence of what splitting costs, since the organisation had to be rebuilt around it.

Netflix’s practice of degrading rather than failing is the optional-dependency lever applied deliberately at scale: a personalised row that cannot be computed is replaced by a non-personalised one, so the recommendation service leaves the availability product for that request. This is the only free lever in the lesson and Netflix’s engineering writing on fallbacks is the clearest public account of treating it as a design requirement rather than an error path.

Martin Fowler’s MonolithFirst (2015) is the process conclusion that follows from all of the above: drawing boundaries correctly requires knowing what changes together, and you learn that by building the thing — so the costs computed here are paid up front while the benefits are conditional on information you do not yet have.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A request that needs all k services succeeds a^k of the time, and this holds whether the calls are serial or parallel — availability composition does not need a topology
  • The useful direction is the inverse: each service must be target^(1/k), which converts "we'll make each service reliable" into a downtime budget you can compare against what is purchasable
  • Three-nines services cannot compose into a three-nines system at all — the budget allows exactly one, and the second has already spent it
  • An optional dependency leaves the availability product entirely, and it is the only lever that costs no latency — which is why almost nobody pulls it
  • In a call tree the latency bill is the depth and the availability bill is the node count, and the node count is exponential in the depth
  • Means add and variances add, so a chain's p99 grows like the square root of the hop count while the sum of the hop p99s grows linearly
  • Summing per-hop p99s is not a conservative upper bound — it is not a bound at all, and there is a two-line counterexample

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  3. Cued recall

    Your chain has eight network hops. The slowest hop's p99 is 9.1 ms. Someone says the chain adds "about 9 ms, worst case" and someone else says "9.1 × 8 = 73 ms". What is it actually, and why are both wrong?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    34.0 ms. Both shortcuts fail because they use the wrong composition rule. Means add and variances add, so the chain's spread grows like √k while the sum of the p99s grows like k — the truth is therefore strictly between the two and closer to the bottom, at 3.7× the worst single hop and 47% of the sum.   5. Cued recall

    A request fans out to four services, each of which calls four more. What does that cost, and which of the costs is the one nobody draws?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Eighty-five services, three hops on the critical path. The latency bill is the depth — 1.5 ms of network on the critical path — and the availability bill is the node count: 0.999^85 = 91.85%.   6. Near transfer

    A frontend calls a GraphQL gateway that resolves eleven fields, each from a different backend, all in parallel. The team argues that because the calls are parallel the availability cost is the availability of the slowest service rather than a product. Are they right, and what would make them right?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    They are wrong about availability and right about latency, and they have swapped the two rules. Latency under parallel fanout is the maximum, which is the tail-at-scale result and the reason parallel fanout has a tail problem rather than a mean problem. Availability is the product regardless of topology, because the request needs every field to return: 0.999^11 = 98.91%, or 96 hours a year. Nothing about issuing the calls simultaneously changes which of them must succeed.

    What would make them right is the lever this lesson says nobody pulls: make fields optional. A GraphQL response that can return null for a field with an error entry, and a UI that renders without it, removes that backend from the product entirely — eleven fields with seven optional composes as 0.999^4, which is 99.6%. That is a 2.7-fold reduction in failed requests bought with no latency cost and no extra hardware, and it is a product decision disguised as a schema decision. It is also the honest version of "blast-radius isolation", which is only a real benefit when the new dependency is optional; when it is required, splitting enlarges the blast radius by exactly a^k.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a task is divided among several independent participants, all of whom must succeed for the task to succeed — so reliability multiplies downward while the work is merely rearranged, and the only escape is to make a participant's contribution optional rather than to make the participant better. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A document requiring nine signatures. Each approver is unavailable one working day in a thousand — excellent, individually. The document is blocked nine days in a thousand, and the standard response, "we will hold approvers to a higher standard", is the target^(1/k) demand: to get the document to three nines each approver must now be unavailable less than one day in nine thousand, which is roughly thirty-five years of perfect attendance. The escape is not better approvers. It is deciding that six of the nine signatures are advisory, which removes them from the product for free — and it is a governance decision, not an availability one, which is exactly why the technical version of it never gets made either.

    Other instances: a relay race, where the team's chance of finishing is the product over four runners and the baton exchange is a network hop that adds its own independent variance — which is why relay times are worse than the sum of the individual bests. A supply chain of single-sourced components, where a manufacturer with nine sole suppliers at 99.9% each has a production line that stops 78 hours a year, and dual-sourcing is precisely the "optional dependency" fix. And a proof depending on nine unproved lemmas, where the reader's confidence is a product and the fix is to weaken the theorem until it needs fewer of them.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
