Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Why averages lie explained well nowhere else

Why the average response time describes almost nobody

Your dashboard says 26 milliseconds and it is not lying. Four requests in five are faster than that number, one in a thousand takes three hundred, and the figure on the dashboard is not a percentile of anything. Then somebody averages the p99s across ten machines and the last honest number on the page is gone too.

Read first — queueing

01

Two people look at the same service and disagree about whether it is fast

You run one API endpoint. It reads from a cache, and when the cache misses it does real work. Concretely: 95 requests in 100 are served from cache in 5 milliseconds, and 5 in 100 miss and take 120 milliseconds. Traffic is light — the server is busy about 30% of the time.

Your dashboard shows one number for response time: 26 milliseconds. That is correct. It is the arithmetic mean, computed exactly as advertised.

Here is what else is true about that same traffic, measured from the same requests:

  • Half of all requests finish in 5 milliseconds.
  • 79% of requests are faster than the “average” request.
  • One request in a hundred takes just under 200 milliseconds.
  • One request in a thousand takes about 300 milliseconds — about twelve times the average and sixty times the median.

So when support forwards you a complaint that the page takes a third of a second, you look at the dashboard, see 26 milliseconds, and conclude the customer is wrong or their network is bad. They are not wrong. They are one of the 5% who missed the cache, at a moment when two other requests were already queued in front of them, and your monitoring has no number on it that describes their experience at all.

Then it gets worse in a way that is harder to notice. You have ten application servers. Each reports its own p99 to the metrics system. The dashboard shows the p99 across the fleet by averaging those ten numbers. That value is not the p99 of anything. It is not even an approximation of the p99. Later on this page there is a worked example where the averaged figure reads 144 milliseconds, the true answer is 60 milliseconds, and the real damage — 900 milliseconds for 1% of all traffic — appears in neither.

02

The obvious fix: alert on average latency. Watch it never fire.

The natural response is to put a threshold on the number you already have. “Page me if average response time goes over 100 milliseconds.” It is one line of config, everybody does it, and it does not work.

03

The intuition: an average is a single number pretending to be a crowd

Put ten people in a room. Nine of them earn 30,000 a year and one earns 3 million. The average income in the room is 327,000. Every sentence you could write using that number is false about all ten people.

Nobody is fooled by that example, because the shape of the data is obvious. Response times have exactly the same shape and it is not obvious, because you never see the individual requests — you see a chart of one number per minute.

The reason latency has that shape is not exotic. It is two ordinary things stacking up:

  • Requests are not all the same amount of work. A cache hit and a cache miss are different jobs that happen to arrive through the same endpoint. So are a user with 4 orders and a user with 40,000.
  • Queueing adds delay unevenly. From the previous lesson: at 70% load the worker is idle a third of the time, and yet requests that arrive during a burst wait behind several others. The wait is not spread evenly across requests. Most get none of it and a few get all of it.

An average tells you the total divided by the count. A percentile tells you what somebody actually experienced. Only one of those is a promise you can make to a user.

04

The mechanism: what a percentile is, and three things a mean is not

Sort every response time from fastest to slowest. The pth percentile is the value at the point p% of the way through that sorted list. If the 99th percentile is 300 milliseconds, then 99 requests in 100 finished in 300 milliseconds or less, and 1 in 100 took longer. The 50th percentile is the median — the halfway point. The usual shorthand is p50, p95, p99, and p999 for the 99.9th.

The mean is the sum of all response times divided by how many there were. That is a different kind of quantity from a percentile, and the difference matters in three ways.

The mean is not a percentile

There is no rule fixing where the mean sits in the sorted list. It goes wherever the slow requests drag it. In the rig at 30% load it sits at the 79th percentile: the “average” request is slower than four requests in five. Add a handful of very slow requests and the mean moves; add them to the fast half and the median does not move at all. That insensitivity is what makes the median useful and the mean fragile.

The tail is a fixed multiple of the mean, even in the friendliest case

It is tempting to assume the tail is only bad because your service is badly behaved. It is not. Take the most benevolent model in queueing theory — one server, random arrivals, random service times, what is called M/M/1 — and the time a request spends in the system is exponentially distributed. For that distribution every percentile is an exact multiple of the mean, and the multiple does not depend on load at all:

q(p) = mean × ln(1 / (1 − p/100))

So p50 = 0.69 × the mean, p99 = 4.61 × the mean, p999 = 6.91 × the mean — at any utilisation. And the mean itself sits at the 63.2nd percentile, never the 50th.

Read that again: in the best-behaved system anyone models, the slowest 1 in 1,000 requests takes seven times the average, and the average is already slower than 63% of requests. Nothing is broken. That is just what the distribution is. Press Use exponential service in the rig and the carets under the axis are those four multiples, computed from the formula and drawn beside what the simulation measured.

Real services are worse than this, because their service times are not exponential — they are lumpy, like the cache-hit-or-miss mixture the rig defaults to. In that mode the p999 is 12 times the mean rather than 7.

And you cannot average them

This is the mistake that quietly destroys a monitoring stack. Percentiles do not add and they do not average. Here is an exact worked example — ten shards, a thousand requests each, no simulation involved, all of it plain counting:

ShardRequestsTheir response timesThat shard’s own p99
1–9, healthy1,000 each980 at 10ms, 20 at 60ms60ms
10, hot partition1,000900 at 10ms, 100 at 900ms900ms

Now pool all 10,000 requests: 9,720 of them took 10ms, 180 took 60ms, and 100 took 900ms.

Not one request in that data set took 144 milliseconds. The averaged figure is 2.4 times the truth, and it still fails to surface the real problem, because the hot shard’s damage is 1% of total traffic and therefore lives at the p999, not the p99.

The error is not even reliably in one direction. Average a small quiet group (1,000 requests, all 10ms, p99 = 10ms) with a large busy one (100,000 requests, 98% at 20ms and 2% at 800ms, p99 = 800ms) and the average of the two p99s is 405ms while the pooled truth is 800ms. This time the dashboard reads half the real figure.

The fix is the one DDIA states outright: keep histograms, not percentiles, and add the histograms. Bucket counts are additive — a bucket that saw 40 requests on one machine and 60 on another saw 100 requests — so you can combine them across machines and across time windows and then read the percentile off the total. This is what HdrHistogram, t-digest, DDSketch and OpenHistogram exist to do, and it is why a Prometheus histogram and a Prometheus summary are not interchangeable: only one of them can be aggregated.

05

The numbers: which percentile, and when the distinction starts to bite

PercentileAffectsTells you
meannobody in particularthroughput and capacity; useful for L = λW, useless as a user promise
p50half your requestswhat a typical request feels like
p951 request in 20a user who makes twenty requests expects to hit it about once
p991 request in 100the standard SLO percentile; where load and variance first show up
p9991 request in 1,000with fan-out, most of your users. See the next lesson.
p99991 request in 10,000dominated by events you do not control; Amazon stopped here deliberately

The mean is not useless — it is the right input to Little’s Law and to any capacity or cost calculation, because those genuinely care about totals. It is the wrong thing to put in an SLO or an alert.

When does the gap between mean and tail start to matter?

  • From the very first request, if your work is lumpy. The rig at 30% load — a service nobody would call loaded — already has a mean at the 79th percentile. Cache hit versus miss, small account versus large, cheap query versus full scan: any of these is enough.
  • Above roughly 50% utilisation, always. Queueing delay is unevenly distributed by construction, so it widens the distribution faster than it moves the mean.
  • At any fan-out above about 10. If one user action touches 10 backends, the user waits for the slowest of the 10, which lands around each backend’s p93. At 100 backends it is the p99.3. That is the next lesson and it is the single strongest argument for caring about the tail.

One number worth internalising for measurement, not for design: to estimate a p999 you need a lot of requests. A percentile is estimated from the observations above it, so a p999 computed over 3,000 requests rests on 3 of them and is noise. A service doing 50 requests per second produces 3,000 requests per minute — so a one-minute p999 on that service is not a measurement. The rig samples 60,000 requests and its p999 still wobbles a few percent either side of the exact answer; you can see it move against the caret. That noise is not a bug in the rig. It is the reason the next slot exists.

06

The trade-off: a percentile target is a purchase, and so is measuring it

Every lesson here has to name what you gave up. Choosing a high percentile as your target costs three separate things, and they are all real.

One: engineering effort with sharply diminishing returns. Amazon specifies internal service response times at the 99.9th percentile, and explicitly decided the 99.99th was not worth chasing. That is not laziness — the difference between p999 and p9999 is increasingly made of events you do not control: a neighbour’s garbage collection, a retransmit, a disk deciding to think about its firmware. You can spend arbitrary money there and buy very little.

Two: statistical power, which you pay for in time resolution. The higher the percentile, the more requests you need before the number means anything. You can have a trustworthy p999 or a one-minute reporting window, and on a low-traffic service you cannot have both. Widening the window to get a stable p999 means you find out about incidents later. That is a genuine, unavoidable exchange, and it is why low-traffic services are often better served by an SLO on p95.

Three: storage and cardinality. Getting the tail right means keeping distributions, not single numbers, and a histogram is dozens of counters where a mean was one. Multiply that by endpoint, by instance, by status code, by region, and the metrics bill becomes a line item somebody will ask you to justify. The honest answer is that the mean is cheaper because it throws away the information you need.

And then the shape of the fix itself involves a trade:

To improveYou doYou pay
p50make the common path fasterordinary optimisation; no tension
p99reduce variance — bound work per request, paginate, bulkhead slow operations, run at lower utilisationidle capacity, and complexity in the request path
p999attack rare events — hedged requests, retry the straggler, cap tail latency by giving up on the slowest workextra load, extra complexity, and sometimes wrong answers
p9999control the whole machine — pinned cores, no co-tenants, no shared disksa great deal of money, for 1 request in 10,000

There is one more trade hiding in the choice, and it is the interesting one. An SLO on the median optimises for the customers who are cheapest to serve. An SLO on the p999 optimises for the customers with the most data — which, as DDIA points out, are frequently the ones who have bought the most from you. So “which percentile” is not only a statistics question. It is a question about which users you have decided to be fast for.

Amazon describes response time requirements for internal services in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though this affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. This is because the customers with the slowest requests are often those who have the most data on their accounts… Optimizing the 99.99th percentile was deemed too expensive and found to not yield enough benefit for Amazon’s purposes. Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., pp. 40–41

07

In the wild

Amazon, and why p999 became the default. The Dynamo paper and DDIA both record Amazon’s decision to specify internal services at the 99.9th percentile rather than the mean or the p99, on the grounds that the slowest requests belong to the accounts with the most data and therefore the most value. The second half of that decision matters just as much: they judged the 99.99th percentile too expensive for the benefit, and stopped. (DDIA 2nd ed., pp. 40–41.)

Averaging percentiles, named as a bug. DDIA does not hedge about this:

Beware that averaging percentiles (e.g., to reduce the time resolution or to combine data from several machines) is mathematically meaningless. The right way of aggregating response time data is to add the histograms. Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., p. 42

Every dashboard that plots a fleet-wide p99 by averaging per-instance p99s is doing the meaningless thing, and most of them do. It is worth checking yours before you next trust one in an incident.

Coordinated omission, or why your load test agrees with you. Gil Tene’s term for a measurement bug that makes tail latency vanish: a load generator that waits for a response before sending the next request stops sending during exactly the stall it should be recording, so the slow period contributes a handful of samples instead of thousands. The result is a percentile chart that systematically under-reports the tail, and it is the reason a service can pass a load test and fall over in production at the same throughput. HdrHistogram was written partly to make the correction easy. (Gil Tene, “How NOT to Measure Latency”.)

The libraries that exist because of this. HdrHistogram, t-digest, DDSketch and OpenHistogram all solve the same problem: record a latency distribution cheaply and accurately enough that high percentiles survive aggregation. DDIA lists them at p. 42. Reaching for one is the practical form of everything on this page.

Where the mean is still the right answer. Capacity and cost. Little’s Law needs a mean sojourn time, not a p99; so does “how many threads do I need” and “what does a million requests cost me”. The failure is not using means, it is using a mean where a promise was required.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A percentile is what somebody actually experienced; an average is the total divided by the count
  • For an exponential distribution p50 = 0.69 × mean and p99 = 4.61 × mean, independent of load
  • The mean sits at the 63.2nd percentile, never the 50th
  • Percentiles do not average and do not add across services
  • Which percentile to pick is a business decision about whose experience counts

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why can you not average the p99s of ten shards to get the fleet p99?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a percentile is a position in a sorted list, not a quantity. Averaging positions is meaningless — the fleet p99 depends on the whole combined distribution and on the traffic weight of each shard. A shard taking 1% of traffic with a 10 s p99 barely moves the fleet number; one taking 40% moves it a lot. To combine, merge the distributions (or the histograms) and then take the percentile.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    An average-latency alert never fires while users complain constantly. Explain the mechanism.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The mean is dominated by the bulk of fast requests. If 99% of requests take 50 ms and 1% take 5 s, the mean is 100 ms — indistinguishable from healthy. Meanwhile 1 in 100 requests is a five-second stall, and a user making ten requests per page load has a ~10% chance of hitting one. The alert is measuring the total; the complaint is about the tail.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why is Amazon's choice of p999 a business argument rather than a technical one?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the slowest requests belong to the customers with the most data — the most items in a cart, the longest order history — which is to say the highest-value customers. The percentile you protect is a statement about whose experience you are willing to degrade. Amazon also judged p9999 not worth the cost, which is the same argument run the other way.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    A dashboard reports p99 = 400 ms computed per-minute, then averaged over the hour for the graph. Why might the real hourly p99 be much worse?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because averaging 60 per-minute p99s smooths away the minutes that were bad. If 58 minutes had a p99 of 200 ms and two had 6 s, the averaged graph reads ~400 ms and looks fine — but the hour's actual p99, over all requests pooled, is dominated by those two minutes. Aggregating percentiles over time has the same defect as aggregating them over shards.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a summary statistic chosen for mathematical convenience can be systematically blind to the cases that matter most. Name an instance outside latency.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Mean income versus median in economics — one billionaire moves the mean and not the experience. Average class size versus the size of the class the average student sits in (the class-size paradox). Mean time between failures for a component whose failures are clustered. In every case the fix is the same: report a distribution or a specific quantile, and be explicit about whose experience the number describes.
    Did you have that?
