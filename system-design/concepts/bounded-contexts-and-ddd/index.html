Tier 8 · Architecture and patterns Bounded contexts & DDD

Fourteen raw readers share one word, and 83% of the time one is already wrong

"Customer" means a billing account with a payment method and a dunning state inside Billing, and a person with a sentiment and a ticket history inside Support. Both models are correct. Neither is correct outside its own boundary, and the word is spelled identically in both, which is the entire trap — nothing in the code flags the difference. A bounded context is the boundary inside which one model is valid; this lesson prices what happens at the seam between two of them, where letting one context read the other's raw representation of a shared term costs something very different from routing that read through one explicit translation.

01

The same word, two models, and nothing that flags the difference

Billing’s database has a customer table. So does Support’s. Nobody planned this collision — both teams needed a customer, both used the obvious noun, and for a while it did not matter because neither team read the other’s data.

Then Support’s dashboard started showing a customer.status field pulled straight from Billing, to flag accounts at risk. In Billing, status is a billing lifecycle: active, past_due, dunning, cancelled — driven by payment events. In Support, “status” had always meant something else: whether someone is mid-conversation. A customer in Billing’s dunning state is not necessarily unhappy; a lot of dunning is a card that expired on a normal renewal. Support’s dashboard started reading dunning as “this person is a problem,” because that is what the word suggests to a support agent, and nothing in the code said otherwise.

This is a bounded context violation, and it is worth being precise about what that term names, because “bounded context” gets used as a synonym for “microservice” and it is not one. A bounded context (Eric Evans, 2003) is the boundary inside which one model and one vocabulary are internally consistent. Billing’s model of status is correct — inside Billing. Support’s older, different sense of “status” was also correct — inside Support. Neither model is wrong. The word crossed a boundary without anyone translating it, and the boundary is not drawn on an architecture diagram; it is drawn wherever a model stops being self-consistent.

A related, easily conflated idea: a subdomain is a fact about the problem — Billing, Support and Fulfillment are subdomains of the business whether or not a line of code has been written yet. A bounded context is a fact about the solution — the boundary actually drawn in running software. They do not have to be the same partition. decomposition-by-capability-and-subdomain prices what happens when a service boundary is drawn by capability (what the business does) instead of by subdomain (what a concept is): a concept shared by several capabilities either gets duplicated once per capability or centralized behind one more hop, and that lesson prices both sides. This lesson assumes the boundary is already drawn and asks the narrower question sitting inside either choice: whatever the boundary is, crossing it with an ambiguous term has a price, and that price is computable.

02

The mechanism: n independent guesses against one

Support’s dashboard is not the only place customer.status gets read. Over eighteen months, fourteen different call sites in Support’s codebase ended up reading Billing’s raw field directly — a dashboard, an alert rule, three reports, an export job, a handful of one-off scripts. Nothing forced any of the fourteen engineers who wrote them to confront Billing’s definition, because there was no artifact whose job was to state it. Each one made an independent judgment call about what the field meant.

Call that judgment wrong with probability misreadRate. Fourteen independent calls are fourteen independent coin flips, so the chance at least one is already wrong is the standard compound-probability shape:

P(at least one wrong) = 1 − (1 − misreadRate)n

At a 12% misread rate and 14 raw readers: 1 − 0.88¹⁴ = 83.3%. Not “one of our fourteen integrations might be subtly wrong” — a coin-flip-away-from-certain chance that one already is, right now, before anyone has found it.

The cost side is just as exact. Every misread found downstream — in a support escalation, in a bad metric someone finally questions — costs costPerDefectHours to track down and fix, and by linearity of expectation the total is:

cost, no translation = n · misreadRate · costPerDefectHours

At the numbers above: 14 × 0.12 × 6 = 10.08 hours. Double the raw readers and this exactly doubles — it is linear, not compounding, because each defect is found and fixed independently.

Now the alternative: route every read through one function — billingCustomerToSupportView(), or whatever a team calls it — that states the mapping explicitly, once, and is covered by a contract test against both sides’ real definitions. This is the anti-corruption layer this lesson forward- references; strangler-fig-and-acl builds one end to end. Priced here, it is a one-time build cost plus a single residual risk — the translation itself can still be wrong, but there is one trial instead of n, caught by the one test written to catch it rather than scattered across fourteen call sites nobody thought to re-examine:

cost, one translation = buildCostHours + boundaryErrorRate · costToFixBoundaryHours

At 8 hours to build and a 3% chance the mapping itself needs a 1-hour fix: 8 + 0.03 × 1 = 8.03 hours — and this number does not contain n anywhere. A fifteenth consumer costs a fifteenth call site, not a fifteenth independent chance at a new defect.

Predict A 30th raw call site gets added, at an independently-measured 8% misread rate, 5 hours to find and fix a raw defect downstream. Building one explicit translation costs 10 hours, with a 3% chance the translation itself needs a 1-hour fix. Has the translation already earned its cost at 30 call sites?
n* = (buildCostHours + boundaryErrorRate·costToFixBoundaryHours) / (misreadRate·costPerDefectHours) = (10 + 0.03×1) / (0.08×5) = 10.03 / 0.4 ≈ 25.1 raw readers. At 30, reading raw already costs 30 × 0.08 × 5 = 12 hours against the seam's flat 10.03 — the seam paid for itself five sites ago. The third option confuses two different curves: the PROBABILITY of a misread does compound toward 1 and never truly finishes, but the COST comparison this question asks about is a straight-line crossing, not an asymptote — it resolves at a specific, finite n and stays resolved past it. Ownership (the fourth option) decides WHO writes the seam, not whether one is worth writing — a real question, and a different one.

03

The intuition: a shipping contract that just says 'a ton'

A cargo manifest lists a shipment’s weight as “40 tons.” A long ton is 2,240 lb. A short ton is 2,000 lb. A metric tonne is about 2,204.6 lb. All three are called “a ton” in ordinary speech, in the country that uses each one, and the word gives no sign of which is meant.

The shipper’s insurer reads “40 tons” in its own country’s convention. So does the customs office at the destination port. So does the freight broker reconciling invoices. Three readers, three independent guesses, and if any two disagree the dispute shows up as a discrepancy on a form, weeks later, far from whoever wrote “40 tons” in the first place — exactly the shape of a misread found downstream rather than caught where it was made.

The fix that real shipping contracts converged on is not “everyone should be more careful reading weights.” It is a definitions clause, stated once, at the top of the contract: “ton” in this agreement means the metric tonne. One sentence, read by every party, replaces however many independent guesses the contract would otherwise have invited. That sentence is a translation layer with a single author, and it is worth exactly as much as the dispute it would otherwise have cost — which is the same accounting this lesson runs for a customer.status field.

04

The mechanism, generalised: where the lines cross, and why the crossing point is honest

Section 2 already has the two cost functions. What is worth stating on its own is why they take the shapes they do, because the shape is the entire argument and not a detail of these particular numbers.

Reading raw is linear in n because the decision is repeated. There is no shared state between call site 3 and call site 11 — each one is a fresh, independent roll, so the expected number of misreadings, and the cost that follows from it, is n · misreadRate. Nothing about this changes if the fourteen call sites are in one service or scattered across five; the mechanism is about how many times the decision gets made, not how the code is deployed. This is the same “independent trials compound” shape decomposition.mjs’s serialAvailability uses for a request needing every one of k services to succeed — but it is not the same event, and it is worth being explicit about why it is not reused here: a^k is the probability every dependency succeeds; this is the complementary shape, the probability at least one of n independent readers fails, over an authoring decision rather than a runtime call. Different p, different event, so it gets its own line — matching how this repo already treats every other appearance of 1 − (1 − p)^n, once per file, because what p means is never actually shared between call sites.

Contrast that with decomposition-by-capability-and-subdomain’s driftProbability, which reuses serialAvailability unchanged for a question that looks similar at a glance: whether all of k duplicated copies of a concept are currently in sync. That reuse is correct because the mechanism really is identical — “all k copies agree” and “all k dependencies are up” are the same all-must-hold event with the nouns swapped. This lesson’s event is the opposite shape (at least one of n independent interpretations is wrong) over a different kind of variable (a human authoring decision, not a copy’s staleness), which is exactly the distinction that decides reuse throughout this repo: same formula is not the same license to import it.

Translation is constant in n because the decision is made once and consumed, not repeated. A consumer of the translated shape is not making a fresh judgment about what customer.status means — it is calling a function whose contract was settled when it was written and tested. The number of consumers is now a code-review question (“does this caller handle the return type correctly”), which is a different and cheaper failure mode than “does this caller correctly intuit an ambiguity it never saw stated.”

The break-even is where the two meet:

n* = (buildCostHours + boundaryErrorRate · costToFixBoundaryHours) ÷ (misreadRate · costPerDefectHours)

At this lesson’s numbers, n* ≈ 11.15. Below it, skipping the seam is genuinely cheaper — this is the honest half of the model, the one a lesson selling anti-corruption layers would be tempted to skip. A term with one or two raw readers is not worth a dedicated translation function; the break-even formula is exactly the tool that says so, rather than a rule of thumb about “always add a seam.”

And the two contexts do not hold still. Evans’s point about bounded contexts is not just that models differ at a moment in time — it is that each context’s model changes on its own schedule, because each team is solving its own problem and learns things the other team never sees. Every such change is a drift: a new dunning sub-state Billing adds for a new payment provider, a redefinition of “active” Support makes for its own reasons. Each drift re-exposes the entire raw-reader population to a fresh chance of misreading the new meaning, while a translation absorbs it in the one place built to be revisited:

annual cost, no translation = driftsPerYear · n · misreadRate · costPerDefectHours
annual cost, one translation = driftsPerYear · boundaryErrorRate · costToFixBoundaryHours

At two drifts a year, the scenario above costs 20.16 hours a year raw against 0.06 hours a year translated — and the one-time 8-hour build is recovered in 8 ÷ (20.16 − 0.06) ≈ 0.40 years, under five months. This is not a new formula; it is the same linear-vs-constant shape from section 2, multiplied by how often the disagreement gets a chance to recur.

06

The trade-off: a seam is not free, and it is not the only pattern

Everything above argues for building a translation once the raw-reader count clears n*. Building one has real costs this model prices honestly and real costs it does not price at all.

Priced here: it is one more artifact. Someone owns billingCustomerToSupportView() now — it needs its own test, its own review when either side’s model changes, and it sits on the path of every read. boundaryErrorRate and costToFixBoundaryHours are exactly this cost, kept deliberately small in this lesson’s numbers because a reviewed, contract-tested seam genuinely is safer per-trial than an unexamined raw read — but small is not zero, and a seam that nobody revisits when Billing adds a new status value has quietly become a fifteenth raw guess wearing a translation’s name.

Not priced here: which side should own it. Vaughn Vernon’s Implementing Domain-Driven Design (2013) names this the context map, and the ownership question is most of what it is about — this lesson’s translationLayerCost is silent on who pays buildCostHours:

patternwho writes the translationwhen it fits
Shared Kernelboth teams, jointlythe two contexts are willing to co-evolve one small shared model — rare, and expensive to keep agreed
Customer/Supplierthe upstream team, for its consumersupstream has the model and the incentive to keep it stable for known downstream consumers
Conformistnobody — downstream just adopts upstream’s model as-isdownstream has no leverage to ask for a translation at all, and the upstream model is good enough to live with
Anti-Corruption Layerthe downstream team, unilaterallydownstream needs its own model protected and cannot or should not wait on upstream to change
Open Host Service / Published Languageupstream, as a standing contractmany consumers, so upstream publishes one stable shape instead of negotiating one translation per consumer

This lesson’s numbers are the Anti-Corruption Layer row — Support builds the seam unilaterally, because Support is the one whose model is at risk and Billing has no reason to change. The other four rows have the same linear-vs-constant shape underneath them; what differs is which team’s hours pay buildCostHours, which is an organizational question this lesson’s arithmetic does not settle and should not pretend to.

And a seam does not eliminate ambiguity — it relocates the one remaining conversation. Somebody still has to decide what the translation should say, which means somebody from each side has to agree on the mapping at least once. The seam does not remove that conversation; it makes sure it happens exactly once, in one place, instead of fourteen times, silently, by accident.

07

In the wild

The term is Eric Evans’s, from Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software (2003), which is where “bounded context” and “ubiquitous language” both originate as a pair: a vocabulary is only guaranteed consistent inside the boundary drawn around it. Vaughn Vernon’s Implementing Domain-Driven Design (2013) is the source most engineers actually reach for day to day, and it is where the context-mapping patterns in the table above — Shared Kernel, Customer/ Supplier, Conformist, Anti-Corruption Layer, Open Host Service — get their standard names.

Kleppmann and Riccomini’s Designing Data-Intensive Applications (2nd ed.) names DDD once, on page 54, in the maintainability chapter’s discussion of managing complexity: domain-driven design is listed alongside the Gang of Four’s design patterns as a methodology for building application-level abstractions, with a pointer to Evans (2003) and a note that the book itself is about the general-purpose primitives underneath such techniques rather than the techniques themselves. That is the entire treatment — a one-sentence pointer, not an argument — which is worth saying plainly rather than stretching it into a citation it cannot support.

The most expensive real instance of this exact failure is not a business term at all — it is a unit. NASA’s Mars Climate Orbiter was lost on arrival in September 1999 because the contractor’s software delivered thruster-performance data in pound-force-seconds while the navigation software at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory expected newton-seconds, and no step in the pipeline translated between them. The two teams shared a value with no shared agreement on what unit it was in — a bounded-context violation in every sense this lesson uses the term, just with a physical quantity standing in for “customer” — and NASA’s own Mishap Investigation Board (Phase I report, November 1999) named the absence of a verification step, not the arithmetic itself, as the root cause. A single explicit check at the handoff — one seam — was the fix that was not there.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A bounded context is where one model and one vocabulary are valid — the same word used outside it names a different concept, not the same concept restated
  • A subdomain is a fact about the problem space (what the business does) and a bounded context is a fact about the solution space (a boundary drawn in running software); they are not required to be the same partition
  • With no explicit translation, every raw reader of a shared term makes its own independent guess at what it means, so at n readers the probability at least one is already wrong is 1 − (1 − p)^n
  • Reading a shared term raw costs exactly n · misreadRate · costPerDefect — linear in the number of readers — while one explicit translation costs a one-time build plus a single residual risk, which never reads a consumer count at all
  • The two costs cross at a real, computable n* — below it, skipping the seam is genuinely cheaper; the honest half of this model is that an ambiguous term with one or two readers is not yet worth a translation layer
  • Bounded contexts do not hold still — each side's model keeps evolving on its own schedule — so the same linear-vs-constant shape recurs on every drift, and it is the annual gap, not just the one-time gap, that decides whether the seam pays for itself
  • An anti-corruption layer is the general name for that one explicit translation; this lesson only prices what it is worth, `strangler-fig-and-acl` is where one gets built

  3. Near transfer

    Instead of building a translation layer, a team decides each of the 14 consuming teams should just "be more careful" — add a code comment next to every raw read, warning that Billing's customer status means something different from Support's. Does that fix the scaling problem the same way an explicit translation does?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No, and the reason is the mechanism, not the effort. A comment does not turn n independent judgment calls into one — it is still 14 separate engineers, at 14 separate times, each deciding for themselves whether this read needs the warning heeded, so the cost stays n · misreadRate · costPerDefect: linear in n, exactly as before, just with a (hopefully) somewhat lower misreadRate. What actually changes the shape from linear to constant is routing every read through one piece of code — a function, not a warning — so that there is structurally one place to get it right instead of fourteen. "Be more careful" can lower p; only an explicit seam collapses n trials into one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: many independent readers each interpret one ambiguous shared symbol according to their own local convention, so the chance any one of them gets it wrong compounds with every reader — until a single authority states the conversion once, and the risk stops multiplying. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The word "ton" in a shipping contract. A long ton is 2,240 lb, a short ton is 2,000 lb, and a metric tonne is about 2,204.6 lb — three different quantities, one spelled-the-same word, and a cargo manifest, an insurance policy and a customs declaration can each read it according to their own country's convention with nobody the wiser until the weights are reconciled. The fix in trade contracts is exactly this lesson's seam: state the unit explicitly, once, in the contract's definitions section, rather than trusting every clerk who touches the shipment to guess correctly.

    Other instances: "billion", which meant 10⁹ in American usage and 10¹² in the traditional British long scale for most of the 20th century, so a translated financial report or treaty figure needed the scale stated explicitly or it was off by a factor of a thousand. And a meeting time given without a time zone — "3pm" read locally by every invitee is n independent guesses at what instant is meant, and the fix everyone eventually adopts, writing "3pm UTC" once in the invitation, is a translation layer with exactly one author.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
