Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Build vs buy, in dollars

A $0.99-per-resolution AI agent is the cheapest option in month 1 and the most expensive by month 28, and discounting the bill at 12% a year pushes the build-vs-buy crossover from month 44.8 to month 59

Tier 8's `build-vs-buy-vs-managed` prices this same three-way decision in engineer-weeks of switching cost against a flat rebuild figure — one crossover, because a rising line only ever meets a flat one once. This lesson prices it in dollars of total cost of ownership, and the finding is that the three modes are not shaped alike, so the crossover is not one month, it is a sequence: build is a big cost on day one plus a shallow slope, buy is a flat per-seat line that never had a day-one cost to begin with, and managed is priced per unit of the thing that actually grows when the product succeeds — which makes its bill a parabola, not a line. A line with an intercept, a line through the origin, and a parabola through the origin cross each other in a specific order, and which option is cheapest changes twice before month 30, in a worked example built entirely from real, dated seat and usage prices.

Read first — build-vs-buy-vs-managedcost-attribution

01

A support team, and three ways to price the same decision

A product team needs a customer-support system: intake, routing, canned answers, and a way to resolve the easy tickets without a human. Three real ways to get there, three real prices.

Build it. Ticketing, routing, a rule-based triage bot, on-call for when it breaks. Call it 1,200 engineer-hours to build and 25 engineer-hours a month to keep running — a real quarter-plus of a small team’s time, priced at the same fully-loaded rate this tier already uses: $85 an hour, reused unchanged from cost-attribution.mjs.

Buy it. License a support desk. Zendesk’s Suite Team plan lists at $55 per agent per month on annual billing — a flat fee per seat on the support team, whether that seat closes ten tickets a month or ten thousand. Eighty agents: $4,400 a month, from day one, forever, as long as the licence is held.

Use a managed AI agent. Intercom’s Fin prices at $0.99 per resolved conversation — no seats, no licence, a bill that moves with however many conversations the product generates. Early on, at a modest 1,000 resolutions a month, that is $990 a month — under a quarter of the buy price.

Every dollar figure on this page is a real, named, dated list price or a declared engineering-rate input, current as of 2026-08 — see the sources. Substitute your own; nothing below depends on the level, only on the SHAPE each option’s cost takes as time and usage move.

Read that last paragraph again: the managed option is the cheapest of the three by a wide margin, on day one. The rest of this lesson is about why that stops being true, and exactly when.

02

Three shapes, not one crossover

build-vs-buy-vs-managed (tier 8) already showed that a rising cost and a flat cost cross exactly once — engineer-weeks of vendor switching cost climbing against a fixed rebuild figure. That mechanism produces ONE crossover because it only ever has two shapes: a line and a flat horizontal. This lesson’s three options are not two shapes — they are three, and none of them is flat:

  • Build is an intercept plus a shallow slope: buildHours × $/hr once, then maintainHours/month × $/hr every month after. A line that starts high and climbs slowly.
  • Buy is a line through the origin: seats × seatPrice every month, no build phase, no intercept. It starts at zero and climbs at a fixed rate for as long as the seat count is held.
  • Managed is priced per unit of usage, and usage here is not fixed — it is a product’s own growth. A monthly bill that itself rises linearly with time, running-totalled over the months that have passed, is not a line, it is a parabola: the area under a linearly rising rate is quadratic in time.

A line with an intercept, a line through the origin, and a parabola through the origin do not share one crossing. They share up to three, pairwise, and the order those crossings happen in is the actual finding — not a single “break-even point” but a sequence of handoffs.

Predict Managed starts far cheaper than buy, and buy starts far cheaper than build (which begins with a $102,000 upfront cost). As the months pass and usage keeps growing, in what order does the cheapest option change?
Managed's bill is a parabola through the origin, so however far behind it starts, its growth term (173.25·T² in the worked scenario) eventually overtakes any fixed-rate line — it crosses buy's flat $4,400/month line at month 19.7. Build's big upfront cost keeps it the most expensive option for a long stretch regardless of its shallow slope, until buy's compounding flat payments finally pass it — at month 44.8. So the order is managed cheapest, then buy, then build: exactly the reverse of the order they started in.
Show it without answering

Three things worth naming from that rig before the derivation.

The parabola always wins eventually, whatever its starting position. However cheap managed looks on day one, a quadratic term with any positive growth rate outgrows a fixed linear rate given enough months. That is not a property of these particular prices — it is a property of a parabola against a line, full stop.

The two handoffs share one input. Usage growth decides both when managed overtakes buy and, indirectly, how long buy gets to be the cheapest option before build’s fixed cost finally pays for itself. Drag that one slider and both amber lines move.

Build hours only move ONE of the two handoffs. A bigger build estimate delays build’s own crossover with buy — it does nothing to the managed-vs-buy handoff, because build does not appear in that comparison at all. The rig’s three sliders map onto the three pairwise crossings unevenly, and that asymmetry is itself information: the number that decided whether to build was never in competition with the number that decides whether to keep the AI agent.

03

A car, a lease, and a rideshare

Three ways to get around a city, priced exactly the same way as the three ways to run a support desk.

Buy the car. A large payment up front, then a small ongoing one — insurance, fuel, the occasional repair. Whether you drive it every day or once a month, the purchase price is already spent.

Lease the car. A fixed payment every month, regardless of mileage. No purchase price at all — that is the entire appeal of leasing — but the monthly number does not move whether you drive ten miles a month or ten thousand.

Take a rideshare everywhere. No purchase, no lease, a price per mile. For someone who drives twice a month this is obviously the cheapest option, by a wide margin, and it is the option everyone would recommend on day one.

Then their commute changes. Miles driven start climbing — steadily, month over month — and the per-mile bill starts climbing with it, while the lease payment sits exactly where it always sat. Somewhere down the road, the rideshare bill passes the lease payment. Later still, it passes what buying the car would have cost, spread over the same years. Nobody who chose rideshares at two trips a month made a mistake. The mistake would be never noticing that the trips had become twenty a week, and never running the arithmetic again once they had.

04

The three shapes, derived

Build: an intercept, plus a slope that never grows on its own

buildCost(T) = buildHours · rate  +  maintainHours/mo · rate · T

A quadratic with c2 = 0. Not a simplification — the ongoing cost of running a system you already built does not accelerate on its own with the passage of time; it accelerates only if the system’s SCOPE grows, which is a different decision (and a different lesson) than the one priced here.

At the worked numbers — 1,200 hours, $85/hour, 25 hours/month — that is $102,000 on day one plus $2,125 every month after.

Buy: a line through the origin, because the thing billed is yours to hold flat

buyCost(T) = seats · seatPrice · T

No intercept — a licence has no build phase — and no acceleration, because the quantity being billed (your OWN headcount) is exactly the thing buying is supposed to let you hold flat while the product around it grows. That is the entire economic case for “buy” over “hire in proportion to load,” made visible as a term that is simply absent from the formula.

Eighty seats at $55: $4,400 a month, a straight line from zero.

Managed: a parabola, because the bill is the running total of a rising rate

managedCost(T) = unitPrice · volume0 · T  +  unitPrice · growth/mo · T² / 2

The monthly bill at time t is unitPrice · (volume0 + growth·t) — itself a straight line in t. The CUMULATIVE bill through month T is its running total, ∫₀ᵀ unitPrice·(volume0 + growth·t) dt, which is exactly this expression. The T² term is not an approximation of anything: it is the exact integral of a linearly rising bill, and it is the one term neither build nor buy has.

At $0.99 per resolution, 1,000 resolutions a month to start, growing 350 a month: $990 the first month, rising by $346.50 every month after that in the monthly rate, and cumulatively 990T + 173.25T².

Two crossings, both closed form

Every option above is a quadratic in T — c0 + c1·T + c2·T² — with build and buy simply carrying c2 = 0. Two such curves cross wherever their difference is zero, and a quadratic-minus-quadratic difference is itself at most a quadratic — solved directly, not searched for:

(A.c2 − B.c2)·T² + (A.c1 − B.c1)·T + (A.c0 − B.c0) = 0

Build vs buy has no T² term at all (both carry c2 = 0), so it reduces to one line: T = buildCost₀ ÷ (buySlope − buildSlope) = 102,000 ÷ (4,400 − 2,125) ≈ 44.8 months. Buy vs managed keeps the T² term, has two roots, and the one that matters is the positive one past zero: 19.7 months. Build vs managed, by the same quadratic formula: 27.8 months — after which managed is also more expensive than build, though buy stays cheaper than both until month 44.8.

Graded against a brute-force scan of the same three curves at every integer month from 1 to 600: agreement to the scan’s own resolution, because there is nothing approximate in either side — the closed form and the scan are evaluating the identical polynomial.

Ranking, not just crossing

Three curves produce three pairwise crossings, but only two of them change who is cheapest overall — the build-vs-managed crossing at month 27.8 happens while buy is already ahead of both, so it swaps who is in second place without moving the winner. The winner’s own handoffs are:

month 0 – 19.7  →  managed cheapest month 19.7 – 44.8  →  buy cheapest month 44.8 onward  →  build cheapest

Three regimes from three shapes, and the ORDER is the finding: the option that looked cheapest on day one is not merely eventually overtaken — it becomes the single most expensive of the three, because a parabola outgrows every fixed-rate line it is compared against, given enough time.

The dollar a discount rate doesn’t spend the same way twice

Every number above compares raw dollars, as if a dollar spent in month 50 costs what a dollar spent in month 1 costs. It does not. At a 12%/year discount rate — a declared input, the same style engineerHourUSD is declared in cost-attribution.mjs — a future cost is worth less today, and the three curves do not shrink by the same fraction:

NPV = c0  +  Σ monthlyCost(m) ÷ (1 + rₘ)ᵐ

rₘ is the monthly-compounded equivalent of the annual rate. Build’s c0 — the $102,000 — is paid at month 0 and is NOT discounted at all; only its small monthly slope shrinks. Buy’s and managed’s ENTIRE bill is paid out across the horizon, so a much larger share of each is discounted.

At 60 months and 12%/year: build’s undiscounted $229,500 becomes an NPV of $198,874 — a 13.3% reduction. Buy’s undiscounted $264,000 becomes $200,586 — a 24.0% reduction, nearly twice build’s. Managed’s $683,100 becomes $474,513 — a 30.5% reduction, the largest of the three, because more of its bill sits even later on the clock. The practical consequence: discounting moves the build-vs-buy crossover later, from month 44.8 to month 59 — roughly 14 months, 32%, later — because buy keeps looking relatively cheaper for longer once every future dollar is worth less than face value. A TCO comparison that skips discounting is not neutral; it specifically flatters whichever option front-loads its cost, which is build, every time.

06

What this model does not price

Everything above optimises pure TCO across three cost shapes, and that is a narrower question than “should we build, buy, or use a managed service” — the wider one is tier 8’s.

Usage growth is assumed linear, and real products are not. A step-function launch, a viral month, a churn event — any of those breaks the T² term’s own assumption and moves every crossing computed here. The fix is not a better formula, it is re-running this arithmetic against your own measured growth curve rather than trusting a straight-line extrapolation from month one.

“Buy” assumed seats stay flat, and that is a choice, not a law. If the support team grows proportionally with ticket volume instead of staying fixed, buy’s own curve stops being a line and becomes a parabola too — at which point buy and managed are racing each other with the same shape, and the winner depends on which per-unit price is smaller, not on which curve bends. Whether seats actually stay flat as usage grows is an operational commitment, not a pricing fact, and it is the single assumption this model is most sensitive to.

Real vendor pricing is rarely one number for very long. Seat prices step at volume tiers, usage-based prices often have a free allowance and later a lower marginal rate, and enterprise deals are negotiated, not listed. The two real prices used here (Zendesk’s $55/seat and Intercom’s $0.99/resolution) are current list prices as of 2026-08; a negotiated enterprise rate on either one moves every crossing in this lesson without changing the SHAPE of a single curve.

Switching cost is not in this model, and it is the point of the sibling lesson. Passing the build-vs-buy crossover says building would now be cheaper going forward — it says nothing about what it costs to migrate 80 agents’ workflows and however many customers’ open tickets off a licensed product, which is exactly the engineer-weeks build-vs-buy-vs-managed (tier 8) prices. Reading this lesson’s crossover month as “time to switch” without reading that one’s switching cost is the single most common way this arithmetic gets misapplied.

The discount rate is a declared input with a real effect on the answer, not a rounding choice. 12%/year is a mid-range corporate hurdle rate, not a measured fact about this decision — a team financing the build differently, or facing a different cost of capital, gets a genuinely different crossover month from the identical cost curves, and that sensitivity is worth stating rather than hiding behind one number.

And engineer-hours are the most averaged input in the whole model. $85/hour, reused from cost-attribution.mjs, varies 2–3× by market and seniority. The build curve’s INTERCEPT scales directly with it — halve the rate and the build-vs-buy crossover arrives at roughly half the month, because the one number holding build back from being cheap on day one is exactly the one number this model is most honest about being a guess.

07

In the wild

Zendesk’s per-agent pricing is the archetypal “buy,” and it is public. Suite Team lists at $55 per agent per month on annual billing, Suite Professional at $115 — verified against zendesk.com/pricing, 2026-08. Neither price cares how many tickets a seat closes, which is the entire reason “buy” produces a flat line rather than a usage-shaped one.

Intercom’s Fin is the archetypal “managed,” and its unit is a resolved conversation, not a seat. “Fin is priced at $0.99 per outcome,” charged once per conversation regardless of how many questions it answers within it — verified against intercom.com/pricing, 2026-08. A per-outcome price is a deliberate design choice by the vendor, not an accident of billing convenience: it means Intercom’s own revenue grows exactly when a customer’s usage grows, which is the same mechanism that makes the customer’s bill a parabola once usage itself is growing.

A blended, fully-loaded engineering rate is a declared input everywhere it appears in this tier, not a survey figure. engineerHourUSD = $85/hour is reused unchanged from cost-attribution.mjs, which states plainly what it is: total compensation plus overhead divided by billable hours, for a mid-level engineer in a mid-cost region, varying 2–3× by market. Reusing it here rather than re-declaring a second, slightly different number is the point — a build estimate and a labour estimate elsewhere in this tier should move together when the underlying rate assumption changes, not independently.

Discounting a future cost is not exotic finance, it is the same arithmetic a mortgage or a lease already runs. A dollar promised in five years is worth less than a dollar in hand today, by whatever the cost of capital is — 12%/year here, a declared mid-range corporate hurdle rate in the same style as every other declared input in this tier. The finding worth carrying past this page is not the specific rate, it is the DIRECTION: discounting always flatters whichever option defers its cost, and in a build/buy/managed comparison that option is never build.

And tier 8 already named the non-dollar half of this decision. build-vs-buy-vs-managed prices control, customization ceiling, and the engineer-weeks it costs to leave a vendor once integration has deepened — a real crossover of its own, in a different currency. Neither lesson is the complete answer alone: this one says WHEN each option is cheapest in dollars; that one says what it costs to change your mind once you have chosen.

Managed's monthly bill starts at $0.99 × 1,000 = $990 — under a quarter of buy's — and rises by $0.99 × 350 = $346.50 every month after that, because the usage it is billed on keeps growing while the seat count does not. Solving 4,400T = 990T + 173.25T² for the positive root gives T ≈ 19.68 months. It flips from managed-cheaper to buy-cheaper, not the other way, because managed starts below buy and a rising quantity that starts below a flat one can only catch up from beneath — the reverse would require managed to start ABOVE buy, which a metered service at low volume never does.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Same three options. Undiscounted, build overtakes buy at month 44.8 — build becomes the cheapest option from then on. Discount every future dollar at 12% a year and recompute. Does the crossover move earlier or later, and by roughly how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Later — to month 59, roughly 14 months (32%) later. Build's cost is $102,000 paid at month 0, which no discount rate touches, plus a small ongoing slope; buy's $4,400/month is paid out across the whole horizon, and every one of those future dollars is worth less today than its face value. At a 12%/year rate, build's 60-month total shrinks from $229,500 to an NPV of $198,874 — a 13.3% reduction — while buy's shrinks from $264,000 to $200,586, a 24.0% reduction. Buy shrinks more in relative terms because more of its bill sits later on the clock, so it stays the cheaper option for longer before build's fixed, undiscounted cost catches up.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    "Managed is the cheapest option for this workload — the numbers say so — so we should build everything on the managed service going forward." What does the crossover analysis actually say, and where does that sentence go wrong?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The crossover analysis says managed is cheapest up to month 19.7 of this specific usage trajectory — a scenario where the metered volume is small today and provably GROWS. It is not a verdict about managed services in general, and it is not even a verdict about THIS one beyond the horizon computed. The sentence's error is treating one point in an ordering that is known to flip twice as if it were the ordering's steady state — exactly the mistake `build-vs-buy-vs-managed` (tier 8) warns against from the other direction, where passing a lock-in crossover does not retroactively make the earlier choice wrong. Here it is the mirror image: being ahead today does not make an option permanently ahead, when the very shape of its cost curve (a parabola against two lines) guarantees it will not stay ahead once usage keeps growing.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    Name a non-computing instance of: one option costs a lot up front and little afterward, another costs a fixed amount regardless of how much you use it, and a third costs almost nothing per unit of use — until the amount you use it grows past the point where a fixed fee would have been cheaper all along.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Owning a car, leasing a car, and taking a rideshare everywhere. Buying a car outright is a large upfront cost and a small ongoing one (insurance, maintenance) — the intercept. Leasing is a flat monthly payment regardless of how many miles you actually drive — the line through the origin. Rideshare is priced per mile, and it is by far the cheapest option for someone who drives twice a month — right up until a change in circumstances (a new commute, a move to the suburbs) makes miles driven grow month over month, at which point the per-mile bill is a rising curve chasing down a lease payment that never moved. Nobody who started taking rideshares everywhere because it was obviously cheaper made a mistake — the mistake would be never re-running the arithmetic once the commute changed.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
