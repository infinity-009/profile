Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Provisioned vs spot explained well nowhere else

Spot was 70% off and the job cost 1.99× what on demand would have

Three prices for identical hardware, and only one of them is a cost. Preemptible capacity is 60-90% off and can be taken back with a couple of minutes' notice, so its real cost is its price times the wall clock it wastes — and the discount it has to beat turns out to equal that waste exactly. The waste has a minimum at a checkpoint interval of sqrt(2c/λ), which nobody computes. The committed half is one line and nobody computes that either: a 40% discount needs 60% utilisation.

01

A 70% discount, and the bill went up

A nightly job. Feature engineering and a training pass, six hours of compute on one accelerated node, seven nights a week — call it 180 useful hours a month. On demand the node costs $4.00 an hour, so the job costs $720 a month and everybody knows the number.

Somebody notices the provider sells the identical hardware as preemptible capacity at 70% off. Same instance type, same region, same performance. $1.20 an hour instead of $4.00. The ticket says the bill will go from $720 to $216 and it is approved in a sentence.

The invoice comes back at $1,431.

Nothing is broken and nothing is mispriced. The node really did cost $1.20 an hour. What the ticket missed is that the capacity is reclaimed — the provider takes the machine back when it wants it, after a short warning — and this job holds its state in memory and cannot resume. Every reclaim throws away everything done since the job started, and the job starts again. On this instance type a machine survives about two hours on average; getting another one takes about five minutes.

So the job needs six uninterrupted hours out of a machine that lasts two. It gets them eventually, at a cost of 6.63 machine-hours per useful hour. 180 useful hours became 1,193 billed hours, and $1.20 × 1,193 is $1,431 — 1.99× what on demand would have cost.

There is a second consequence and it is worse than the money. Six hours of work now takes 39.8 hours of wall clock. The nightly job is no longer nightly.

Every dollar figure on this page is an illustrative input, dated 2026-08, low confidence, and almost certainly wrong for your bill. Substitute your own — nothing here depends on the level. Every finding below is a discount, a utilisation, an interval, or a ratio, and all four survive every price being wrong.

02

Then checkpoint. But how often?

The belief this lesson is breaking is not exotic. It is the one on the ticket: a 70% discount is a 70% saving, because it is the same machine at a lower price. The first half of that sentence is true. The second half assumes the hour you bought and the hour of work you needed are the same thing, and on preemptible capacity they are not — you are billed for the failed attempts too.

The fix is obvious and it is right: save progress periodically, so a reclaim costs you only the work since the last save rather than all of it. What is not obvious is how often, and this is where the arithmetic nobody does lives. Checkpoint every thirty seconds and you spend your life writing checkpoints. Checkpoint once an hour and a reclaim costs you half an hour. There is a best answer, it is a specific number of minutes, and it depends on exactly two things: what a checkpoint costs and how often you are reclaimed.

The rig below draws the whole decision. The teal curve is what an hour of useful work costs on spot, as a multiple of the on-demand price, at each checkpoint interval. The crimson dashed line is on demand. The slate dotted line is a three-year commitment on a machine you keep busy — because that is the other price the same hardware is sold at, and it is the comparison teams skip.

Predict A six-hour nightly job holds its state in memory and cannot resume. The capacity it runs on is reclaimed on average every two hours; a replacement machine takes five minutes to get. Spot is 70% off, so $1.20 an hour against $4.00. Against $720 a month on demand, what does the month cost?
Wall-clock time per useful second is (e^λW − 1)(1/λ + r)/W — the expected time to get W uninterrupted seconds out of a machine that dies at rate λ, retrying from the start each time. With λW = 3 that is 6.63, so 180 useful hours become 1,193 billed hours and $1.20 × 1,193 = $1,431. The first option is the reasoning on the ticket, and it is wrong because the discount is per hour held while the job needs work completed — every abandoned attempt is billed in full. The last option is the near-miss worth understanding: the expected cost is a closed form and needs only the rate, never the times. And there is a consequence nobody predicts: the same 6.63 means six hours of work takes 39.8 hours, so the job stops fitting in a night.
Show it without answering

Three things in that rig are worth naming out loud.

The curve has an interior minimum, and almost nobody looks for it. Checkpoint frequency is usually set by a round number in a config file — every 1,000 steps, every 10 minutes — and the right answer is a computable quantity that moves with the instance type you are running on. On this machine a four-minute checkpoint wants a 28-minute interval; a fifteen-minute checkpoint wants a 50-minute one.

The bottom of the curve is flat and the right-hand side is not. Anywhere from 0.8× to 1.25× of the optimum is within 1% of the best cost, so this does not need a decimal point. But eight times too often costs 101% more waste while eight times too rarely costs 318%. The penalty is asymmetric, so when in doubt checkpoint more often than you think.

Drag the checkpoint cost up and spot loses to a commitment before it loses to on demand. The slate line sits at 0.40× — a three-year commitment at 60% off, on a machine kept busy. The teal curve’s minimum crosses it long before it crosses 1.00×. The cheapest per-hour price on the price list is not the cheapest way to buy an hour of work, and the gap between those two sentences is this whole lesson.

03

Working in a café where the power might cut out

You are writing a long document on a laptop in a café. The power is unreliable and the battery is dead, so at any moment the machine goes off and you lose whatever you have not saved.

Saving costs you something. Not much — a few seconds and a break in your thinking — but it is not free, and you pay it every single time whether or not the power was about to cut.

Not saving costs you something too, and how much depends on when the cut comes. If it comes just after you saved, you lose nothing. If it comes just before you were going to, you lose everything since last time. On average, if the cut is equally likely at any moment, you lose half the gap — which is the whole reason there is a best gap rather than “save as rarely as you can bear”.

Put those together and the shape is obvious once you see it. Save every ten seconds and you spend the morning saving. Save every two hours and each outage costs you an hour. Somewhere between is a gap where the two costs are balanced, and that gap gets shorter the more often the power cuts and longer the more annoying saving is. It moves like a square root of the ratio, which is why halving the outage rate does not double the gap — it multiplies it by about 1.4.

Three more things from the same café, and each is a finding rather than a flourish.

Sometimes the lights flicker first. If a two-second flicker reliably precedes the cut, you save then — and you never lose anything at all, so periodic saving becomes pointless. This is the single biggest thing in the whole model, and it only works if saving is faster than the flicker.

Some work cannot be saved. A phone call cannot be checkpointed. If the call is two minutes long you will get through it; if it is three hours long on a line that drops every hour, you will never finish it, no matter how many times you start again. What decides that is not “can it be saved” but how long it has to run against how often the line drops.

And the seat is not the only way to buy a desk. The café seat is cheap and someone may need it. There is a co-working place charging by the hour, and the same place will rent you a desk for a year at 40% off. That last one is worth it if you turn up more than 60% of the days — and that sentence contains no prices, which is why it is the one to remember.

04

Where the waste comes from, and the interval that removes most of it

The quantity that is not on the price list

Preemptible capacity has a price and a cost, and they differ by one factor. Call it the stretch: wall-clock seconds billed per second of useful work completed.

cost per useful hour = spot price × stretch

On demand the stretch is 1, because nothing takes the machine away. On spot it is 1 plus everything you pay for and do not get: the checkpoints you wrote, the work a reclaim destroyed, and the minutes spent waiting for another machine.

Derive the stretch once and everything else follows. A segment is T seconds of compute followed by a checkpoint write costing c, so it needs S = T + c seconds of uninterrupted machine time to commit T seconds of progress. Let the machine be reclaimed at hazard λ per second it is running and let r be the delay before another one is serving. Write E for the expected wall time to get one segment committed. Either the machine survives all of S, or it dies at some X shorter than S, in which case you have spent X + r and must run the segment again from the last checkpoint:

E = E[min(X, S)] + P(X < S) · (r + E)   ⟹   E = (eλS − 1) · (1/λ + r)

And stretch = E ÷ T. Three sanity readings: at λ = 0 it is exactly (T + c)/T, the checkpoint overhead with no losses; with free checkpoints taken continuously it is 1 + λr, the restart tax alone; and for large λS it grows like eλS, which is why a long job that cannot resume is not merely expensive but exponentially expensive.

Graded against a simulation that holds a machine, samples when it is taken away, throws the uncommitted work on the floor and counts the clock, across fifteen combinations of interval, checkpoint cost, preemption rate and restart delay: the closed form and the simulation agree to within 0.29%, over hundreds of thousands of measured preemptions.

The discount you need is the clock you waste

Spot beats on demand when (1 − discount) × stretch < 1. Rearranged, that is the most useful line on this page:

discount spot must beat = 1 − 1 ÷ stretch

Which is also, read the other way, the share of wall-clock time that produced nothing. Same number, two sentences, no price in either. A job that wastes a third of its clock needs a third off. A job that has to be run twice on average needs 50%.

Half an interval, and the square root that falls out of it

Two costs, pulling opposite ways. The checkpoint overhead is c seconds paid every T, so it is c/T of the clock and it falls as T grows. The lost work is what a reclaim destroys, and this is the term with the interesting shape: preemptions are memoryless, so one lands with equal probability anywhere inside the interval, and the expected age of the unsaved progress it finds is half an interval.

waste ≈ c/T  +  λT/2   ⟹   T* ≈ sqrt(2c/λ)

Differentiate and set to zero: −c/T² + λ/2 = 0. This is Young’s 1974 result, and the square root is why it is worth carrying in your head — a checkpoint four times more expensive wants an interval only twice as long.

The exact answer replaces that approximation with a root that has no elementary form but two useful consequences. Substituting u = λT and k = λc, the stationarity condition is −ln(1 − u) − u = k, whose left side is the series u²/2 + u³/3 + u⁴/4 + … — truncate at the first term and you recover the square root. And because that series diverges as u approaches 1:

T* < 1/λ, always

No checkpoint cost whatsoever justifies an interval longer than the mean time between preemptions. It approaches that ceiling from below as the checkpoint gets more expensive and never crosses it, because past it a segment would essentially never commit.

Substituting the same condition back into the stretch collapses it entirely, which is the identity the model is built on and the one worth checking twice:

stretch at the best interval = (1 + λr) ÷ (1 − λT*)

So the checkpoint cost affects the answer only by setting T*, and the restart delay only multiplies. Verified two ways — by this expression and by evaluating the renewal form at T* — and then against the simulation.

How wrong the memorable formula is, and what that costs

The square root is the leading term, so its error grows with λc. Measured: +1.5% on a twenty-second checkpoint against capacity reclaimed every six hours, +9.2% on a four-minute checkpoint reclaimed every two hours, and +15.7% on a four-minute checkpoint reclaimed every forty-five minutes. It always overshoots.

And it barely matters, which is the honest reading. Being 15.7% wrong about the interval costs 0.39% on the bill, because the optimum is flat — 0.8× to 1.25× of it is within 1%. Use the square root. What is worth being careful about is the direction of the error when you are guessing rather than computing: eight times too rarely costs 318% more waste, eight times too often costs 101%.

The lever that is not in the formula: the notice window

Providers do not seize a machine silently. They send a warning first — documented as two minutes on one large provider and as little as thirty seconds on two others, so check yours rather than trusting a round number. If your checkpoint takes less than the notice, you write it when the notice arrives, and then nothing is ever lost:

checkpoint fits the notice ⟹ best periodic interval is infinite, and stretch = 1 + λ(c + r)

Periodic checkpointing becomes pure overhead, because its only purpose was to bound a loss that no longer happens. What remains is one drain and one restart per preemption — and the drain is not itself at risk, since the machine is going away at the end of the notice either way.

On the story machine that is the difference between needing 10.98% off and needing 4.26% off — a factor of 5.4 — bought by making a checkpoint smaller rather than by negotiating a price. It is the largest single lever in the model, and it is an engineering property.

The other half: a commitment is a utilisation question and nothing else

Provisioned capacity — reserved instances, savings plans, committed use — is the opposite trade. There is no reclaim risk at all, so the stretch is exactly 1. What you give up is that you pay for the term whether you use it or not. So:

a commitment at discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d

Exactly, not approximately, and with no price, no instance type and no workload in it — the price cancels. 40% off needs 60% utilisation. 60% off needs 40%. 35% off needs 65%.

That one line disposes of the nightly job from slot 1: it runs 180 of the month’s 720 hours, so 25% utilisation, and a one-year commitment at 35% off needs 65%. Priced per useful hour, the commitment costs 2.60× on demand and a three-year commitment 1.60×. Neither is close. The nightly job belongs on spot with a checkpoint, and on no commitment at all.

Real fleets do not have one utilisation, though — they have a demand curve. The same break-even applies per unit of capacity, which turns it into a marginal rule:

commit to the k-th node only while it is busy more than (1 − d) of the time

Total cost is k(1 − d) per hour plus on demand for everything above k; differentiate and the minimum is where P(demand ≥ k) = 1 − d. So the optimal commitment level is the d-th percentile of your own demand curve: at 60% off, commit to the 60th percentile.

On a fleet swinging between 4 and 35 nodes, averaging 17.4 — half of peak — that rule says commit to 19 nodes at a 60% discount and 12 at a 35% one. Both were found by the percentile and confirmed by searching every possible level. The saving against buying everything on demand is 40.3% and 17.9%.

Both mistakes cost real money. Committing to peak — which is what “we reserved the fleet” usually means — costs 34.9% more than the percentile at a 60% discount, and at a 35% discount it is worse than committing nothing at all. Committing to nothing leaves the whole 40.3% on the table.

The number I expected to be different

I expected the residual result to be a rule of thumb. It is closer than that: measured against the simulation, the mean work destroyed per preemption is 0.507 of an interval with a twenty-second checkpoint and 0.556 with a four-minute one, and the exact expression matches the measurement to within 0.09%. What I had wrong was the direction of the correction — I assumed the exponential’s bias toward early failures always pushes the loss below T/2, and it does, but a second effect pushes the other way and is often larger: a preemption during the checkpoint write loses the whole interval, not part of it. With an expensive checkpoint that dominates and the loss is above half an interval. Two corrections, opposite signs, and “about half” is good to 10% only because they partly cancel.

06

The trade-off: this buys the cheapest hour and can cost you the deadline

Everything above optimises one quantity — dollars per hour of completed work — and that is not the only thing you are buying.

The stretch is also a schedule, and the schedule is usually the constraint. A stretch of 1.82 does not just cost 82% more machine-hours; it means a twelve-hour job takes 21.8 hours. If it feeds a morning report, the cheapest option on this page is the wrong option and no amount of cost arithmetic changes that. The honest framing is that spot converts money into variance, and variance in completion time is what a deadline is made of. The tail matters more than the mean, and this lesson computes only the mean — the p99 completion time of a preemptible job is far worse than 6.63× its length, and a scheduler that promises “by 6am” cannot be argued into it.

A whole fleet is usually reclaimed together, and this model assumes independence. Preemptions are correlated: the provider reclaims a capacity pool, so a hundred workers on one instance type in one zone go at once. That breaks the model in three ways at the same time. The independent-hazard assumption is wrong, so a distributed job that needs all its workers has a much worse effective rate than any single worker’s. Every survivor restarts simultaneously and reads its checkpoint from the same object store, which is a thundering herd on the storage tier and a retry storm on the control plane if the restarts are not jittered. And you cannot get replacement capacity, because everyone else in that pool is asking for it at the same moment, so the r in every formula above is not five minutes when it matters most. The mitigation is spread — several instance types, several zones — and that is a real design change rather than a config flag.

Checkpoints are bytes, and this model prices only their time. A checkpoint is written somewhere, it is often written every few minutes, and it is usually kept. That is storage capacity, write operations, and possibly bytes crossing a zone boundary — none of which appears in c. On a large model checkpointed every twenty minutes for a week, the storage bill is not a rounding error, and the write-operation cost of many small shards is its own trap. The terms are in cost-per-million-requests; the tiering decision is storage-cost-tiers and is not written yet.

The discount and the preemption rate are not independent, and the model treats them as if they were. The deepest discounts are on the capacity nobody wants — which is also the capacity most likely to be reclaimed. So a rig slider that raises the discount without raising the rate is optimistic, and the correct way to use the grid above is to read the rate you measure rather than the discount you are offered. Measure it: the mean time between preemptions on your instance type in your zone is observable, and it is the only input in this lesson you cannot substitute a guess for.

The commitment half hides a longer risk than its arithmetic shows. u > 1 − d prices the hours. It does not price being locked to an instance family for three years, which is longer than a hardware generation — the commitment can outlive the machine you wanted it for, and a workload that moves to newer hardware strands it. It also does not distinguish a commitment denominated in instance-hours from one denominated in dollars per hour of any eligible usage; at the same headline discount the second is strictly more flexible, and the difference is not small. That is reserved-capacity-lock-in, and it is not written yet.

And the two break-evens on this page do not compose. A commitment and spot are not alternatives on the same axis: the sensible shape for most fleets is a committed floor at the demand percentile, on demand for the shape above it, and spot for the interruptible work alongside — three mechanisms, three different decisions. Nothing above tells you how to size all three at once, because that needs your demand curve and your interruption tolerance together, and any answer I gave would be a plausible number rather than a computed one.

07

In the wild

The square root is fifty years old and it came from supercomputers, not clouds. John Young derived sqrt(2c/λ) as a first-order approximation to the optimal checkpoint interval in 1974, for machines whose problem was hardware failure rather than a provider reclaiming capacity. John Daly extended it to a higher-order estimate in 2006 and characterised exactly where the first-order form degrades — which is the same boundary this lesson measures at +15.7%. The mechanism is identical because the model is identical: a memoryless interruption, a save that costs something, and a loss of half an interval. Spot instances are a new price list on a very old piece of arithmetic.

The interruption notice is a documented platform contract, and it is not the same everywhere. One large provider documents about two minutes between deciding to reclaim a spot instance and taking it; two others document roughly thirty seconds for the equivalent product. That single number decides which side of this lesson you are on, because a checkpoint that fits inside it removes the lost-work term entirely. Check the current figure for your provider and your product — the values move, and a four-times difference in the notice window is a four-times difference in what “small enough” means.

Systems built specifically to train large models on preemptible capacity exist, and what they chose is informative. Varuna (EuroSys 2022) makes large-model training work on cheap, preemptible, low-bandwidth instances by reconfiguring the pipeline when workers disappear. Bamboo (NSDI 2023) goes further and does redundant computation rather than more frequent checkpointing — each pipeline stage speculatively computes its neighbour’s work, so a preemption costs a partial recomputation instead of an interval. Both are the same recognition arrived at from the other end: on preemptible capacity the checkpoint interval is a first-class design parameter, and if you can get the lost-work term to zero by some other means, you should.

Spot prices are set by a policy, not only by a market. Ben-Yehuda and colleagues’ 2013 analysis of EC2 spot pricing found long stretches in which the price was not consistent with being driven purely by supply and demand. The practical reading is not conspiratorial: it is that the discount is not a measurement of scarcity, so you cannot infer your preemption rate from the price you were offered. The rate has to be observed, and it is the input this lesson is most sensitive to.

And the commitment break-even is the oldest idea here, sold outside computing for a century. A season ticket at 40% off the daily fare pays above 60% of days travelled. A gym membership, a maintenance contract, a leased van against a rented one: every single one is u > 1 − d, with the price cancelling. The reason it goes wrong in cloud bills and not in commuting is that nobody knows their utilisation — a commuter counts days, and an organisation that cannot divide its bill by workload cannot count hours. Which makes cost attribution a prerequisite for this entire page rather than a refinement of it.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Spot's cost is its price times a stretch factor — wall-clock seconds per useful second — so the discount it must beat is 1 − 1/stretch, which is exactly the share of the clock that produced nothing
  • A preemption destroys the work since the last checkpoint, and because arrivals are memoryless it lands uniformly inside the interval, so the expected loss is about half an interval
  • Waste is the checkpoint overhead c/T plus the lost work λT/2, which has a minimum at T* ≈ sqrt(2c/λ) — and T* is always below the mean time between preemptions, whatever the checkpoint costs
  • Un-checkpointable work is not a separate regime but the same formula with the interval equal to the whole job, so what decides the verdict is the preemption rate times the uninterrupted run length
  • A checkpoint that fits inside the interruption notice can be written when the notice arrives, which destroys no work at all and makes the best periodic interval infinite
  • A commitment at discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d, exactly, with no price and no workload in it
  • With varying demand, commit up to the d-th percentile of the demand curve, because the k-th unit of capacity earns a commitment only if it is busy more than 1 − d of the time
  • A committed machine kept busy can be cheaper per useful hour than a preemptible one being restarted, so the lowest per-hour price is not the lowest cost

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A six-hour nightly job holds its state in memory and cannot resume. Capacity is reclaimed on average every two hours and a replacement takes five minutes. Spot is 70% off. Both the bill and the schedule go wrong — by how much, and why?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Wall clock per useful second is (e^λW − 1)(1/λ + r)/W, and with λW = 3 that is 6.63. So the bill: 180 useful hours a month become 1,193 machine-hours, and at the discounted $1.20 an hour that is $1,431 against $720 on demand — 1.99×, not 0.30×. And the schedule, which nobody predicts: six hours of work takes 39.8 hours of wall clock, so a nightly job stops being nightly. The reason both go wrong at once is that the discount buys hours and the job needed work. Every failed attempt is billed in full.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Your checkpoint takes four minutes and capacity is reclaimed every two hours. How often should you checkpoint, and what does getting it wrong by a factor of two cost you?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Every 28 minutes. The waste is c/T for the writes plus about λT/2 for the work a preemption destroys; differentiate and the minimum is at T* ≈ sqrt(2c/λ) — the square root of twice the checkpoint cost over the preemption rate. Here that approximation says 31 minutes and the exact answer is 28.4, so it is 9.2% too long. But the curve is flat at the bottom: being 9.2% wrong about the interval costs 0.2% on the bill, and anywhere from 0.8× to 1.25× of the optimum is within 1%. What is not symmetric is the direction — eight times too often costs 101% more waste, eight times too rarely costs 318%. If you must guess, guess short.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Two identical jobs cannot checkpoint at all. One runs for twenty minutes, the other for six hours. Capacity is reclaimed every six hours and every two hours respectively. What discount does each need?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    3.6% and 84.9% — a factor of 24, from the same formula with nothing changed but the numbers. That is the finding: "un-checkpointable" is not a regime, it is the corner of the same curve where the interval equals the whole job, and what actually decides the verdict is λ × the uninterrupted run length. At λW = 0.056 the stretch is 1.04; at λW = 3 it is 6.63. The actionable inverse is a runbook line: at a 70% discount and one reclaim every two hours, nothing longer than 4.0 hours belongs on spot without a resume, and even at 90% off the limit is only 7.1 hours.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Your checkpoint takes twenty seconds and the provider gives two minutes' notice before reclaiming. What changes, and by how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Everything, and by 5.4×. Write the checkpoint when the notice arrives and no work is ever lost — so periodic checkpointing becomes pure overhead and the best interval is infinite. The stretch collapses to 1 + λ(c + r): the drain plus the restart, per preemption, and nothing else. On the same machine that needs 10.98% off with periodic checkpointing every 8.7 minutes, draining on the notice needs 4.26%. This is the single largest lever in the whole model and it is an engineering property, not a pricing one: make the checkpoint smaller than the notice window. Note the notice is not two minutes everywhere — one large provider documents two minutes and two others document thirty seconds, which moves the threshold by 4×.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A team runs a fleet whose demand swings between 4 and 35 nodes, averaging 17.4 — half of peak. A three-year commitment is 60% off. How much do they commit to, and what is the mistake in both directions?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    19 nodes, which is the 60th percentile of their own demand curve — because the k-th node earns a commitment only if it is busy more than 1 − d of the time, so the level where P(demand ≥ k) = 1 − d is where committing stops paying. That is $29,892 a month against $50,080 on demand only, a 40.3% saving. Both mistakes are common. Committing to peak — 35 nodes, which is what "we reserved the fleet" usually means — costs 34.9% more than the optimum, and at a 35% discount it is worse than committing nothing at all. Committing to nothing leaves the whole 40.3% on the table. And the simple version of the rule is worth memorising on its own: a commitment at discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d, so 40% off needs 60% utilisation.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    You are backfilling a column across 400 million rows. The database is failed over for patching every few hours, and an in-flight transaction is rolled back when that happens. Each batch commit costs an fsync plus index maintenance — call it two seconds. Apply this lesson to choosing the batch size, and say what the two wrong answers are.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The mapping is exact, with different nouns. Checkpoint cost becomes the commit — the fsync and index maintenance you pay once per batch whatever its size. Preemption becomes the failover, which rolls back the batch in flight and destroys exactly the work since the last commit. Restart delay becomes reconnecting and re-establishing the cursor. So the total time per useful row is commit overhead per row plus the expected rollback, the rollback costs about half a batch because the failover lands uniformly inside it, and the batch duration that minimises the total is sqrt(2 × commit cost ÷ failover rate) — with a hard ceiling at the mean time between failovers, since a batch longer than that will essentially never commit. Both wrong answers follow: one row per transaction pays the commit 400 million times and is the "checkpoint constantly" corner; one giant transaction is the "never checkpoint" corner, and if the run exceeds the failover interval it does not merely cost more, it never finishes — the same exponential wall as an un-resumable six-hour job. There is a bonus term the cloud version does not have: a long transaction also holds locks and grows the undo log, so the right-hand penalty is steeper here than the formula alone says.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: work accumulates in a place that can be taken away without warning, saving it costs something each time, so the loss per interruption is about half the interval since the last save — and total waste therefore has a minimum at an interval proportional to the square root of the save cost over the interruption rate. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Placing protection on a lead climb is the sharpest one. A fall costs you roughly twice the distance to your last piece of gear, placing a piece costs time and strength, and the whole skill is choosing the spacing — climbers do this by feel and the feel is a square root: they place more often exactly where the fall risk per metre is higher, which is what dividing by λ means. Others: inspection intervals on a production line, where inspecting costs a stoppage and skipping it means a defect scraps everything made since the last check, and the economically optimal interval has been derived in that square-root form since the 1930s; stocktaking and bank reconciliation frequency, where the count costs labour and the discrepancy grows with the gap; a surveyor transcribing a field book, or any records kept in a medium that can be lost. The second half of this lesson transfers to something much more everyday: a season ticket at 40% off the daily fare pays if you travel more than 60% of the days — a commitment at discount d beats paying per use above utilisation 1 − d, exactly, with the fare cancelling out. And the marginal version is how many seats a restaurant leases versus how many it could fill on a Friday: you take the space that is busy more often than the discount, not the space that would be busy at the peak.
    Did you have that?
