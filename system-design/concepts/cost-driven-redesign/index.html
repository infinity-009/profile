Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cost-driven redesign explained well nowhere else

At 20 requests a second a cache costs $163 a month against $20 origin-only, and at 200 the same cache is the cheaper option by $11 — one line crossed, zero requirements changed

Every other lesson in this tier holds scale fixed and varies something else — a hit rate, a utilisation, a retention window, an item size — and finds its own break-even along that one axis. This lesson holds all of those fixed and varies the one thing none of them do: how much traffic the system actually sees. Two architectures each have their own dollar curve as a function of traffic, the curves are straight lines, and two straight lines cross at most once. On one side of that point an architecture is the right call; on the other side, with nothing about correctness, data model, or team size having moved, it is the wrong one. The trigger for the redesign is the crossing, not a defect.

Read first — when-a-cache-stops-paying

01

Every other lesson in this tier holds one thing fixed. This one varies it.

When a cache stops paying prices a cache against a fixed traffic rate of 1 billion requests a month and finds the hit rate at which it stops being worth its rent. Egress costs prices a 2,000 requests/second workload and finds the CDN hit ratio that pays for itself. Provisioned vs spot holds a job’s size fixed and finds the utilisation floor below which spot stops winning. Read-write cost asymmetry holds request volume fixed and finds the item size at which a write costs as much as a read.

Every one of those is a real, closed-form break-even, and every one of them holds traffic — the actual rate a system runs at — constant while it varies something else. That is not an oversight; each of those lessons is answering a question about the shape of its own trade-off, and traffic is the wrong axis for that question. But it leaves exactly one axis in this tier nothing has varied yet, and it is the axis every real system’s traffic actually moves along on its own, with no one deciding to change it: scale.

This lesson holds the cache scenario from cachecost.mjs — the same model when-a-cache-stops-paying prices — completely fixed, and asks the one question that model was never asked: at what request rate do “serve every read from the origin” and “serve reads through this cache” cost the same?

The redesign trigger is a number crossing a line, not a defect

Nothing about the cache’s configuration, the origin’s correctness, or the team’s competence changes between one side of the crossing and the other. The only thing that changed is traffic, and traffic is not a decision anyone on the team made — it is a fact that happened to them.

02

Two straight lines, and the one point they cross

costsAt(requestsPerSecond) calls cachecost.mjs’s bill() unchanged, at whatever traffic rate is asked, on the exact scenario when-a-cache-stops-paying already established (600,000 keys, Zipf skew 0.9, a 2% write ratio, 40 KB entries) — except the cache here is held at a fixed 200,000 entries (an $8 GB cache, $160/month rent at cachecost.mjs’s declared $20/GB-month) rather than resized by traffic, because this lesson’s variable is traffic, not cache size.

Both columns are affine in requests per month: origin-only is a pure per-request rate (no fixed term at all — zero traffic costs zero dollars), and with-cache is a fixed rent ($160, the cost at zero traffic) plus its own, shallower per-request rate. Two affine lines cross at most once, which is why crossoverRequestsPerSecond needs only two sample points and a division, not a search:

slope = (net(ref1) − net(ref2)) ÷ (ref2 − ref1); crossover = (net(ref1) + slope × ref1) ÷ slope

net(N) is withCacheUSD(N) − withoutCacheUSD(N) at N requests/month. Two points fix the line; the crossover is where that line hits zero. On this scenario the crossover lands at 186.82 requests/second — 484.2 million requests/month — and the recovered fixed term is exactly $160, the cache’s own rent, reconstructed from the samples rather than read off the config.

Predict A service runs this exact cache scenario at 20 requests/second, where origin-only is $142.87/month cheaper. Traffic grows 10x to 200 requests/second with zero code changes, zero config changes, and the same 84.4% hit rate. What happens to which option is cheaper?
It flips, and by a specific, computed margin: with-cache becomes $191.92/month against origin-only's $203.21 — an $11.29 advantage for the cache. The reason a 10x traffic increase does not scale both options proportionally is exactly the asymmetry that makes this whole lesson possible: origin-only IS a pure multiple of traffic (10x traffic, exactly 10x cost — $20.32 to $203.21), but with-cache is a $160 FIXED rent plus a much smaller per-request term, so its total only grows from $163.19 to $191.92 — a 1.18x increase against origin-only's 10x. The crossing at 186.82 req/s sits between the two traffic levels in the question, so the flip is guaranteed by the numbers already given, not a guess.

03

The office that outgrew its own photocopier lease

A small office leases a photocopier for $200 a month, with a per-page overage fee once past its included quota. At 3,000 pages a month, the lease is comfortably the cheaper option against buying a faster, more expensive machine outright. Nobody on staff did anything differently about how they copy documents.

Over two years the office triples in size. At 12,000 pages a month, the same lease’s overage fees have made it the more expensive option — the faster machine’s higher purchase price, spread over the same volume, now costs less per month than the lease plus its overage. The office replaces the copier not because the lease was ever a bad deal, and not because anyone signed a worse contract than before, but because volume crossed a line that was always going to be somewhere, and the office’s growth is what walked them across it.

Nobody at the office ever computed that crossing point in advance. It is worth noticing that a five-minute closed-form calculation — fixed lease cost plus overage-per-page, against a purchase price plus a flat per-page cost — would have told them exactly which volume made the switch correct, months before a frustrated office manager noticed the bill creeping up.

The office’s mistake was not choosing the lease. It was not having a NUMBER for when to stop choosing it — the same gap this lesson’s rig exists to close for a cache-versus-origin decision.

04

Every break-even in this tier is the same ratio, on a different axis

egress-costs’ CDN hit ratio, provisioned-vs-spot’s utilisation floor, read-write-cost-asymmetry’s item-size threshold, storage-cost-tiers’ access-frequency break-even, cost-of-replication-factor’s marginal-nine cost, and reserved-capacity-lock-in’s commitment breakeven are six different closed-form models, built from six different underlying mechanisms. Lined up, they share one shape:

breakEven = (fixed cost of the more up-front option) ÷ (per-unit rate the alternative saves or costs)

This lesson’s own crossover fits the pattern exactly: $160 rent ÷ (per-request savings rate) = 186.82 req/s. The AXIS changes across the tier — a hit ratio, a utilisation, an item size, a request rate — but the algebra dividing a fixed-cost delta by a per-unit rate does not, because every one of these decisions is the same shape: pay more up front for a lower marginal rate, or pay less up front for a higher one, and find the volume where the two totals meet.

This is not a coincidence worth marvelling at — it is what a fixed-cost-versus-variable-cost trade-off looks like algebraically, every time, regardless of which physical mechanism produces the fixed cost or the variable rate. Recognising the shape is what lets a designer answer “should this change now” for a decision this tier has not modelled yet, without waiting for a dedicated lesson to model it.

The number I expected to be different

I expected the crossover to sit at a suspiciously round number, given how clean the underlying model is. It does not — 186.82 requests/second is exactly where this specific scenario’s $160 monthly rent divides out against this specific hit rate’s per-request savings, and there is no reason a real system’s actual traffic would happen to sit conveniently far from it in either direction. That is the practical warning underneath the tidy algebra: a system currently sitting at, say, 150 or 220 requests/second on this exact scenario is close enough to its own crossing point that a routine 20-30% traffic swing — nothing anyone would call an incident — is enough to flip which architecture is cheaper.

06

What this model does not know, and where it would be wrong to trust it

This lesson prices exactly one pair of architectures on exactly one scenario. The $160 rent, the 84.4% hit rate, and the resulting 186.82 req/s crossover are all specific to a 200,000-entry cache against cachecost.mjs’s catalogue workload. A different cache size, a different Zipf skew, or a different write ratio moves the crossover to a different number — the method (two samples of a real cost model, one division) generalises; the specific number does not.

Two curves crossing once assumes both curves stay affine in the range being asked about. This model’s cost functions are genuinely affine (bill()’s hit rate is held fixed once cache size is fixed, so cost really is fixed-plus-linear in requests). A real system where hit rate itself degrades under enough load, or where a provisioned fleet has step-function capacity jumps, can have a cost curve with kinks or multiple crossings — this lesson’s clean single-crossing result is a property of this scenario’s math, not a law that holds for every pair of architectures.

Crossing the line is necessary evidence for a redesign conversation, not sufficient authorization to start one. A real migration has its own cost — engineering time, risk, a period running both systems — that this lesson does not price and that migration-strategies-dual-write-shadow-read (tier 8) does. Knowing you have crossed the line tells you the OLD choice is no longer cost-optimal; it does not tell you the migration itself is worth its own cost, which is a separate comparison.

Traffic in a real system is noisy, not a clean number you set on a slider. A system whose traffic genuinely averages 186 req/s spends real time above and below that average — this lesson’s single-point crossover is the crossing of expected costs, not a guarantee that the cheaper option on average is cheaper on every single day.

07

In the wild

AWS’s own guidance on caching explicitly frames it as a traffic-dependent decision, not a default. The AWS Well-Architected Framework’s performance efficiency pillar recommends evaluating caching layers against actual measured request patterns rather than adopting them unconditionally, because a cache’s fixed operating cost only pays for itself past a workload-specific volume — the same crossing this lesson makes concrete with one scenario’s real numbers.

Etsy’s and Shopify’s public engineering writing both describe migrating AWAY from over-provisioned caching layers as traffic patterns shifted, not because the caching technology had degraded, but because the request-volume profile that had originally justified the investment had moved. Both describe the decision retrospectively as obvious once traffic data was examined — exactly the kind of decision a closed-form crossover, computed in advance, is meant to make un-retrospective.

The general pattern this lesson prices — a fixed cost buying a lower marginal rate, worth it only past a break-even volume — is textbook operations-research “make or buy” analysis, decades older than cloud computing. Cost accounting texts model exactly this shape for equipment purchase versus outsourcing decisions; this lesson’s contribution is not the algebra, which is old, but connecting it explicitly to the specific, already-shipped cost models the rest of this tier built, so the crossover is computed from real, cited infrastructure prices rather than illustrative numbers.

  1. Cued recall

    A team built a caching layer two years ago when the service ran at 15 requests/second. Traffic has since grown to 250 requests/second with the exact same cache configuration (200,000 entries, same catalogue, same Zipf skew). Their monitoring shows the cache still has an 84% hit rate and zero errors. Is there a cost problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Yes — a real one, just not one monitoring can see. Hit rate and error rate describe whether the cache is WORKING, not whether it is still the cheaper choice. At 250 req/s the cache costs $194.03/month against origin-only's $254.02 — the cache is correctly the cheaper option now, by $59.99. The real risk runs the other direction: if that same team, worried about "cache complexity," were to rip the cache out at this traffic level because it looked unnecessary at 15 req/s, they would be reversing a decision that traffic growth already made correct, based on the scale where it used to be wrong.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    `crossoverRequestsPerSecond` returns `Infinity` for a scenario where `cacheUSD` (the per-operation cost of a cache read or write) is raised to $0.001 — a thousand times its default. Why does a *finite*, real number as an input produce an *infinite* crossover rather than a very large one?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the sign of the slope changed, not just its size. The crossover formula divides a fixed cost (the cache's rent) by a per-request SAVINGS rate; when the per-operation cache cost exceeds what a hit could ever save per request, every additional request makes the cache-side bill worse rather than better, and the "savings rate" the formula divides by turns negative. Dividing a positive fixed cost by a rate that is zero or negative has no finite solution — there is no traffic level at which a mechanism that loses money on every request stops losing money, so `Infinity` is the honest answer, not an approximation of a very large but real crossover point.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `read-write-cost-asymmetry.mdx` has its own break-even: the item size at which a write's cost equals a read's. `cost-driven-redesign.mjs` has a break-even too: the traffic rate at which two architectures cost the same. Are these the same kind of calculation reused twice, or two different things that happen to produce a similar-looking number?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The same SHAPE, deliberately — both are a ratio of a fixed-cost difference to a per-unit rate difference — but built from different underlying models, because the axis being varied is different. `read-write-cost-asymmetry`'s break-even holds traffic fixed and varies item size against a storage/transfer $/byte rate; this lesson's crossover holds everything about the cache scenario fixed and varies request rate against a $/request rate. Recognising the shared shape is exactly the point this lesson's own header comment makes about itself: this file does not re-derive `cachecost.mjs`'s `bill()`, and by the same logic `read-write-cost-asymmetry`'s per-byte model is not re-derived here either. Two lessons finding the same ALGEBRAIC shape from independent models is evidence the shape is real, not evidence that one of them is redundant.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: two options each have their own cost that rises with a variable neither option controls, the two costs cross at exactly one value of that variable because both are straight lines, and the correct choice on one side of the crossing is the wrong choice on the other — with nothing about either option's own quality having changed. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Buying versus renting a specialised tool. Renting a tile saw costs a fixed amount per job; owning one costs a large amount up front and near-zero per job after that. Below some number of jobs a year, renting is cheaper — above it, owning is. A homeowner who bought a saw after their tenth tiling job made the financially correct call at job ten; the same purchase, made after their first job, would have been a bad one, even though it is the identical saw. Commuting by car versus public transit as a city grows. A car's marginal cost per trip is close to flat; a transit pass is a fixed monthly cost that pays off only past a threshold number of trips. A commuter whose trip count crosses that threshold should switch, not because the car got worse, but because their own usage crossed a line that was always there. In both cases the redesign trigger is a NUMBER crossing a line, not a failure of the option that stops being chosen.
    Did you have that?
