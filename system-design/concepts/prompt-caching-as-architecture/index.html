Tier 12 · Agentic systems and graph retrieval Prompt caching as architecture explained well nowhere else

A twelve-token trace id at the front of your prompt multiplies the bill by 6.65× — $2,940 per thousand turns against $442 — and the fix is to move it to the end

A prefix cache matches from the front of the request and stops at the first byte that differs, so the cost of a variable byte is set by the mass behind it and not by its size. Which makes the ORDER of a prompt an architectural decision with a price attached — and makes the remedy for a mutation almost never "remove it".

Read first — kv-cachesemantic-caching

01

Twelve tokens, added for debugging, and a bill that went up 6.65×

A platform team runs a coding agent. Someone adds a per-request trace id as the first line of every prompt so support can correlate a complaint with a request. Twelve tokens. It ships on a Tuesday.

The input bill for the week goes from $442 per thousand turns to $2,940.

Nothing else changed. No more requests, no bigger prompts, no model change — the requests are twelve tokens larger, which is 0.0053% more content. Time to first token goes from 0.77 seconds to 4.30 seconds, and the on-call engineer looking at the latency graph and the finance dashboard has no reason to connect either of them to a debugging aid.

The same twelve tokens, moved to the last line of the prompt, cost nothing at all.

02

The belief this breaks: a small addition to a prompt is a small cost

It is not the size of what you changed. It is the size of what is behind it.

cached = Σ tᵢ · Πj≤i (1 − rⱼ)

A prefix cache matches the request against what it has stored from the front, token by token, and stops at the first difference. So segment i is served from cache only if segment i is unchanged and every segment ahead of it is unchanged too — which is that product. A segment with churn rⱼ = 1 zeroes every term after it, whatever its size, and however stable those later segments are on their own.

Two consequences, and neither is what a reader expects from “caching”.

A mutation’s price is the mass behind it. Twelve tokens at position 1 and 2,500 tokens at position 1 cost the same, because the priced quantity is neither of them — it is the 227,600 tokens whose cache entry they invalidated. This is the whole lesson and it is easiest to see as a budget:

And the fix is a reorder, not a removal. If the price is set by position, position is the knob. The trace id is genuinely useful; nothing about this argues for deleting it. Moving it to the last line takes the same twelve tokens to 95.4% cached — the identical bytes on the wire, at one eighth of the price.

03

The intuition: a correction held for the next edition

Before phototypesetting, a newspaper column was a physical stack of metal type. Each line was cast as a single slug and the slugs were stacked in a column.

Correcting a word meant recasting its line — one slug, cheap. Correcting a word by inserting one meant that line no longer fitted, so the last word pushed onto the next line, which pushed onto the next, all the way to the bottom of the column. A one-word insertion at the top of a column was a column’s worth of work. The same insertion in the final line was one line.

Compositors knew exactly what this implied, and it is worth noticing that they did not conclude “avoid corrections”:

  • Corrections were batched and held for the next edition, so one reflow paid for many changes.
  • Late corrections were preferred over early ones — not because they mattered less, but because they cost less.
  • When an early change was unavoidable, the fix was to absorb it locally: pad or trim within the line so the following lines never moved. Change the content, protect the boundary.

That third one is the move this lesson is about, and it is the one that has no equivalent in ordinary caching intuition. You do not make the mutation smaller. You move the boundary so there is nothing behind it.

04

The mechanism: what order maximises the cached prefix, and why it is a sort

The objective is the law in slot 2, and it has an exact optimum.

Consider swapping two adjacent segments A and B. The change in expected cached tokens is proportional to t_A(1−r_A)·r_B − t_B(1−r_B)·r_A, which is positive exactly when t_A(1−r_A)/r_A > t_B(1−r_B)/r_B. Every adjacent pair, one comparison — so sorting on t(1−r)/r descending is globally optimal. This is Smith’s rule from scheduling, arriving in a place it was not written for, and the tests grade it against full permutation enumeration over 200 random segment sets because an adjacent-exchange argument is exactly the kind that is right in the small and wrong in the large.

Read the index and it says three things, in increasing order of usefulness:

r = 0 makes the index infinite. Stable content leads unconditionally, at any size. A 1-token segment that never changes outranks a 1,000,000-token segment with any churn at all. This is the rule people already half-know as “put the system prompt first”, and it is the only part of the ordering that needs no measurement.

r = 1 makes the index zero. Anything that changes every request goes last, and there is no tie-break among them worth having — once you are behind the mutation you are already paying.

In between, the index decides, and it is not size order. A 57,700-token append-only log outranks a 150,000-token document block that gets edited on a quarter of requests. That is the case where a real team gets it wrong, because the document block feels like the thing to protect: it is the biggest segment and the most expensive to build. Measured on the median turn, that one swap is 72.6% cached against 79.0%.

The four habits below are all real, all common, and all content-preserving — every one of them can be fixed by moving bytes rather than deleting them. The prompt is the measured median Copilot agentic turn: 227,600 tokens, of which the paper reports 95.4% cached.

The numbers: forty turns of an agent session, four layouts

One turn priced in isolation understates this, because a session’s prompt grows. Forty turns at the sizes established in context engineering: a 6,200-token resident block, 6,460 tokens appended per turn, prompt growing from 12,660 to 264,600 tokens — 20.9× across the session.

“Tokens re-written” is the column to read, because it is the one with no upside. Those are tokens the model had already processed, whose result was already stored, sent again and charged again at a surcharge. Five million of them in a forty-turn session. Not slow, not risky — paid for twice, and invisible on every dashboard because the prompt sizes are identical in all four rows.

The append-only design pays for each token exactly once, and that is a stronger property than a high hit rate. Turn 40 sends a 264,600-token prompt and pays the write price for 6,460 of them: the tool results and the reply that did not exist last turn, plus the question. The prompt grew 21× and the written part did not grow at all. That is the shape worth designing for — not “most of it is cached” but “nothing is ever written twice”.

The classic RAG template is the expensive one, and it is expensive for a reason that has nothing to do with retrieval. Injecting retrieved documents between the resident block and the history is the default in every tutorial. It costs 6.9×, because the injection point is ahead of the history and the history is where the mass is. Retrieval belongs after the log, or — if the model needs it before the log to reason well — the log belongs in a separate, stable position. Same documents, same reasoning, one eighth of the bill.

06

The trade-off: everything that manages context for you destroys the prefix

Compaction saves money and spends cache, and the two show up on different dashboards. Rewriting the conversation log into a summary is the standard answer to a growing prompt, and it works: at a 60k cap the same forty-turn session costs $1.73 instead of $2.94 — 41% less — because five of the turns carry a 3,000-token summary instead of a log. But a rewritten prefix is a cache miss by definition. On the turn compaction fires, only the 6,200-token resident block survives and the rest of the request is a cold prefill: mean TTFT across the session goes 0.95s → 1.57s, and 2.84s on the compaction turn itself against 1.01s the turn before. Push the cap to 40k and it sharpens into something absurd on its face: the compaction turn’s prompt is 2.4× smaller than the previous turn’s and its bill is 1.08× higher. Compaction is not a cost optimisation with a latency cost attached; it is a trade of cache for context, and which of those you are short of is the actual decision.

Every other context-management technique has the same shape, and the good ones are late-binding. Just-in-time context fetches when needed rather than preloading — which is right on attention grounds and also right here, provided the fetched result lands at the end. Memory tiers inject facts, and where they inject decides the price. Reranking reorders retrieved chunks, and a reranker that changes chunk order between turns has broken the prefix for a quality gain nobody priced. The rule that survives all of them: anything that can change must sit behind everything that cannot, and a system that manages context by rewriting history in place has no cheap position left to put it.

A cache entry is a clock as well as a prefix, and the longer TTL is a bet rather than an upgrade. Entries expire; with exponential think-time of mean g and a TTL of m minutes, the miss share is exp(−m/g), and the break-evens are unkind. The 5-minute cache stops paying for itself at a 20.4-minute mean gap — past that, the 1.25× writes on misses cost more than never caching at all. The 1-hour cache at 2× writes holds out to 80.3 minutes. But it beats the 5-minute cache only up to a 109.5-minute mean gap: past that, almost everything expires under either TTL, and the cheaper write wins. So there is a window where the long TTL is decisively right — at a ten-minute cadence it is 0.105× against 0.798×, because the 5-minute cache is missing 60.7% of the time — and a region on each side where it loses money. You cannot pick a TTL without knowing your cadence, and cadence is a property of your users.

And the honest limit of all of it: this models one cache, and production has many. The break-evens above assume your request lands where its prefix is. Behind a load balancer it may not — which is the whole subject of prefix-aware routing in the inference gateway, and the reason the measured hit rate in the Copilot traces falls from 90% within a turn to 55% at a turn boundary and 8% on a model switch. A perfectly ordered prompt routed to a cold replica is a cold prefill. The layout is necessary and it is not sufficient.

07

In the wild

The measured numbers here are production traces, not a model of production. The median agentic turn — 227,600 prompt tokens, 1,900 output tokens, 95.4% of the prompt served from cache — is from Microsoft Research’s analysis of GitHub Copilot traffic (arXiv:2608.00101). The calibration in this lesson is checked to reproduce that 95.4% exactly rather than approximately, because it is the anchor everything else hangs from. The 1,900 output tokens against 227,600 input is worth sitting with on its own: an agentic turn is 120× more reading than writing, so input pricing is the pricing, and the input multiplier is the only lever with real leverage on it.

Providers have made the write surcharge explicit, which turns this from folklore into arithmetic. Anthropic prices cache reads at 0.1× the base input rate, 5-minute writes at 1.25× and 1-hour writes at 2.0×. That spread is what makes the ordering question quantitative: the difference between the best and worst layout of identical content is 8.2× on the input line, and you can compute it from a template without deploying anything.

Which is why “the prompt is a data structure with an ordering constraint” is now a normal thing for a framework to assert. Explicit cache-breakpoint markers in the Anthropic API, prefix-aware routing in vLLM and llm-d, and the convention in every serious agent framework that tool definitions are pinned and sorted, all encode the same rule from the same direction. The reason tool-schema serialisation order became a documented concern — a detail that would be beneath notice anywhere else — is the row in slot 4: an unpinned map iteration order costs $2,911 per thousand turns against $442, for byte- identical content.

The failure mode this lesson is really about is that none of it shows up as a regression. No test fails when a timestamp moves into the system prompt. Prompt sizes are unchanged; outputs are unchanged; correctness is unchanged. What changes is a multiplier on a line item and a few seconds of prefill, and the two people who would notice are looking at different dashboards. The cheapest safeguard is an assertion, not a metric: pin the prefix in a test, and fail the build when the first 10,000 tokens of the assembled prompt are not byte-identical across two consecutive assemblies.

  6. Near transfer

    Same shape, different domain: a pianist who has memorised a sonata. Where is the prefix, and why does the position of a wrong note decide how expensive it is?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A memorised piece is stored as a chained motor sequence — each phrase cued by the one before it — so it is a prefix structure, and every performer knows the consequence: you can start from a phrase boundary and you cannot start from the middle of a phrase. Change a fingering in bar 4 and everything after it has to be re-learned, because the chain that reached bar 5 was cued by the old bar 4. Change the same fingering in the final bar and the cost is one bar. Which is why the practised remedy is not "avoid changes" but re-chunk: insert an entry point just ahead of the change, so the re-learning starts there instead of at the top. That is identical to moving a variable byte to the back — the mutation stays, and the mass behind it goes to nearly zero.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: in a structure whose validity is prefix-dependent, the cost of a change is proportional to the mass downstream of it and independent of the change's own size — so the cheapest correction is the one made latest, and the right response to an unavoidable early change is to move the boundary, not to shrink the change.     How sure are you, before you look?
    A frameshift mutation. Inserting a single base early in a gene shifts every codon after it and garbles the whole protein; the same single-base insertion after the stop codon costs nothing, and a three-base insertion anywhere costs only itself because it preserves the frame. One base, and the price is set entirely by how much sequence follows. Others: hot-metal typesetting, where a word inserted at the top of a column forced every line below it to be reset and a word at the bottom forced one — the reason corrections were held for the next edition rather than made immediately; numbered clauses in a contract, where an inserted clause renumbers every cross-reference after it, which is why drafters append and why sub-clause lettering exists at all; a marshalling yard, where a wagon inserted mid-consist means uncoupling everything behind it; and a film reel before digital editing, where a splice at reel start meant re-timing every cue after it. In all of them the same two remedies appear and only the second scales: batch the changes, or move them to the end.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
