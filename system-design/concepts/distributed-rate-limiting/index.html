Tier 6 · Running it in production Distributed limits explained well nowhere else

Ten nodes enforcing 100 requests a second do not enforce 1,000 — they enforce anything from 100 to 1,000

Divide a limit by the node count and a customer sitting exactly on their published rate gets three times as many 429s, because a per-node quota is a bulkhead and it costs the same statistical multiplexing. Coordinate to fix it and you buy a round trip per request and a new thing that can be down. Every production limiter is approximate; the only real question is which direction its error points.

Read first — rate-limitingbulkheads

01

The customer is inside their limit, and can prove it

You publish 1,000 requests per second per API key and you run ten API nodes behind a load balancer. The limiter is the one from the previous lesson — a token bucket, correct, tested — and each node enforces 100 requests per second, because ten nodes times a hundred is a thousand.

A customer paces their client at exactly 1,000 requests per second. They get 76 rejections a second. They open a ticket with a graph showing they never exceeded your published rate, and they are right: over any rolling second they sent 1,000, and you refused 7.6% of it.

A single shared counter of 1,000 would have refused 2.5% of the same traffic. Not zero — real traffic arrives unevenly, so a client averaging 1,000 per second genuinely exceeds 1,000 in some seconds and is correctly refused. But 7.6% against 2.5% is three times as many 429s, at identical total quota and identical total load. Nothing is misconfigured. Nothing is overloaded. The ten quotas add up to exactly the number you published.

Then two more things happen, both of which follow from the same arithmetic.

Their traffic stops arriving evenly. They enable HTTP keep-alive, so their connections pin to whichever nodes they landed on, and one of your ten nodes starts receiving twice its share. Admitted throughput drops to 88.1%. In that state your fleet is refusing 119 requests a second while 119 tokens a second go unspent on other nodes — the two numbers are equal, because the quota you handed out and the load you offered are the same total. Every rejection has an unused token sitting somewhere the request cannot reach.

You deploy. For ninety seconds the rollout drains eight of the ten nodes. The two survivors enforce 100 each, so your published limit of 1,000 is now 200. Later that week traffic grows and the fleet autoscales to thirty nodes; nobody changes the limiter configuration, and your published limit of 1,000 becomes 3,000. The number you advertise to customers is now a function of your deployment topology, and it is not written down anywhere.

02

Why the quotas do not add up

The belief this breaks is the one that made the design look like arithmetic: N nodes each enforcing limit/N enforces the limit. It does not, and the reason has a name you already have.

A quota of q requests per window W admits a request, remembers it for exactly W seconds, and then forgets it. While all q are remembered, arrivals are refused and gone. That is a loss system — q servers, no queue, blocked calls cleared — and Erlang solved it in 1917 for telephone trunk groups. Erlang’s loss formula is insensitive to how long each hold actually lasts, so with arrivals at rate λ the rejection probability of a quota q is exactly:

P(refused) = B(q, λW)

Erlang-B, with requests-per-window as the slot. Asserted in limiters.test.mjs by running Poisson traffic through the slidingLog limiter and comparing the measured rejection rate against the recursion in bulkhead.mjs — two algorithms, no shared code path, agreeing to under 1% across six configurations.

Which means a per-node quota is a bulkhead, and not by analogy. It is the same formula on the same arithmetic, with requests-per-window where a connection pool has slots. Ten quotas of 100 is four pools of six. So everything bulkheads proves about partitioning a shared resource transfers here unchanged: you bought independence, you paid statistical multiplexing, and the cost is convex in the number of partitions.

The identity is asserted rather than asserted-by-prose: splitting a limit of 1,000 ten ways gives a refusal rate identical to poolingLoss() on a pool of 1,000 divided into ten, to twelve decimal places. If that were not true, reusing the bulkhead model here would be a coincidence.

Predict Ten nodes, a global limit of 1,000 requests per second, each node enforcing 100/s. A client offers exactly 1,000/s and the load balancer routes each request to a uniformly random node. What fraction of the global limit do you actually admit?
92.4%. Each node is a loss system of 100 slots offered 100 erlangs, and B(100,100) = 7.57% refused; one counter of 1,000 slots offered 1,000 erlangs refuses only 2.48%. The quotas do add up — what does not add up is the burst absorption, because a quota of 100 cannot borrow the tokens the other nine nodes are not spending. Same total quota, same total load, 3.05× the rejections.
Show it without answering

Four things to read off it.

The dotted line is not 100%, and that matters. A client averaging its exact limit exceeds that limit in some windows, so a perfect global limiter still refuses 2.5% of it. Confusing “less than the limit” with “wrong” is the most common error in this area; the quantity you care about is the gap between teal and the dotted line, which is what the distribution cost you.

The loss is convex in the node count. One to two nodes costs one percentage point. Ten to fifty costs eight more. Twenty nodes admits 89.5%, fifty admits 84.1%, a hundred admits 78.5% — a fifth of your customers’ legitimate traffic refused by arithmetic alone. This is why the design survives: at three or four nodes it costs almost nothing, and the cost arrives gradually, one autoscaling event at a time.

Imbalance is free under overload and expensive at the limit. Push offered to 200% and every node saturates, so skew stops mattering and you admit essentially the whole limit. That is the uncomfortable half of the result: the scheme is accurate against the client hammering you and inaccurate against the one obeying you.

No scheme puts teal on the line with crimson on zero. Central counter: teal exactly on the dotted line, crimson pinned at 1.00 message per request. Lease: crimson drops to 0.05 and teal drops with it. Broadcast: teal goes above the line, which is a limiter that does not limit, and crimson climbs as N². That is not four implementations to choose between. It is one constraint, seen from four angles.

03

The intuition: ten friends and one bar tab

You have £1,000 behind the bar and ten friends. You want the group to spend at most £1,000.

Give everyone £100 in an envelope. No coordination, no queue at the till, nobody waits. But Ravi drinks more than Sam, so Ravi is dry by nine o’clock while Sam still has £80 in a sealed envelope. The group spends £780 of a £1,000 tab and someone was refused a drink. Nothing went wrong. Envelopes cannot be lent.

Everyone asks you before every drink. Now the group spends exactly £1,000. There is also a permanent queue in front of you, every drink is slower than it needs to be, and when you go to the bathroom the entire group either stops drinking or drinks unmetered. You have become the bar.

Everyone comes to you for £100 at a time. One trip per hundred pounds instead of one per pint. The group cannot overspend, because you hand out the money and you can count. What it loses is the £40 in Sam’s pocket at closing time — you can never get above £1,000 and you will often land below it, by at most what everyone is holding.

Everyone shouts their running total across the room every ten minutes. No trips at all, and nobody has an envelope they cannot spend. But in the ten minutes after the group crosses £1,000, nine people are still buying on a total they heard ten minutes ago, and the group overspends by whatever nine people can get through in ten minutes.

There are exactly two currencies here: how often you talk, and how wrong you are willing to be. You can spend one to buy the other, and there is no third option. Every distributed limiter in production has picked a point on that line — the ones that look exact have simply hidden the round trip.

04

The mechanism: four schemes, four bounds

Vocabulary, once. N is the number of nodes enforcing the limit. L is the global limit, in requests per window W. A quota is a per-node allowance. Routing shares pᵢ are the fractions of traffic each node receives, summing to 1. Everything below is per key, as in the previous lesson.

Quota ÷ N — no coordination, two separate losses

admitted = Σᵢ aᵢ · (1 − B(qᵢ, aᵢ))  where aᵢ = pᵢ · offered, qᵢ = L/N

Two losses stacked, and it matters that they separate. The burst loss is the Erlang term and it exists even with a perfect balancer. The imbalance loss is what is left as the limit grows large, and it is exactly Σ max(0, pᵢ − 1/N) — the total variation distance between your routing distribution and uniform. Asserted both ways in limiters.test.mjs: against traffic routed through N real limiters, and against the total-variation form in the fluid limit.

Two consequences of that second term are worth stating on their own.

The effective global limit spans a factor of N. Send everything to one node and you enforce L/N. Spread it perfectly and you enforce L. Ten quotas of 100 means the real limit is anywhere from 100/s to 997/s, and your load balancer picks, not your configuration file. Envoy states the same arithmetic from the other side: without its local_cluster_rate_limit setting, “the total rate limit of whole gateway will be N × X tokens per second”.

“Random” routing is not even, and the smaller the quota the less even it is. For L requests spread uniformly at random over N nodes, the busiest node’s count is the maximum of N roughly Poisson counts, so:

So the customers on your small plans get the worst enforcement, which is the opposite of what anyone intends. Central counter — exact, and on the critical path

ops/s = request rate  ·  connections held = rate × round trip

Little’s Law, and the number a capacity plan wants. At 100k req/s and a 1 ms round trip that is 100 connections held permanently and 100k operations a second against one datastore; across an availability zone at 5 ms it is 500 connections.

The latency tax is covered in rate limiting, as is the fail-open versus fail-closed choice. What that lesson only gestures at is the shape of the load: limits are per key, the interesting keys are hot by definition, and one counter is one key on one shard. A tenant sending 40% of your traffic is 40% of your limiter’s writes landing on a single slot — which is hot partitions, arriving through a component you installed to protect yourself.

Lease — batch the round trips, strand some quota

Each node reserves k tokens in one round trip and spends them locally. The reservation debits the shared budget when it is granted, which is what makes the error one-directional.

round trips = rate / k  ·  shortfall ≤ N × (k − 1)

Never over-admits: the budget was already debited. Under-admits by whatever is still sitting in leases when the window ends, and a node holding tokens would not have refreshed, so it holds at most k − 1. Simulated in limiters.test.mjs against a leased budget that never reads the bound: admitted never exceeds L, never falls below L − N(k−1), and reaches three quarters of that bound when nodes are unevenly loaded.

Two operational details that decide whether this works.

A node told the budget is empty must stop asking. Otherwise an over-limit client turns the lease back into one round trip per request, exactly when the system is most loaded. The test asserts the round trip ceiling, and it only holds because of that rule.

Stranding is caused by idle nodes, not busy ones. Under uniform overload every node spends its whole lease and the shortfall is near zero. Give nine of ten nodes a trickle and each takes a lease of 50 to serve five requests, stranding 45 apiece. So a lease reintroduces the per-node quota problem in miniature — which is the same trade in a smaller unit, and it is why k is a real tuning decision and not a constant.

Broadcast — count exactly, share late, overshoot

Every node counts its own admissions exactly and broadcasts the count every T. A node admits while the last shared total plus its own count since is under the limit.

overshoot ≤ (rate − hottest node’s rate) × T  ≈  (1 − 1/N) × T/W × L

At every node’s last admission, sharedTotal + own count ≤ L. The true total is sharedTotal + Σ counts, so the excess is what the other nodes admitted since their last broadcast — hence rate minus the hottest node’s rate, times the period. One caveat the test asserts both halves of: this bounds the overshoot by the count that arrives during a period, so it is a hard bound only when that count is deterministic. Under Poisson arrivals it is a mean, and a burst beats it.

Set T equal to W and the overshoot approaches the limit itself — which is the fixed-window boundary burst from the previous lesson, arriving through a completely different mechanism and landing on the same 2×. The bug that lesson is named after is not a property of fixed windows. It is a property of deciding on stale information, and a gossip period is staleness with a knob on it.

Two things move, in opposite directions, and this is the whole scaling story.

A lease gets more accurate as the limit grows, because the stranded tokens are a fixed N × (k − 1) against a larger L: 190 out of 1,000 is 19%, 190 out of 100,000 is 0.19%. Turn that round and it is a sizing rule — to hold the error under 2% at ten nodes you can afford k = 3 on a limit of 1,000 and k = 201 on a limit of 100,000, so the round trips fall by half in the first case and by two hundred times in the second. The lease is the scheme whose error you can make arbitrarily small simply by having more traffic.

Broadcast gets cheaper as the limit grows and no more accurate, because its cost is N(N−1)/T messages a second regardless of the request rate, while its error stays at about T/W. At 1,000 req/s it costs 0.9 messages per request and saves you almost nothing over a central counter. At 100,000 req/s it costs 0.009 and saves you everything. Gossip is worth it exactly when the request rate is large compared with N²/T — and the N² is why the node count is the thing that kills it: at 50 nodes and the same period it is 24,500 messages a second.

And one property that is genuinely unpleasant: the overshoot bound is what arrives during T, so it scales with how hard the client pushes. A client at its limit gets 9% over; a client at 2× its limit gets 18% over; a client at 10× gets 90% over. The error is largest against precisely the traffic the limiter exists to stop.

Placement: the same schemes, a different N

Every table above assumed the nodes enforcing the limit were wherever your fleet happens to run. In practice a per-tenant quota can be enforced in three different places on the request path, and the choice is orthogonal to which of the four schemes you pick: at the edge, where every CDN PoP checks the quota before the request reaches anything you operate; at an API gateway, a fleet you run yourself in front of your services; or per service, a sidecar or library inside every backend instance. Moving the enforcement point does not invent new arithmetic — it is the identical quota-÷-N / central-counter / lease / gossip menu from the section above, called at a different N. placementModel in limiters.mjs calls distLimiter unchanged, once per tier; the two quantities that are genuinely new are how much of the traffic that layer can even see, and how much of the request path a bad request has already walked by the time it is finally rejected.

Fleet size first, because it decides everything else. An API gateway is a small, centrally operated fleet — ten nodes is the exact scenario this lesson has been running throughout. A CDN’s edge PoPs number in the hundreds. A service mesh sidecar runs inside every backend pod, and any fleet with more than a handful of services at more than a handful of replicas each puts that count in the thousands. Illustrating with 10, 100 and 1,000 — the same order-of-magnitude ladder the table earlier in this section already climbs — quota ÷ N alone admits:

PlacementNodesAdmittedRefused

The first two rows are not new figures — they are the ten- and hundred-node rows of the table above this section, relabelled by where those nodes physically sit. The third is: a limit of 1,000 split a thousand ways gives every node a quota of exactly one, so each is a loss system offered one erlang, and B(1, 1) = 1/2 has a closed form you can check by hand — half of everything is refused, with no imbalance and no overload, before a single request has misbehaved. Random routing alone makes it worse as the fleet widens: the busiest of ten nodes sees 1.16× its fair share, the busiest of a hundred sees 1.88×, and the busiest of a thousand sees 5.51× — hotNodeFactor(1000, n) for n = 10, 100, 1,000.

The other three schemes do not scale free either. A lease of k = 20, sized above to strand a tolerable 19% of the gateway’s budget, strands nodes × 19 tokens at the wider tiers — 1,900 at the edge and 19,000 per service, both bigger than the 1,000-token limit itself, so both admit 0%: the whole budget is gone before anyone can spend it. Holding the stranding to the 2% the gateway reaches with k = 3 needs k = 1 at a hundred nodes and k = 1 at a thousand — which is a central counter with extra steps, since a lease of one token is a central counter. Broadcast gossip gets worse in the dimension that actually costs money: gossipMsgsPerSec is N² in the node count, so the same 100 ms period that costs 900 messages a second at the gateway costs 99,000 at the edge and 9,990,000 per service — 99× and very nearly ten thousand times the 1,000 requests a second it is policing. Only the central counter is indifferent to placement, because its cost is the request rate and nothing else: 1,000 ops/second and one connection at every tier, the one bill that does not change when you move it.

Visibility. A layer can only limit traffic that reaches it.

seen(edge) = 1 − internal  seen(gateway) = 1 − internal − bypass  seen(service) = 1

placementVisibility in limiters.mjs. internal is the share of total volume that is a service-to-service call which never crosses the edge or the gateway at all — not traffic those layers decline to limit, traffic they structurally never observe. bypass is the share of the total that is external yet reaches a service without a gateway hop, such as a static asset or an edge-terminated path.

Suppose, illustratively, that 30% of a tenant’s traffic is internal, east-west calls between services, and a further 10% of the total is external traffic that skips the gateway. The edge then sees 70% of everything, the gateway sees 60%, and per-service enforcement sees 100% — not because it is a better limiter, but because it is the only layer sitting on every path a request can take. Push internal to 100%, an API with no public surface at all, and the edge and gateway see 0%: the quota configured there is not wrong, it is simply never consulted.

Wasted work. The flip side of seeing more is catching later. A request an upstream layer chose not to reject keeps travelling, and every layer it crosses has spent real infrastructure on it — TLS termination and abuse inspection at the edge, authentication and routing at the gateway — before the check that finally rejects it runs. Enforcing only per service means a request that should have been refused at the edge instead walks through both of those for nothing: 2 hops of infrastructure spent on a request that was always going to be rejected, against 1 for enforcing at the gateway and 0 for enforcing at the edge (wastedWork in limiters.mjs, a prefix sum over the hop costs in front of the enforcing stage). What a hop actually costs is architecture-specific, and this model does not invent a number for it; what is not architecture-specific is the direction — catching a request one layer earlier never costs more, and catching it later is never free.

When to use which

  • Under about 1,000 requests per second, or one process. Do not distribute anything. A hash map of GCRA timestamps, as the previous lesson says. This lesson is a description of a problem you do not have.
  • A few thousand per second, three to ten nodes. A central counter is fine and you should measure before believing otherwise: 5,000 ops/s is nothing for Redis, and 5 connections is nothing for anyone. Reach for a lease when the round trip starts showing in your p99, and size k so that N × (k − 1) is a percent or two of the limit — at ten nodes and a limit of 1,000 that is k = 2 or 3, which only halves the round trips. At a limit this small a lease barely pays, and knowing that is worth more than the lease.
  • Tens of thousands per second and up. The central counter is now a datastore with its own capacity plan and its own hot key, so approximate becomes structural rather than optional. Lease first, because its error shrinks as you grow; broadcast when the node count is small enough that N² is affordable and the request rate is high enough that a per-request round trip is not.
  • A shared limit that must also catch internal misuse. Per-service is the only placement that sees a service-to-service call at all, but it is also the widest fleet of the three, so quota ÷ N and gossip — both of which assume a small N — stop being options. A central counter, or a lease shrunk to near k = 1, is usually what is left.
  • Any limit that appears in a contract or an invoice. Never a scheme that can over-admit.
06

The trade-off: what you gave up, and which way you gave it up

You cannot have exact global limits, low latency and availability at once, and it is not a close call. Exactness needs one serialising point per decision. That point is a round trip on the critical path of every request, including all the ones it was never going to refuse, and it is a component whose failure forces the choice between an unlimited service and an unavailable one. Give up exactness and both come back. Every production limiter has given up exactness; the ones that appear not to have are running the round trip and not showing it to you.

The direction of the error is a decision, and almost nobody makes it deliberately. A lease can only under-admit: you refuse traffic that was entitled, which is a support ticket and a customer who can prove you are wrong. A broadcast can only over-admit: you allow traffic that was not entitled, which is unbilled work, a capacity plan quietly invalidated, and — if the limit was protecting a downstream — an incident. These are not the same error with different signs. Under-admitting damages trust, over-admitting damages the thing the limiter was installed to protect. Pick per limit, not per company: an abuse limiter should probably over-admit and a billing limiter must not.

Placement is a fourth axis, and it composes with the other three. Coordination mechanism, error direction, and now where on the request path the check lives are not independent choices. The same lease that strands a defensible 19% at a ten-node gateway strands the entire budget at a hundred-node edge fleet or a thousand-node service mesh, because stranding is nodes × (k − 1) and the fleet just got a hundred or a thousand times wider — the knob you tuned for one placement is simply wrong for another. Pushing enforcement downstream to see traffic an earlier layer structurally cannot — a service-to-service call that never touches the edge — always means paying the coordination-cost curve at a larger N, and catching abuse after more of the request path has already run. There is no placement that is simultaneously the cheapest to coordinate, the most visible, and the earliest to reject; picking one is picking which two you give up, the same way exactness, latency and availability worked at the top of this section.

A per-node quota is a bulkhead, so it also buys what a bulkhead buys. This lesson has been unkind to quota ÷ N, and it should not be mistaken for a verdict. Local quotas need no shared state, add no latency, cannot be a single point of failure, and absorb a burst without waking anything up. Envoy’s own guidance is to use them in front of a global limiter, “to reduce load on the global rate limit service”. The correct reading is that a local quota is a cheap safety ceiling and a poor contract: use it where the number is yours to protect, and something coordinated where the number is something you promised.

Approximate limiting stacks with everything else you are already approximating. The window boundary from the previous lesson, the sliding-counter estimate, clock skew between nodes, and now a lease shortfall or a broadcast overshoot. These do not cancel; they compose, and mostly nobody computes the composition. If you are running a fixed window and a gossip period equal to a quarter of it, your published limit has two independent reasons to be wrong by tens of percent and your dashboard shows neither.

And what none of this fixes. Every scheme here bounds arrivals. None of them knows that one request scans a billion rows, none of them bounds concurrency, and none of them helps when ten thousand well-behaved clients arrive at once — that is still load shedding’s job, and it still needs a signal none of these have. Distributing the limiter makes the limiter harder. It does not make it do more.

07

In the wild

Envoy documents the N × X arithmetic in its own configuration reference. The HTTP local rate limit filter applies a token bucket per Envoy process, and the local_cluster_rate_limit field exists precisely to fix what happens otherwise: “given an Envoy gateway that contains N Envoy instances and a rate limit rule X tokens per second. If this is set, the total rate limit of whole gateway will always be X tokens per second regardless of how N changes. If this is not set, the total rate limit of whole gateway will be N × X tokens per second.” That is slot 1 of this lesson, written as a vendor caveat. Note also what the fix requires: dividing by live cluster membership, so the limiter now depends on service discovery. Envoy local rate limit filter

Envoy also recommends layering the two, which is the practical answer this lesson arrives at: “Local rate limiting can be used in conjunction with global rate limiting to reduce load on the global rate limit service. For example, a local token bucket rate limit can absorb very large bursts in load that might otherwise overwhelm a global rate limit service.” Local quota as a burst ceiling, coordinated limit as the contract. Envoy global rate limiting

Doorman is the lease scheme, open-sourced by YouTube, and it is explicit that it is cooperative. A client asks a master for capacity and receives a lease with an expiry_time, a refresh_interval and a capacity; it then enforces locally and refreshes on that interval. The documentation states the property that makes this a lease rather than a counter — clients are expected to honour the grant, the lease length and the refresh interval — and it specifies what happens when the server is unreachable: leases expire and the resource falls back to a configured safe capacity, which may be a fixed per-client number, a dynamic share, zero, or unbounded. That list of four is the fail-open-or-closed decision, made a configuration option rather than a default. youtube/doorman

The academic treatment is twenty years old and is about exactly this trade. Raghavan, Vishwanath, Ramabhadran, Yocum and Snoeren, “Cloud Control with Distributed Rate Limiting” (SIGCOMM 2007) sets out to make distributed limiters behave “as if they traversed a single, shared limiter”, and proposes two: Global Random Drop, which estimates global demand by distributed aggregation and drops probabilistically, and Flow Proportional Share, which allocates by flow count so that TCP’s own behaviour comes out right. Both are periodic-broadcast schemes in the sense of this lesson, and the paper’s subject is the accuracy you get for a given amount of inter-limiter communication. Worth reading for the framing more than the algorithms: the goal is stated as indistinguishability from a single limiter, which is a much sharper specification than “roughly enforce the limit”. drl-sigcomm07.pdf

Cloudflare’s account of rate limiting at their scale is the memory argument, and it generalises. They chose the two-counter sliding-window approximation over an exact log because a log of timestamps per key was not affordable at their key count. The reason it belongs here is that it is the same move one level down: when exactness is unaffordable, you do not abandon the limit, you pick an approximation whose error you can state. (Cloudflare engineering blog, “How we built rate limiting capable of scaling to millions of domains”, 2017.)

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A sliding-window quota of q per window W is an Erlang loss system, so its rejection rate against offered load A = λW is exactly B(q, A) — which makes a per-node quota a bulkhead with requests-per-window as the slot
  • Splitting a limit of 1,000 over ten nodes takes a compliant client from 2.5% refused to 7.6% — 3.05× as many 429s at identical total quota and identical total load
  • With N quotas of limit/N the effective global limit is anything from limit/N to limit, chosen by the load balancer, and it scales with the live node count instead of staying put
  • Uniform random routing alone makes the busiest of 10 nodes see 1.16× its share and the busiest of 50 see 1.54×, so the overshoot grows as the per-node quota shrinks
  • A central counter's operation rate equals your request rate and it holds rate × round trip connections by Little's Law — 100 connections at 100k req/s and a 1 ms hop
  • A lease of k tokens cuts round trips to one in k and under-admits by at most nodes × (k − 1) stranded tokens; because the reservation debits the budget it can never over-admit
  • Broadcasting local counts every T over-admits by at most (rate − hottest node's rate) × T, which is about T/W of the limit, and costs N(N−1)/T messages a second no matter what the request rate is
  • Splitting a limit costs nothing under uniform overload and a lot at exactly the published rate, so the bill lands on the compliant client rather than the abusive one
  • Moving the same four schemes from a ten-node gateway to a hundred-node edge fleet or a thousand-node service mesh does not change the mechanism, only N — quota ÷ N falls from 92.4% to 78.5% to 50.0%, and a lease sized for the gateway strands the entire budget at the other two

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    You split a 1,000/s limit into ten quotas of 100/s. A customer paced at exactly 1,000/s opens a ticket about 429s and can prove they never exceeded their limit. What is happening, and what is the number?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Each node is a loss system of 100 slots offered 100 erlangs, and Erlang-B says it refuses B(100,100) = 7.57%. One counter of 1,000 slots offered 1,000 erlangs refuses B(1000,1000) = 2.48%. Same total quota, same total load, 3.05× as many rejections — because a quota of 100 cannot absorb a burst using the 900 tokens the other nine nodes are not currently spending. The customer is right and the limiter is doing what it was configured to do; the configuration was the error.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why is a per-node quota the same object as a connection-pool bulkhead, and what follows from that?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a quota of q per window W holds an admitted request for exactly W seconds and clears anything arriving while all q are held — M/G/q/q, an Erlang loss system, with requests-per-window as the slot. So the arithmetic is identical to splitting a pool of 24 into four pools of 6, and everything the bulkheads lesson proves transfers: the cost is convex in the number of partitions, so the first split is cheap and the fiftieth is not; most of the independence arrives early; and the loss is invisible because it was never a line item, it was just the shared counter working.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your fleet autoscales from 10 nodes to 30 and nobody touches the limiter configuration. What is your published limit now?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Three times what you published. The effective limit is the per-node quota times the number of live nodes, so it tracks your fleet size — up on a scale-out, down during a deploy that drains two thirds of the fleet. Envoy documents exactly this in its local rate limit filter: without local_cluster_rate_limit, "the total rate limit of whole gateway will be N × X tokens per second". The fix is to divide by live membership, which means the limiter now depends on service discovery, which means it has a new way to be wrong.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A central counter is exact. Name its three costs, with a number for each.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Latency — one round trip added to every request the limiter admits, including all the ones it was never going to refuse. Concurrency — Little's Law, rate × round trip: at 100k req/s and a 1 ms hop that is 100 connections held permanently, and 500 across an availability zone. Availability — the limiter's op rate equals your request rate, so at 100k req/s it is a 100k ops/s datastore that your entire service now depends on, and per-key limits mean the interesting tenant is one hot key on one shard.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A lease and a broadcast are both approximate. How do their errors differ, and which do you want for a billing limit?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A lease debits the shared budget when it is granted, so it can only ever under-admit — by at most nodes × (k − 1) tokens stranded in leases nobody got to spend. A broadcast counts exactly and shares late, so it can only ever over-admit — by at most what the other nodes pushed during one period, roughly T/W of the limit. For billing you want the lease: over-admitting means you gave away chargeable work and cannot invoice it. For abuse protection you may prefer the broadcast, because a false 429 on legitimate traffic is the more expensive error there.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Why does shortening the gossip period stop helping, and what replaces the cost you saved?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the overshoot falls linearly in T while messages rise as N(N−1)/T. At 10 nodes and a 1-second window, T = 100 ms costs 900 messages a second to hold the error to 9%; T = 10 ms costs 9,000 messages a second — more traffic than the 1,000 req/s it is policing — to reach 0.9%. The saving was never in the period, it is that gossip cost is independent of the request rate: the same 900 messages a second police 100,000 req/s, where a central counter would need 100,000 operations. Gossip is the right answer when the request rate is large compared with N²/T, and the wrong one otherwise.
    Did you have that?
  7. Cued recall

    You run this limiter at a ten-node API gateway today. A security review asks you to also enforce it inside every backend service, because a compromised service could call another service directly and skip the gateway. What changes, and what stays the same?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    What changes is visibility and the N you pay coordination cost at. The gateway does not merely decline to limit a direct service-to-service call — it never receives it, so per-service enforcement goes from seeing 0% of that traffic to 100%. What stays the same is the menu: it is the identical quota-÷-N / central / lease / gossip choice, now evaluated at perhaps a thousand nodes instead of ten. Quota ÷ N alone falls from 92.4% admitted at ten nodes to 50.0% at a thousand, where every node's quota is exactly one request. A lease of k = 20 that strands a tolerable 19% of the budget at ten nodes strands the entire budget at a thousand, because nodes × (k − 1) scales with the fleet and the 1,000-token limit did not — you would shrink k toward 1 (a central counter, at that point) rather than keep the gateway's setting.
    Did you have that?
  8. Near transfer

    You limit each tenant to 50 concurrent in-flight requests, and you implement it across 8 app servers by giving each server a semaphore of 6 per tenant. A tenant complains of rejections while your dashboards show 31 requests in flight. What is the arithmetic, and what would you actually do?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The same Erlang loss system, and now literally so: 6 servers of capacity offered its share of 50 erlangs. B(6, 6.25) = 22% against B(50, 50) = 10.5% — twice the rejections at identical total capacity — plus routing imbalance, which for 50 arrivals over 8 servers makes the busiest see well over its share. And 6 × 8 = 48, so you quietly published 48 rather than 50. What you would actually do: keep the local semaphores as a cheap ceiling but raise each to something like 12 so ordinary clustering does not trip them, and accept that the real aggregate is now 96 — because concurrency limits exist to protect a thread pool, and a loose global bound that never false-rejects is worth more than a tight one that does. If the 50 is a contractual number rather than a safety number, it cannot live in local semaphores at all; it needs a lease.
    Did you have that?
  9. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: enforcing a global budget from local decisions costs either a round trip per decision or a bounded error, and the direction of that error is a design choice rather than an accident. Where else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Airline seat inventory, where local reservation systems oversell deliberately and the bound is measured in denied boardings. Card networks authorising offline up to a floor limit, which is a lease — the merchant terminal holds an allowance and reconciles later. Ten debit cards on one account, which is this lesson with the sign flipped. Fishing quotas divided by boat, where the unfished portion of one boat's quota is exactly the stranded token. Electricity grid frequency regulation, where every generator acts locally on a shared signal and the error shows up as drift. In every case the same three questions decide it: how stale may the shared view be, how much may we be wrong by, and which way do we prefer to be wrong — and the third is the one that is almost never asked out loud, which is why systems that must not overshoot are so often built out of mechanisms that can only overshoot.
    Did you have that?
