Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cost attribution explained well nowhere else

Chargeback nets $2,417 a month by metering a $31,035 bill down to the tenant, while showback nets $200 and goes negative on the exact same pipeline

[Egress costs](/concepts/egress-costs) prices a $31,035-a-month transfer bill and ends on a warning: none of that arithmetic is executable without dividing the bill by a team. This lesson is that division, priced. Showback and chargeback are not the same mechanism at two prices — one moves a real number on a real budget and the other does not, and that difference is worth six times as much incentive per dollar correctly attributed. Building the pipeline that can defend a bill line costs real engineering hours regardless of mechanism, and on the bill above, chargeback justifies building it down to the tenant while showback only ever justifies the free tier — anything finer loses money, no matter how cheap cloud storage and query pricing get, because the number that decides it is an incentive nobody's budget will produce.

Read first — egress-costs

01

A bill with no owner is not executable, it is a rumour

Egress costs ends with a number and a warning. The number: a JSON API at 2,000 requests a second with 40 KB responses runs a transfer bill of $31,035 a month. The warning, in that lesson’s own words: “none of this is executable without cost attribution. Most organisations get one undifferentiated data transfer total per account, which makes all of this arithmetic a model rather than a measurement.”

Say six teams share that API — a mobile client, a partner integration, an internal dashboard, three tenants of a multi-tenant product built on it. The bill is real. Nobody’s team budget reflects a share of it, because the provider’s invoice has one line: data transfer out, $31,035. Every cost lever in this tier — CDN break-evens, replication factor, cache sizing — assumes a team can see its own share of a number like that move when it acts. Right now, none of them can.

Here is the assumption that quietly stands in for a decision: “we’ll divide it by request count, or by headcount, or just tell people what it is.” Three different sentences, and they describe three different things — a metering scheme, a formula, and a mechanism for whether the number matters at all. Conflating them is the mistake this lesson exists to price apart.

attribution mechanism ≠ attribution granularity

“How finely is usage measured” and “does the measurement touch anyone’s budget” are independent knobs. You can meter perfectly and show it on a dashboard nobody’s spend depends on (showback), or meter crudely and still put a real number on a real P&L (chargeback). Four granularities and two mechanisms make eight cells, not two, and this lesson’s whole job is showing they are not worth the same thing.

02

Showback tells a team a number. Chargeback bills them for it.

Showback reports usage back to a team — a dashboard, a monthly email, a Slack digest — with no money moving. Chargeback actually bills the team’s budget: the number appears on their P&L, their manager sees it in a budget review, and going over plan has the same consequence any other overspend does.

Both need a granularity of metering underneath them, and this is the second knob:

GranularityWhat it takes to buildAccuracy — share of true usage attributed correctly
AccountNothing. The provider’s own bill, one line.0% — no per-team split exists at all
Cost-allocation tagEnable the provider’s free export, tag resources55% — shared services land wherever the tag owner is
Per-tenant, hourlyApp code stamps every request with a tenant id90% — most shared calls still get socialised
Per-requestThe stamp survives every internal hop and shared call99% — the practical ceiling

Accuracy is not a property of the mechanism, it is a property of the pipeline, and it costs real engineering time to raise regardless of whether the number it produces ever touches a budget. That cost is the other half of this lesson, and it is mostly not what people expect it to be.

Two prices in this file are real and dated 2026-08: Amazon Athena’s on-demand query price, $5 per TB scanned, verified against aws.amazon.com/athena/pricing; and object storage capacity, reused unchanged from egress.mjs at $0.023/GB-month. Engineer-hour cost and both elasticities are declared inputs, in the same style every other tier-9 lesson uses for a number nobody can look up for you — dated, marked confidence: ‘low’, with a caveat naming what would change them. The model in src/sim/cost-attribution.mjs keeps the two kinds separate so it is visible which is which.

Predict A team builds PERFECT per-request metering — 99% of usage correctly attributed — and reports it as a showback dashboard, no budget impact. On the $31,035/month bill split six ways, chargeback's elasticity is a declared 0.12 and showback's is 0.02. Does the perfectly-accurate showback pipeline pay for itself?
The pipeline costs $2,727.54/month to run (98.7% of that is engineering labour, not storage or query cost) and a 0.02 elasticity on 99%-accurate attribution of a $31,035 bill realises only $614.49/month in savings — net −$2,113.05. Accuracy and elasticity are separate multiplicative terms; raising one all the way to its ceiling does not compensate for the other being small. “Eventually” is the trap in option 2: nothing about amortising the build cost changes the STEADY-STATE elasticity, so a longer amortisation window narrows the gap but never closes it while the mechanism stays showback.

03

The photocopier that taught every accounting department this lesson

Long before anyone metered a byte, offices metered photocopies, and the two ways they did it are this lesson’s whole argument with the technology stripped out.

Showback: a monthly report goes to every department head — “your department made 4,200 copies this month, at an average cost of $0.04 each, for $168.” No money moves. Some department heads read it and mention it in a meeting. Most file it. Usage barely changes, because reading a number about your own behaviour is not the same act as paying for it.

Chargeback: the same $168 appears as a line item on the department’s own budget, debited from the same account that pays for everything else the department buys. Suddenly someone asks why Legal makes four times the copies Engineering does, whether the fax machine really needs to print a confirmation page, whether the report that goes to nine people needs nine copies. Nothing about the photocopier changed. What changed is that the number now competes with every other thing that department’s budget could have bought.

Both regimes needed the same meter — a counter on the machine, or a swipe-card log if you wanted to know which department made which copies. The counter’s accuracy was never the limiting factor. What determined how much copying fell was whether the number it produced touched anyone’s spending decision.

The lesson generalises past copiers because the failure mode does: whenever a shared resource is reported without being billed, usage drifts toward whatever is easiest for the user of the resource, not whatever is cheapest for the organisation paying for it — and no increase in the REPORT’s precision fixes that, because precision was never the variable in question.

04

Four numbers per cell, and the ratio that decides an upgrade

Both sides of this trade-off are closed-form. The administrative side first, because it is the one this file is built to correct a misconception about:

adminCostUSD = (buildHours × engineerHourUSD ÷ 12 + maintainHoursPerMonth × engineerHourUSD) + (storedGB × storageGBMonth + reportsPerMonth × storedGB ÷ 1000 × athenaPerTBScanned)

Labour, then infrastructure. At per-request granularity on the egress-costs workload — 5.18 billion requests a month, one 40-byte event per request, 30 reports a month each conservatively re-scanning the WHOLE month’s table — the infrastructure term is $35.87. The labour term is $2,691.67. Metering every single request on this workload is 98.7% engineering time, which is the number this file was built to produce: cloud per-byte prices being cheap does not make attribution cheap, because the expensive part was never bytes.

savingsUSD = bill × accuracy(granularity) × elasticity(mechanism)

Two multiplicative terms and neither compensates for the other being small. On the $31,035 bill, tenant-level (90% accuracy) under chargeback (0.12 elasticity) realises $3,351.77; the SAME 90%-accurate metering under showback (0.02) realises $558.63 — a 6x gap that is entirely the mechanism, none of it the pipeline.

netValueUSD = savingsUSD − adminCostUSD

The number that actually decides anything. It is not “is metering worth building” in the abstract — it is worth building AT A GRANULARITY, FOR A MECHANISM, ON A BILL OF THIS SIZE, and all four of those are inputs a real organisation actually has.

And the break-even, in the same ratio-of-a-cost-delta-to-a-benefit-rate shape as every other threshold in this tier — egress-costs’s CDN hit ratio, when a cache stops paying’s hit rate:

upgradeBreakEvenUSD = (adminCost(fine) − adminCost(coarse)) ÷ (elasticity × (accuracy(fine) − accuracy(coarse)))

Tag → tenant costs an extra $793.33/month to run and buys 35 points of accuracy. Under chargeback that pays for itself above $18,889/month — 793.33 ÷ (0.12 × 0.35). Under showback, same pipeline, same accuracy gain, the denominator is six times smaller, so the threshold is $113,333/month — a bill size almost no single team’s share of a shared service reaches.

The number I expected to be different

I expected the infrastructure cost of per-request metering to be the deciding term — a per-request event pipeline sounds expensive. At real 2026 cloud prices it is $35.87 a month for 5.18 billion events, because storage and Athena scanning are both priced per byte and a 40-byte event compresses to almost nothing at that price. The number that actually gates every decision in this lesson is engineering hours, and engineering hours do not get cheaper the way a per-GB price does — which is why “just meter everything, storage is cheap now” is true and irrelevant at the same time.

06

What this model does not know, and where it would be wrong to trust it

Elasticity is a declared input standing in for a controlled experiment nobody has run on your organisation. The 0.12/0.02 split is chosen to make the qualitative claim — chargeback’s incentive is several times stronger — computable, not to be quoted as a measured fact. What survives if the absolute numbers are wrong by half in either direction: every ratio and every break-even in slot 5’s third table, because they are built entirely from the RATIO of the two elasticities, and that ratio is the one number this lesson actually asks you to believe.

Accuracy is not free to raise even within one granularity, and this model treats it as fixed per tier. Getting tenant-level metering from 90% to, say, 95% accuracy without moving to per-request granularity — better reconciliation rules for shared calls, mostly — is real engineering work this model has no line for. Treat the four accuracy figures as representative points on a curve, not a complete map of it.

Chargeback has a real cost this file does not price: disputes. A number that debits someone’s budget gets argued with, in a way a dashboard never does. “That wasn’t our traffic” meetings, an appeals process, a policy for shared-infrastructure splits that someone has to defend — all of that is engineering-adjacent labour that scales with how contested the number is, which correlates with how much money is at stake, which is exactly the case where chargeback is most worth building. The maintainHoursPerMonth figures in this file are a floor, not a ceiling.

Gaming is a real response to a real incentive, and a strong incentive is not automatically a correctly-aimed one. A team facing chargeback for egress has a genuine reason to under-provision redundancy, badge internal calls as someone else’s traffic, or lobby for their workload to be reclassified as “platform.” None of that is a metering-accuracy failure — accuracy can be perfect and the incentive can still point somewhere the organisation does not want it to point. This is the real cost of the strong incentive this lesson prices as a pure benefit, and it does not show up in netValueUSD at all.

This lesson attributes a bill that already exists. It says nothing about whether the bill is a fair unit to split. Shared infrastructure — a database everyone reads, a service mesh every call crosses — has a real allocation question underneath the metering question, and no granularity here answers “should team A’s heavier usage of shared infrastructure cost them more than team B’s, or is that infrastructure a fixed cost everyone should split evenly regardless of use.” That is a policy decision this file assumes has already been made.

07

In the wild

The FinOps Foundation names chargeback and showback as two separate framework capabilities, not two points on one maturity ladder. Its published Cloud FinOps framework lists “Invoicing & Chargeback” and “Data Analysis and Showback” as distinct capabilities with their own maturity models, rather than treating chargeback as a more-advanced version of showback that every organisation should eventually reach. That matches this lesson’s own finding: which one is worth running is a question about the bill size and the mechanism’s elasticity, not a maturity score.

A cost-allocation label only attributes usage from the date it is applied, and the provider says so explicitly. Google Cloud’s documentation for Cloud Billing data export to BigQuery states that cost data for a given label includes only usage recorded since the label was attached to a resource — label a VM in the middle of a month and its cost history for the first half of that month is not retroactively attributed, it is simply unattributed. This is the granularity question with the comfortable assumption removed: attribution is not a query you can always run later against data you already have, it is a decision that has to be made before the usage happens.

Amazon Athena’s on-demand price is exactly the number this lesson’s infrastructure column is built from. $5 per TB of data scanned, published at aws.amazon.com/athena/pricing as of 2026-08, is the real price behind every queryUSD figure in src/sim/cost-attribution.mjs — and the reason those figures stay under $36/month even at full per-request granularity is that a metering table stored in a columnar, partitioned format scans a small fraction of what this file conservatively assumes every report re-scans the whole thing.

A vendor-neutral open-source project exists specifically because cloud billing exports don’t reach tenant granularity. OpenCost, an incubating CNCF project backed by AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft and others, allocates Kubernetes cost in real time down to the container and namespace level — the per-tenant and per-request rows of this lesson’s granularity table, built as infrastructure because no cloud provider’s own bill goes there. Its existence is itself evidence for this lesson’s central cost claim: the hard part of attribution was never the per-byte price of storing the measurement, it was building something to take the measurement at all.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Chargeback bills a team's own budget for its own usage; showback only reports usage. They are not the same mechanism at different cost — the elasticity chargeback produces is a declared 6x showback's, because a dashboard nobody's spend depends on moves people far less than a number that lands on their P&L
  • Attribution needs a GRANULARITY independent of the mechanism — account, cost-allocation tag, per-tenant, or per-request — and coarse granularity misattributes shared infrastructure, which blunts whatever incentive the mechanism could produce regardless of how real the incentive is
  • The infrastructure cost of metering is real but tiny: storing and querying 207 GB of per-request events for 5.18 billion requests a month costs $35.87 — the admin cost that actually decides anything is 98.7% engineering LABOUR to instrument every code path with an owner, and labour does not fall the way per-GB prices do
  • Net value = bill × accuracy × elasticity − admin cost, and on egress-costs' $31,035/month bill split six ways, chargeback's best granularity is tenant-level ($2,417/mo net) while showback's best is free cost-allocation tags ($200/mo net) — going finer than that is net-negative under showback at any bill size a single team plausibly produces
  • The bill size at which a finer granularity pays for itself is (admin cost delta) ÷ (elasticity × accuracy delta) — the same ratio-of-a-cost-delta-to-a-benefit-rate shape as every other break-even in this tier, and it is 6x higher under showback than chargeback for an identical pipeline
  • A cost-allocation label attributes usage only from the date it was applied forward — GCP's billing export documents this explicitly — so retroactive attribution is not a query problem, it is a fact that was never recorded
  • Whether attribution is worth building is a question about the MECHANISM'S incentive strength first and the pipeline's cost second — a perfectly accurate metering pipeline under showback can still net negative, and a crude one under chargeback can still be worth building

  2. Cued recall

    A VP proposes replacing chargeback with showback to "save the cost of the billing pipeline." The pipeline is per-tenant metering at $935/month, run against the same $31,035/month bill. What actually happens to the two numbers that matter?

  3. Near transfer

    A team ships PERFECT per-request attribution — 99% accuracy, every shared call correctly split — and reports it up as a showback dashboard rather than a chargeback line. Six months later spend on the metered service is unchanged. Was the metering pipeline a failure?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — the metering was flawless; the MECHANISM was the wrong one for the goal. Accuracy and incentive are independent terms in this model: `savingsUSD = bill × accuracy × elasticity`, and showback's elasticity is a declared 0.02 regardless of how the accuracy term reads. Perfect attribution under showback still nets −$2,113.05/month on the $31,035 bill in this lesson's scenario, because the $2,727.54 it costs to run per-request metering vastly exceeds the $614.49 a 0.02 elasticity can ever realise. The near-transfer trap is assuming "more accurate" is the same axis as "more effective" — they multiply into the same number, but one of them has a structural ceiling the other does not, and no amount of engineering on the accuracy term moves the ceiling.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: an incentive that never touches a real budget is a weak incentive no matter how accurately it is measured, and the cost of measuring it does not know how weak the incentive is. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Home energy monitors. A device that shows a household its real-time electricity draw, accurate to the watt, is showback: it reports usage against no consequence, and the empirical savings from installing one are a few percent — real, and far below what the same household does when a `smart' time-of-use TARIFF actually changes the bill for running the dryer at 6pm. The monitor's accuracy was never the limiting factor. Restaurant kitchens and food cost percentage. A chef who sees a plate-level food-cost report (showback) trims garnish; a chef whose BONUS is computed off that same number (chargeback) renegotiates supplier contracts and redesigns the menu — same report, same accuracy, a completely different amount of behaviour change, because only one of them moved the chef's own money. Internal university cost recovery for shared equipment. A department shown its share of a shared microscope's upkeep cost changes little; a department actually BILLED for scope-hours immediately starts scheduling around the busiest days — the metering (a sign-up sheet) can be identical in both regimes, and the entire behavioural difference is which one debits an account someone controls.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
