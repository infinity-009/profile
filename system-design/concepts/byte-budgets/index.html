Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Byte budgets explained well nowhere else

Eight hundred and seventy megabytes is eight and a half gigabytes

Everyone estimates storage from the payload and is wrong by an order of magnitude, systematically and in the same direction. The multipliers — row overhead, indexes, compression that does not apply, rendition ladders, replication — are each individually unsurprising and they compound. Worse, the smallest rows have the worst overhead ratio, so the table you thought was cheap is the expensive one.

Read first — back-of-envelope

01

Ten columns, eighty-seven bytes, and a capacity plan that is out by ten

A users table. Ten columns: an id, an email, a display name, a country, a locale, two timestamps, a referrer id, a plan and a flags integer. Add up the contents and it is 87 bytes per row. Ten million users is 870 MB, and the capacity plan says a gigabyte with room to spare.

The number on the bill is 8.48 GB.

Nothing was measured wrong. Every factor between those two numbers is standard, documented, and would be agreed to instantly if named. They were simply not named, and there are five of them, and they multiply.

Here is the one that should worry you most before we start: the same table’s follows join table — three bigint columns, 24 bytes of content, the simplest row in the schema — has a worse multiplier than the users table. The row you would guess is cheapest is the expensive one, and you probably have a hundred times more of them.

02

The obvious fix — add a safety margin — and why a margin cannot reach it

The instinct is to estimate the payload and round up. Double it. Add 50% “for safety”. This fails for a specific reason worth stating precisely: a margin is additive and the error is multiplicative.

Predict Five multipliers, and you happen to estimate each one within ±30% of the truth. How wide is the range your final answer could be in?
The factors multiply, so their relative errors multiply. The high end is 1.3⁵ = 3.71× and the low end is 0.7⁵ = 0.17×, so the interval spans (1.3/0.7)⁵ = 22×. Nothing cancels — there is no mechanism by which over-estimating the index size offsets under-estimating the replication factor, because both are multiplications in the same direction. This is also why a 50% safety margin is not conservative: it is a rounding error against the width of its own estimate.
Show it without answering
03

The intuition: the quantity surveyor

You want to know what a building costs. You can see the materials, so you price the materials.

Then: cut waste on every length of timber. Labour, as a multiple of materials. Plant hire and access. Professional fees, as a percentage of the running total. Tax on all of it. Contingency on top of that.

Each factor is individually unremarkable — nobody argues that scaffolding is free. And each one is a multiplier, so the materials figure is not slightly low, it is low by their product. The construction industry’s response is not to think harder about materials. It is to maintain standard multiplier stacks by building type, apply them as ranges, and go and measure the widest range first.

That job has a name. A quantity surveyor exists because the naive estimate is wrong by a compounding product, and the profession is the institutional admission that no amount of care about the visible quantity fixes an invisible multiplier.

04

The mechanism: the multiplier stack, factor by factor

Row overhead: where 87 bytes becomes 134.3

1.54×, and the reason it is worse for small rows is that the tuple header is per row, not per byte:

rowcontenton diskratio
ten-column user87 B134.3 B1.54×
three-bigint follow edge24 B52.2 B2.17×

Narrow high-count tables — joins, edges, events — have the worst ratios and the most rows. The tables you have billions of are the ones the estimate is worst on.

Indexes: often more bytes than the data

An index stores its key plus a pointer per row, in its own B-tree, uncompressed. Five indexes on the users table:

indexkey widthbytes at 10M rowslevels
id8 B224 MB3
email29 B497 MB4
(country, created_at)16 B314 MB3
last_seen_at8 B224 MB3
referrer_id8 B224 MB3

Total 1.48 GB against a heap of 1.34 GB — an index-to-data ratio of 1.10×. More than half the table’s storage is index, and note the second row: a wide key costs twice a narrow one and adds a tree level, so it is slower as well as larger.

Compression: a harmonic mean, which is Amdahl’s Law again

This is the factor most often applied wrongly, and the error always goes the same way.

Effective compression is a size-weighted harmonic mean, because you sum compressed sizes, not ratios. Take a corpus that is half media by bytes, with JSON at 4× and text at 3.5×:

  • averaging the published ratios: 2.83×
  • what you actually get: 1.58×
  • overstatement: 1.79×

And because the harmonic mean of positive numbers is never above the arithmetic mean, averaging ratios overstates the saving every single time. There is no case where this error goes in your favour.

The structural form is sharper. With f the incompressible byte share and r the ratio on everything else:

effective ratio = 1 / (f + (1−f)/r)

That is Amdahl’s Law, with incompressible bytes playing the serial fraction — see amdahl-and-usl, and note the same consequence: a ceiling of 1/f regardless of r. A corpus half media cannot exceed 2×, so buying a better compressor is buying a larger r against a term that no longer matters.

Published ratios with their real ranges, because a point value here is the problem:

dataratiorangeconfidence
JSON4×3–5medium
natural-language text3.5×2.5–5medium
columnar numeric12×8–40low
media (JPEG, H.264, AAC)1.0×0.98–1.0high

Two things in that table. Columnar numeric has the widest range on the list and cardinality decides it, not the data type — sorted low-cardinality columns reach 40×, random high-cardinality ones reach 2×. And media can be below 1.0: a compressing filesystem adds container and block metadata to bytes it cannot shrink.

Renditions: the argument is about formats, not breakpoints

An image ladder’s widths fall in a roughly constant ratio, so its total pixels is a convergent geometric series:

Σ r^(−2k)  →  r² / (r² − 1)

For halving widths that is 4/3. For the 1.6× steps of a real ladder it is about 1.6×. So every extra smaller size is nearly free and the sum is bounded however many you add.

What is not bounded is formats. Seven widths in three formats is 21 objects and 4.38 MB from one 900 kB upload — and each format multiplies linearly, forever. The breakpoint argument is the one that does not matter; the format count and whether you keep the original are the ones that do.

Note also that one decision lands on three different resources with three different multipliers: storage sees all 21, a browser fetches 1, and a CDN edge filling its cache fetches 21.

Video: the term everyone omits is the ladder

A 10-minute 1080p video, seven-rung ABR ladder, H.264:

10.8×, and 700 segments rather than one object. The ladder factor is the single largest term and it is the one absent from every back-of-envelope, because the estimate names a resolution and a ladder is not a resolution.

The numbers: the stack, end to end

Every multiplier is between 5.9 and 13.3. An order of magnitude is the normal case, not the bad case, and the edge table is the worst of the four despite being the simplest thing in the schema.

The write side moves with it and is usually forgotten entirely: one inserted user row dirties 264 bytes of heap and index pages, and at RF 3 two replicas receive that too. So the same stack that multiplies your storage bill multiplies your write bandwidth and your cross-zone egress — which is cross-az-traffic and replication-factor-cost in money.

06

The trade-off: the output is an interval, and the middle of it is the wrong answer

The instinct after slot 5 is to memorise better constants. That is the wrong lesson, because the uncertainty compounds too:

factors, each ±30%interval spans
36.4×
411.9×
522.1×

So a stack of five well-understood factors, each estimated well, produces an answer spanning 22×. The correct output of a byte budget is therefore an interval, and that changes what you do next in two concrete ways.

Measure the least-known factor, not the largest one. Each factor contributes to the interval’s width in proportion to the log of its own range. The biggest multiplier is often the best known — replication factor is not uncertain, it is a decision — while a modest factor like bloat may own most of the width. Rank by log-width and go measure the top one; ranking by magnitude sends you to measure something you already know.

And do not take the middle of each range. The product of the midpoints exceeds the geometric centre of the interval, by AM ≥ GM, once per factor — so the careful-sounding method is biased high and the bias grows as you add factors. Use the geometric centre if you need a single number, and prefer to carry the interval.

Which gives the honest reading of an interval: if it spans a decision boundary, the estimate has not answered the question yet. An answer of “between 15 and 180 TB” is not a bad estimate — it is a correct statement that you cannot yet choose an instance size, and the next action is a measurement rather than a rounding.

Two limits of everything above, stated rather than buried:

The row arithmetic is PostgreSQL’s. The 8 kB page, the 23-byte tuple header, the 4-byte line pointer and the alignment rules are that engine’s, taken from its documented page layout. MySQL/InnoDB, SQLite and every LSM engine have different constants and the same shape — a per-row fixed cost, a per-page remainder, and uncompressed index copies. The numbers move; the fact that content is not size does not.

And this lesson prices bytes, not money. A multiplier of 9.75× on a storage line is not 9.75× on a bill, because tiers, compression at rest and lifecycle policies all intervene — that is storage-cost-tiers. What it is is 9.75× on every quantity derived from byte count: backup windows, restore times, replication bandwidth, cache sizing and the working set that decides whether the thing fits in memory at all.

07

In the wild

The page-layout constants are documented and nobody reads them. PostgreSQL’s Database Page Layout chapter specifies the 8 kB page, the item pointer array, and the tuple header including the null bitmap; the alignment rules follow from the C struct layout in htup_details.h. Every figure in slot 4 is derivable from that chapter, which is why this lesson’s model cites it rather than measuring — the arithmetic is exact and public, and the reason estimates are wrong is not that the information is hidden.

The column-ordering result is a known and rarely-applied optimisation. Because alignment padding depends on declaration order, ordering columns widest-first recovers the padding — the 5 bytes in the Budget above. It is free, it is invisible in every ORM, and at ten billion rows it is real storage. It is also the only movable term in the row, which is worth knowing precisely so you stop looking for others.

The video ladder arithmetic is the industry’s own. Apple’s HLS authoring guidance and Netflix’s published work on per-title encoding both start from the observation that a title is a ladder rather than a resolution — Netflix’s contribution being that the ladder should be chosen per title rather than fixed, since an animated film and an action film need different rungs. The relevant point here is that both treat the ladder sum as the quantity, and a capacity estimate that names “1080p” has named one rung of it.

The compression-ceiling result is Amdahl’s, and the collision is not a coincidence. Amdahl’s 1967 argument about serial fractions and this lesson’s incompressible-byte share are the same algebra, because both describe a fixed portion that no amount of improvement elsewhere reduces. Recognising the shape is worth more than either instance: whenever a fraction of the work is immune to your optimisation, the speedup has a ceiling of one over that fraction.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Storage estimates are wrong by a systematic multiplier stack, not by random error, and every factor in it is one somebody left out rather than got wrong
  • A row's on-disk size is content plus varlena headers plus alignment padding plus a 23-byte tuple header plus a line pointer plus the unused page remainder
  • The overhead ratio is WORSE for small rows, so a three-column join table has a worse ratio (2.17×) than a ten-column user row (1.54×)
  • Indexes can exceed the heap: five indexes on a users table are 1.10× the heap bytes, so more than half the table's storage is index
  • Compression is a size-weighted HARMONIC mean, so it is dominated by whatever does not compress, and averaging published per-type ratios always overstates the saving
  • That harmonic form is Amdahl's Law with incompressible bytes as the serial fraction, so a corpus that is half media has a compression ceiling of 2× no matter what
  • An image rendition ladder's total pixels converge to r²/(r²−1) times the top rung, so extra sizes are nearly free and the multiplier is set by the number of FORMATS
  • A 10-minute 1080p video is 450 MB by bitrate × duration and 4.85 GB on the bill, because the ABR ladder sums to 2.37× the top rung
  • Because the factors compound, an estimate 30% off on each of five factors spans 22×, so the correct output is an interval and the factor to measure is the least known one rather than the largest

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A users table has ten columns and 87 bytes of content per row. How many bytes does a row occupy on disk, and where did the rest go?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    134.3 bytes, a ratio of 1.54×, and none of it is waste in the sense of a bug. 87 bytes of content, 4 bytes of varlena length headers on the four short text columns, 5 bytes of alignment padding, a 32-byte tuple header (23 fixed, plus the null bitmap, rounded up to the 8-byte alignment boundary), a 4-byte line pointer in the page's item array, and 2.3 bytes of page remainder because 61 rows fit in an 8 kB page and the rest of it cannot be used. Only the padding and the remainder are movable, and reordering the columns is the only free win available.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Which has the worse storage overhead ratio — a ten-column user row, or a three-column follow-edge row of nothing but bigints?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The edge row, and this inverts most people's instinct. Ten columns with 87 bytes of content give 1.54×; three bigints with 24 bytes of content give 2.17×. The reason is that the tuple header is a fixed cost per row rather than per byte, so the fewer bytes of content a row carries, the larger a share the fixed cost takes. Since join tables, edge tables and event tables are exactly the narrow high-count ones, the tables you have the most rows of have the worst ratios — and 24 bytes of content becomes 52.2 bytes on disk before a single index.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your corpus is half media by bytes and half JSON and text. Benchmarks say JSON compresses 4× and text 3.5×. What compression ratio will you actually get?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    1.58×, against the 2.83× you get by averaging the published ratios — an overstatement of 1.79×. The reason is that effective compression is a size-weighted harmonic mean, not an arithmetic one: you add up compressed sizes, not ratios. And because the harmonic mean of positive numbers is never above the arithmetic mean, averaging ratios overstates the saving every single time, never the other way. The structural version is sharper still: the incompressible share sets a hard ceiling of 1/f, so a corpus that is half media cannot exceed 2× no matter how good the compressor is on the other half.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You are arguing about how many breakpoints an image rendition ladder should have. What does the arithmetic say the argument is about?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not the breakpoints. Because widths fall in a roughly constant ratio, the ladder's total pixels is a convergent series — Σ r^(−2k) → r²/(r²−1), which for widths falling by 1.6× is about 1.6× the top rung and for halving widths is 4/3. So every extra smaller size is nearly free and the total is bounded however many you add. What is not bounded is the number of formats: emitting JPEG, WebP and AVIF triples everything, linearly and forever. Seven widths × three formats is 21 objects and 4.38 MB from one 900 kB upload. The argument to have is about formats and about whether you keep the original, and the breakpoint count is the part that does not matter.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Every factor in your storage estimate is within 30% of the truth. How good is the estimate?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    With five factors, it spans 22× — from 0.17× to 3.71× of what you said. With four factors, 11.9×. That is the whole argument for carrying an interval rather than a point: because the factors multiply, their relative errors multiply too, so "each factor is roughly right" does not make the product roughly right. Two consequences worth acting on. First, the factor to go and measure is the one owning the largest share of the interval's log-width — the least known factor, not the largest one, and those are usually different. Second, taking the middle of each range is biased high: the arithmetic mean exceeds the geometric mean once per factor and the bias compounds, so the habitual careful-sounding method drifts upward with every factor you add.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team is sizing a new events table for a 12-month retention window. They compute 400 bytes per event × 50M events/day × 365 days = 7.3 TB, round up to 10 TB "for safety", and provision it. What is missing, and is 10 TB safe?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The 37% safety margin is roughly an order of magnitude short, and the interval reasoning says the estimate has not answered the question yet.

    Missing, in the order they bite: row overhead — 400 bytes of content is generous enough that the ratio is mild, maybe 1.15×, but it is not 1.0; indexes — an events table gets at least a time index and usually two or three more dimensions, and on a 400-byte row a timestamp index is cheap per row but there will be several, so 1.3–1.6×; replication — almost certainly ×3, which alone consumes the entire safety margin twice over; and bloat between vacuums, 1.1–2.0× depending on the delete pattern, which for a retention-windowed table is the worst case because the deletes arrive in bulk at the trailing edge.

    Multiply: 7.3 TB × 1.15 × 1.45 × 3 × 1.4 ≈ 51 TB. But the honest output is not 51 TB either — it is the interval. Four uncertain factors at ±30% span 11.9×, so the answer is somewhere between roughly 15 TB and 180 TB, and 10 TB is outside the interval entirely. That is the useful finding: not that the estimate was low, but that it was low by more than its own uncertainty, which is the signature of a missing factor rather than a mis-estimated one.

    The right next move follows from the log-width rule: the widest factor here is bloat, and it is also the cheapest to measure — one query against an existing table with a similar delete pattern. Replication is not uncertain at all, it is a decision. Measure the one, decide the other, and the interval collapses to something you can provision against.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a quantity is estimated from the thing you can see, while several independent overheads each multiply it — so the estimate is not approximately right but systematically low by the product of every factor that was left out, and each factor individually looks too small to bother with. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A construction budget. The quantity you can see is materials, and every overhead multiplies it: waste factor on cut material, labour as a multiple of materials, plant and access, professional fees as a percentage of the total, VAT on the whole thing, and contingency. Each is individually unremarkable and each is a multiplier, so the final figure is a small multiple of the materials cost and the estimate that named the materials cost is not slightly low. The industry's response is exactly this lesson's: standard multiplier stacks by building type, applied as ranges, with the largest range measured first — and the profession's name for the person who does this is a quantity surveyor, which is an entire job created by the fact that the naive estimate is wrong by a compounding product.

    Other instances: the true cost of an employee, where salary is multiplied by payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, space and recruitment amortisation to give a loaded cost of 1.25–2× — and the multiplier is why "we can afford them at that salary" is a category error. Rocket mass fractions, where the payload is a small percentage of the launch mass and every subsystem multiplies the propellant needed, with the rocket equation making the compounding exponential rather than merely multiplicative. And the time a task will take, where the estimate covers the work and omits context-switching, review, deployment and the interruptions — which is why the planning fallacy is best treated as a multiplier applied to your own estimate rather than as a failure to think harder.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
