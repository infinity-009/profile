Tier 10 · AI-era systems Quantisation explained well nowhere else

Quantising to four bits bought eight times the concurrency, and the accuracy cost was a choice, not a law

gpu-memory-arithmetic already prices a card as weights, plus a KV cache that grows with concurrency and context, plus a fixed reserve — this lesson does not re-derive that formula, it only shrinks the weights term and asks what the shrinking costs. On one 80 GB H100, a 70B model's weights alone do not fit at FP16, fit with room for two 8,000-token sequences at FP8, and sixteen at INT4 — the identical formula, three bit counts. What that formula has no opinion on is price: OPT-175B's own published WikiText2 perplexity moves 0.4% at four bits done with a calibrated method and 14.6% at the identical four bits done by naive rounding, and at three bits the naive version does not merely get worse — its LAMBADA accuracy is reported as exactly zero. Quantisation is neither the free compression trick it is often sold as nor a fixed tax either way: it is a design decision whose price is set far more by the method than by the bit count.

Read first — gpu-memory-arithmetic

01

Sixteen sequences fit, or two, and the model's weights never changed shape

A 70B chat model, grouped-query attention, one 80 GB H100. Served at FP8, the earlier lesson in this tier priced it: 70 GB of weights, 8 GB left over, and at 8,000 tokens of context that is two concurrent conversations. Everything past the second request is refused, with 6 GB apparently free.

Drop the weights to INT4 — a quarter the bytes of FP8, an eighth of the BF16 the model actually trained in — and the same card, the same context length, the same formula now says sixteen. Nothing about the model’s architecture moved. Nothing was retrained. The only thing that changed is how many bits represent each of 70 billion numbers.

That is the half of this story everyone tells, and it is correctly the good half: an 8x rise in concurrency for changing a data type. Here is the half that gets left out. The same 175-billion parameter model, quantised to the same 4 bits by two different published methods, moved WikiText2 perplexity by 0.4% under one and 14.6% under the other. Same bytes on disk. Same card, same throughput, same 8x. A 40-fold difference in what it cost.

02

The belief this has to break: a smaller number that is somehow the same number

The belief this has to break
Quantisation is basically free. You are not changing what the model computes, only how precisely it stores the numbers it was already going to compute with — a compression trick, not a design decision.

That belief survives exactly as long as nobody asks how the rounding happened. Every weight in a matrix is a float; representing it in 4 bits instead of 16 means picking one of 16 grid points instead of one of 65,536, and the distance between the original value and the nearest grid point is an error that was not there before. That much is unavoidable and it is exactly what bytes-per- parameter measures — the compression ratio is a fact about the format, not a claim about damage.

What happens to that error afterward is not decided by the bit count. Round-to-nearest picks the closest grid point for every weight independently and moves on; the error is simply gone, absorbed into whatever the next layer does with a weight matrix that is now slightly wrong in 70 billion independent places. GPTQ picks one weight, rounds it, measures exactly how wrong that made the layer’s output, and pushes a correction into the weights it has not rounded yet — so the next weight is nudged to partly cancel the error the previous one just introduced.

Same input, same output format, same number of bits saved. One method discards the error it creates; the other spends it on compensating for itself. The GPTQ paper’s own numbers, later in this lesson, are two entirely different prices for an operation that a bytes-per-parameter count describes identically.

03

The intuition: two ways to throw away the low bits of a recording

A sound engineer needs to fit a 24-bit master onto a format that only stores 16 bits per sample. Eight bits of every sample have to go, and there is no way around that — the format has decided the compression ratio, exactly the way a bit-width decides bytes-per-parameter.

The lazy way: round every sample to the nearest 16-bit value and move to the next sample. This works, and on loud material nobody can tell. On a quiet passage — a fade-out, a held note — it introduces a distortion with real structure to it: the rounding error correlates with the signal, so it sounds like buzzing rather than like noise, because “distortion that correlates with the signal” is what a harmonic is.

The careful way: before rounding a sample, add a tiny, calibrated amount of noise (dither), and — going further — shape that noise so most of its energy lands in frequencies human hearing is least sensitive to (noise shaping), which works by tracking the rounding error at each sample and feeding a fraction of it into the next one, so the two partly cancel over time. Same 8 bits discarded. The engineer did not get to keep any of them. What changed is that the error was managed instead of merely produced.

GPTQ is noise shaping for a weight matrix: track the error, feed it forward, let the arithmetic that comes next partly absorb what the arithmetic that came before threw away. Round-to-nearest is the lazy master, and it is not wrong to use it — most of the time nobody can tell, until the passage gets quiet enough, or the model gets pushed to few enough bits, that the correlated error stops being inaudible.

The compression ratio and the damage are two different numbers, decided by two different things. The format decides the first. The method decides the second — and a method that discards its rounding error and a method that redistributes it can share a compression ratio and nothing else.

04

The mechanism: one term in a formula everyone already has, and a second question the formula never asks

The capacity half is not new arithmetic. gpu-memory-arithmetic’s own result is: VRAM is weights, plus a KV cache that grows with concurrency and context, plus a fixed reserve for activations and workspace, and how many sequences fit is whatever is left after the weights, divided by what one sequence costs. Quantising the weights touches exactly one term in that sum:

weight bytes = parameters × (bits per weight ÷ 8)

70 billion parameters at 16, 8, 4 and 3 bits: 140 GB, 70 GB, 35 GB, 26.25 GB. Nothing else in the capacity formula reads this number except the subtraction it appears in — the KV cache term is unchanged, the reserve is unchanged, and the concurrency that falls out is floor((card − weights − reserve) ÷ bytes-per-sequence), exactly gpu-memory- arithmetic’s own quotient. Computed as weightGBAtBits and maxConcurrencyAtBits in src/sim/quantisation.mjs, and checked against maxBatchFromMemory — the formula this generalises — at every bit width the two share.

The accuracy half is not arithmetic at all. There is no formula for “how much does rounding a weight cost a 175-billion-parameter language model” — it has to be measured, on a real model, on a real benchmark, which is why this lesson quotes a paper’s table instead of deriving one. Two methods, same target bit counts:

  • Round-to-nearest (RTN). Every weight is snapped to the closest point on the quantisation grid, independently of every other weight. No calibration data, negligible compute — it is what “just cast the tensor to a lower dtype” does.
  • GPTQ. One-shot and calibrated on a small sample of ordinary text (128 unlabelled segments in the original paper — no task-specific data, so the result is genuinely zero-shot). Weights are quantised one at a time; each time a weight is rounded, the error that introduces is measured against that layer’s actual input statistics (its inverse-Hessian) and pushed into the weights not yet rounded, so later weights are nudged to partly compensate for what earlier ones just got wrong. A few GPU-hours, even at 175 billion parameters — orders of magnitude cheaper than retraining, and the whole point of a post-training method.

Both methods produce a tensor of the identical bit width, the identical number of bytes, and the identical concurrency on the identical card. Only one of them spent any effort on where the error that bit width forces goes.

Three things in that table, in order of how much they should change what you do on Monday.

The bit count and the price are different axes. 4-bit GPTQ (+0.4%) costs less than 3-bit GPTQ (+4.1%), which is the ordering everyone expects — fewer bits, more damage. But 4-bit round-to-nearest (+14.6%) costs more than 3-bit GPTQ (+4.1%), at more bits. A method can lose to a smaller, better-quantised model. Bits-per-weight alone does not even rank these correctly, let alone price them.

3-bit round-to-nearest is not a worse version of 4-bit round-to-nearest — it is a different kind of failure. Going from +14.6% to ≈875% is not the same curve continuing; a WikiText2 score of 7,300 paired with 0% LAMBADA accuracy means the model has stopped producing usable text, not that it produces slightly worse text. GPTQ crossing from 4 to 3 bits costs an extra 3.7 percentage points of perplexity delta. Round-to-nearest crossing the same boundary costs the entire model.

Even 8 bits is not automatically safe. Dettmers, Lewis, Belkada & Zettlemoyer’s LLM.int8() (NeurIPS 2022) found that a naive per-tensor 8-bit baseline tracks 16-bit accuracy only up to 6.7 billion parameters, then collapses once “systematic outlier features” — a handful of dimensions with magnitudes up to 20x the rest — start appearing in every transformer layer. Zeroing just those outlier dimensions alone costs 600-1000% perplexity on their own measurements. LLM.int8()‘s fix — 16-bit precision for the outlier columns, 8-bit for the other 99.9% — is reported to run OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B “without any performance degradation” at all. Same 8 bits, same collapse risk, and it shows up at a higher bit count than the GPTQ table above, which is why “8-bit is basically lossless” is a claim about a specific method’s engineering, not about the number 8.

The trade-off: one lever that is deterministic, one that is a design decision, and one this lesson leaves alone

The two axes compose, and neither substitutes for the other. Choosing a low bit count with a naive method is how a team gets 3-bit round-to-nearest’s collapse while believing they made “the same trade-off, just further along it” as 4-bit GPTQ. Choosing a calibrated method does not make the bit count free either — GPTQ at 3-bit still costs 4.1% perplexity, a real and nonzero number, just a survivable one. The honest specification of a quantised model is a pair: bit count, and the method that produced it — a bit count alone specifies the memory saved and nothing about what it cost.

Quantising the KV cache is the same formula, a different term, and this lesson stops at the door. The rig’s + quantise KV cache button doubles concurrency again by halving the other half of the capacity equation — bytes per cached token, which kv-cache covers in full — and every accuracy number on this page was measured with an unquantised, full-precision cache. Whether a quantised KV cache costs the same, less, or dramatically more accuracy than the equivalent quantised weights is a real, separate question, and the honest answer is that attention reads that cache through a softmax on every step, which has historically behaved differently under precision loss than a weight matrix multiply does. Stacking the two levers is real and it is free memory-wise; treating them as one accuracy line item is not something the numbers above support.

07

In the wild

GPTQ’s own worked example is this lesson’s formula, from the source. Quantised to 3 bits, OPT-175B takes about 63 GB, plus roughly 9 GB for the KV cache at a 2,048-token maximum — small enough for a single 80 GB A100, dynamically dequantising layers as they are needed. The paper notes this explicitly does not work at 4 bits on the same card. For reference: full FP16 needs five 80 GB GPUs, and LLM.int8()‘s 8-bit needs three. One model, four precisions, and the GPU count is the capacity formula this lesson has been drawing all along, reported as a fact about a real deployment rather than derived from one.

Both papers shipped as libraries almost immediately, which is itself evidence the accuracy question is taken seriously rather than assumed away. LLM.int8() became bitsandbytes, merged into Hugging Face Transformers as an load_in_8bit option. GPTQ checkpoints are served today by vLLM, TensorRT-LLM and Transformers as one of several supported quantised formats, alongside AWQ — Lin et al., “Activation-aware Weight Quantization” (MLSys 2024, Best Paper) — a later calibrated method that identifies the roughly 1% of weight channels that matter most by looking at activation magnitudes rather than a Hessian, and protects them with a per-channel scale instead of an error-redistribution pass. Three different calibration strategies, one shared premise: the bit count was never the hard part, and none of the three ship without a published accuracy table next to their compression ratio.

Framework defaults quietly pick a method for you. A one-line “cast to int8” or a checkpoint converter’s default flag is round-to-nearest unless a library specifically documents otherwise — calibration is opt-in because it costs GPU-hours and calibration data the naive path does not need. Reading past the compression-ratio headline to find out which method produced a quantised checkpoint is the single check this whole lesson reduces to.

The reframing worth keeping: a bit count prices the memory saved exactly and says nothing about the accuracy spent. That price is set by what the rounding method does with the error it is forced to create — discard it, and the same bits cost far more; measure and redistribute it, and the same bits cost far less. gpu-memory-arithmetic’s formula answers “how many sequences fit.” This lesson exists because that formula has no opinion on the other question, and the other question is the one with a paper’s worth of nonzero answers attached to it.

Same 8 bits, same 175B model; the naive version fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the bit count.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    You quantise both the weights to INT4 and the KV cache to FP8 on the same 70B/H100/8k setup. Concurrency rises from 16 to 32. Does the 0.4%-perplexity citation above still describe the total accuracy cost?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it describes only the weights' cost. Quantising the KV cache is a second lever on the same capacity formula: it shrinks the other term, not the one GPTQ's numbers were measured against, and it has its own accuracy behaviour that this lesson does not price. Attention reads the cache through a softmax, which is more sensitive to precision loss than a weight matrix multiply is. Stacking two levers doubles the concurrency gain for free; it does not entitle you to add zero to the accuracy bill twice.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team ships a recommendation model at INT8 using their framework's default post-training quantisation — a naive per-tensor round — and offline evaluation shows no regression on the standard benchmark. Three months later, one device segment starts returning garbage recommendations. What likely happened, and what does this lesson say to check before trusting "no regression"?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The naive round-to-nearest baseline is exactly the failure mode LLM.int8() documents: it is fine on average and fails once a systematic outlier appears, and an outlier can be a property of one slice of the input distribution — a device, a locale, a feature range — rather than of the model's typical behaviour. "No regression on the standard benchmark" measured an average; the failure is conditional on a slice the benchmark never isolated. What to check first: the quantisation range and clipping statistics segmented the same way the benchmark is segmented, not only in aggregate — GPTQ's and LLM.int8()'s entire contribution is engineering around exactly this kind of hidden, input-conditional error concentration, not a headline percentage.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: shrinking a representation loses information by an amount the format decides exactly, but whether that loss compounds or stays bounded is a property of the method, not of the compression ratio. Name instances outside machine learning.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Kahan summation is the closest structural match: naively summing a long list of floats in reduced precision lets each rounding error compound into the next addition, while Kahan's algorithm tracks the error term explicitly and feeds it back into the next term — the same shape as GPTQ tracking a weight's rounding error and feeding it into the weights not yet quantised. Audio bit-depth reduction: truncating a 24-bit recording to 16-bit naively produces audible quantisation distortion at low volumes, while dithering adds calibrated noise before rounding and noise-shaping pushes the resulant error into frequencies the ear is least sensitive to — the same 8 bits discarded, an error-shaping method instead of a error-discarding one. Apportionment in elections: rounding vote shares to whole seats independently can violate the total seat count or produce paradoxes, while the largest- remainder and Sainte-Laguë methods explicitly track and redistribute the rounding remainder. In every case the compression ratio is set by the format and is not in dispute; the actual damage is a decision made inside the rounding step, and "we compressed by 2x" specifies nothing about which kind of method did it.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
