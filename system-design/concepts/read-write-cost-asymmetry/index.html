Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Read/write asymmetry explained well nowhere else

One field took the record from 1,010 bytes to 1,025, and the write bill doubled

A write unit covers 1 KB. A read unit covers 4 KB, or 8 KB if you can live with eventual consistency — so per byte a write costs four to eight times a read, and asking for strong consistency doubles the read bill exactly. Then the units are rounded up per operation, which moves the bill off your total bytes and onto your item size distribution. The mean is the wrong statistic, and the right one is computable before you build.

01

Fifteen bytes

A device telemetry table. Firmware on a few hundred thousand devices emits the same twelve numeric fields every few minutes, so every record is the same size: 1,010 bytes. The table takes 400 million writes and 200 million reads a month. It has no secondary indexes. The store bills it in two kinds of unit — one for reads, one for writes — and this month the bill is $262.50, of which $250.00 is writes and $12.50 is reads.

Somebody adds a field. A firmware version string, twenty bytes. Every record is now 1,025 bytes: 1.5% more data, the same devices, the same traffic, the same table, the same number of operations.

The write bill goes from $250.00 to $500.00. Exactly double.

Nothing is broken and nothing is misconfigured. The write unit covers 1 KB, units are charged per operation, and a fraction of a unit is not a thing you can buy — so a 1,010-byte record was one unit and a 1,025-byte record is two. Every write in the table crossed the same line on the same day, because every record in the table is the same size.

The two prices on this page are illustrative inputs, dated 2026-08, low confidence, and probably wrong for your bill by some factor. The unit rule — 1 KB per write unit, 4 KB per strongly-consistent read unit, half a unit for an eventually-consistent read — is a published billing rule rather than a price, and it is stated here from memory: check it against the current documentation before you quote it. Everything below is a ratio, a break-even or a structural claim, so it survives both prices being wrong; if the rule’s constants change, the arithmetic does not.

02

So remove some bytes — and watch nothing at all happen

The belief this lesson is breaking is the one the bill appears to confirm: cost tracks bytes, so to spend less, store less. The team’s next move follows from it. They audit the record, shorten attribute names, drop two fields nobody reads, and get the item from 1,025 bytes back down to 1,020. The write bill halves, and the fix is declared a success.

Then they do the same audit on the other table — product documents, mean item 2,400 bytes — and remove 15% of the bytes from every item. The bill does not move at all. Not a little: not at all, because 2,040 bytes and 2,400 bytes are both between the second and third kilobyte boundary and therefore both cost exactly three write units.

Cost is a staircase in item size. It is flat across a tread and it jumps at a riser, so removing bytes pays only when it moves items across a boundary, and adding bytes is free until it does. Which means the first audit did not succeed because it removed five bytes. It succeeded because it removed the right five bytes, and nobody involved could have told you which those were.

Predict A table's items are all exactly 1,014 bytes and it takes 400 million writes a month, costing $250. A new field takes every item to 1,034 bytes — 2% more data, identical traffic. Write units are charged per operation and rounded up to the next whole kilobyte. What happens to the write bill?
ceil(1014 ÷ 1024) is 1 unit and ceil(1034 ÷ 1024) is 2, so 400 million writes go from 400 million units to 800 million and the bill goes from $250 to $500 — a 2.0× increase for 2.0% more data. The first option is the belief this lesson breaks: the bill does not track bytes, it tracks rounded-up units per operation. The second is the shape people fall back on once they know rounding exists — a smooth margin — and rounding is not a margin, it is a cliff. The last one confuses the 4 KB read block with the 1 KB write block, which is the asymmetry this lesson is about: the same item crosses read boundaries a quarter as often as it crosses write boundaries.
Show it without answering

Three things in that rig are worth naming.

The two gradients are not a detail, they are the pricing. A write unit covers 1 KB and an eventually-consistent read unit stretches to 8 KB, so per byte a write costs 8× an eventual read and 4× a strongly-consistent one. That is a property of the size allowances, not of a price list, and no discount moves it.

The amber line is what you pay and the slate dot is what you calculated. At identical item sizes they agree. Open the spread and they separate, because the average of a step function is not the step function of an average — and total bytes are held constant the whole time.

The strong-consistency curve is exactly twice the eventual one, at every size. Not roughly: measured across all four workloads in this lesson, the read bill under strong consistency is 2.000× the eventual one, and nothing else on the bill changes. It is the only line item in this entire tier that a consistency choice moves directly.

03

The intuition: the courier's weight bands

You ship parcels. The courier charges by weight band: nought to one kilo is one price, one to two kilos is the next, and so on. Four things follow, and only the first is obvious.

A 1.01 kg parcel costs what a 2.00 kg parcel costs. The 990 grams of unused allowance is paid for and thrown away.

Your total tonnage does not predict your bill. Two warehouses shipping the same tonnage in the same number of parcels can pay wildly different amounts, because one of them ships parcels at 0.98 kg and the other at 1.02 kg. To predict the bill you need the distribution of parcel weights and, specifically, how much of it sits just above a band edge. Everyone who has run a fulfilment operation knows this, which is why they argue about packaging materials by the gram.

Removing weight is usually free work. Taking 200 grams out of a 1.6 kg parcel saves nothing. Taking 30 grams out of a 1.02 kg parcel saves an entire band. The saving is not in the weight, it is in the distance to the edge, and only one of those is on your dashboard.

And the bands are not the same size in both directions. Sending is billed in one-kilo bands and receiving in four-kilo bands. So the same parcel costs four times as much to send as to receive, and a business that decided to send more and receive less — for perfectly good reasons having nothing to do with money — has quadrupled the rate at which it crosses band edges.

The fourth point is the one with no everyday equivalent, and it is where the analogy stops being cute: a courier that copied every parcel you sent to four other addresses, and charged you a band for each copy, would be a secondary index.

04

The mechanism: two allowances, one ceiling, and a sum over boundaries

The rule, and the asymmetry that falls out of it

There are two kinds of unit and they have different size allowances.

OperationBlock sizeUnits per blockBytes one unit covers
write1 KB11,024
write, transactional1 KB2512
read, strongly consistent4 KB14,096
read, eventually consistent4 KB0.58,192
read, transactional4 KB22,048

The last column is the whole asymmetry and it contains no money:

per byte, a write costs 4× a strong read and 8× an eventual read

1,024 against 4,096 and 8,192 bytes per unit. A quotient of two published allowances, so no price list, region, discount or commitment changes it. And the two read rows differ by exactly 2, which means strong consistency doubles the read bill — a correctness decision with a line item.

Money multiplies that by one more ratio. A write unit is priced at roughly 5× a read unit, and that multiple has survived a general price cut and holds in both billing modes — per-request and per-hour — which is what makes it structural rather than a tariff. Combining the two: per byte, a write costs about 20× a strongly-consistent read and 40× an eventually-consistent one.

Note where the multipliers sit. unitsPerBlock is applied outside the ceiling, so a transactional write of a 1,229-byte item is 2 × ceil(1229 ÷ 1024) = 4 units, not ceil(2 × 1229 ÷ 1024) = 3. Rounding happens once, per operation, on the item; the multiplier lands on the rounded figure afterwards. Getting that order backwards understates every transactional bill, which is why the model asserts both candidate answers rather than just the right one.

The ceiling, and why it moves the bill onto a distribution

Units are charged per operation and rounded up. So for an item of size S and a block of B, one operation is billed ceil(S / B) blocks, and what you pay over a month is the average of that:

you are billed E[ceil(S / B)], and you calculated E[S] / B

The first is the expected value of a step function of the item size. The second is the step function evaluated at the mean, which is the arithmetic everybody does. They are different numbers, and every finding on this page is a consequence of the difference.

That expectation has an identity worth carrying, because it converts an average over a step function into a sum of probabilities:

E[ceil(S / B)] = P(S over 0) + P(S over B) + P(S over 2B) + …

The layer-cake identity: ceil(S/B) counts how many of the thresholds 0, B, 2B, … the item exceeds, so its expectation is the sum of the chances of exceeding each. Every term is the share of items above one boundary.

Read that sum twice and two things fall out that no amount of staring at a mean will give you.

The first term is 1, for every distribution. Every operation pays at least one whole unit. So a 300-byte item pays for 1,024 bytes on the write side and for 4,096 on the read side, and its bill is almost entirely padding: measured on an activity feed of 300-byte items, rounding alone gets you 300 bytes per write unit against the 1,024 the rule advertises — 3.4× the advertised per-byte price — and the read side is billed 1,265% more bytes than it stores.

Moving a share of items across a boundary costs exactly that share of a unit. Not roughly: exactly, because that share is one term of the sum. Shift 1% of your items from just under a boundary to just over it and the bill rises by 0.01 units per operation. This is the actionable quantity, it is measurable from a size histogram you probably already have, and it is the thing a mean cannot express.

The window: what a mean does and does not tell you

Because x ≤ ceil(x) < x + 1, the billed block count is pinned to a window exactly one block wide:

E[S] / B ≤ E[ceil(S / B)] < E[S] / B + 1

So rounding waste is at most one whole block per operation and at least none — and knowing the mean tells you which window you are in, never where in it you sit. On items near the block size, that window is most of the bill.

Both ends are reachable, which is what makes it a window rather than a bound nobody meets. At a mean of 1,536 bytes, three distributions with the same mean and the same total bytes:

Item sizesMeanWrite units per operationPadding paid for
half at 1,024 B, half at 2,048 B1,536 B1.5000%
every item 1,536 B1,536 B2.00033%
half at 1 B, half at 3,073 B1,537 B2.50063%

A 67% range in the bill at a fixed mean. The first row lands every item exactly on a boundary and wastes nothing; the last sits every item one byte over one and wastes almost a whole block each time.

The realistic version of that is the one in the rig. Take the telemetry table’s 1,010-byte items: identical, every write is 1.000 units. Now hold the mean at exactly 1,010 bytes and spread the sizes by ±36% — the same devices, the same total bytes, just variable-length strings instead of fixed-width fields — and the average write becomes 1.479 units. 48% more money for the same data.

And for items spread smoothly across many blocks the waste converges on half a block per operation: measured against the exponential’s closed form, an item mean of 64 KB wastes 0.500 blocks, and a mean of 8 KB wastes 0.510 — matching the analytic m/B + 1/2 + B/12m to five decimal places. Which is the one case where a mean is enough, and it is the case where rounding stopped mattering.

Where a secondary index lands

An index is a second copy of the item, written every time the item is written, and billed as its own operation with its own ceiling. It adds nothing to the read path.

each index adds one write of its projected size · reads are untouched

So four indexes projecting the whole item make every write cost exactly 5×. And because each index entry gets its own ceiling, a keys-only projection of 80 bytes is not free — it is a whole write unit, the same as a 1 KB item.

Two more billing rules compound with it, and both are about what size the ceiling is applied to:

  • An update is billed on the item’s size, not the change’s. Changing one byte of a 6 KB item costs six write units. This is the rule that makes denormalising expensive, and it is the rule people meet last.
  • How many ceilings a read applies depends on the shape of the access, not on the bytes. One range query over a set of items sums their sizes and rounds once; twenty-one individual point reads round twenty-one times. Same bytes, 10.5 read units against 1. The number of rounding events is an architectural variable, and it is the one you control most cheaply.

The write side owns every one of these bills, and the read side owns every one of the dashboards. Write share runs from 39% to 99%, and the only row under half is the catalogue with its indexes removed. A monitoring setup that reports read latency and read throughput is watching the cheap half.

Rounding is the whole story at the small end and a rounding error at the large end. The 300-byte feed pays 3.4× the advertised per-byte write price from rounding alone, and 10.2× once its two indexes are counted; against a bill charged on bytes with the same allowances and no rounding at all, it pays 10.55×. The 9 KB documents table pays a 5.7% rounding tax and its bill is decided by the raw per-byte asymmetry instead.

Four indexes are worth 2.55× on the whole bill and nothing at all on the read path. The catalogue’s writes go from 2.845 units to 14.226 — exactly 5×, because each index entry is the same size as the item and rounds identically — and its read units do not change by a single bit. The bill goes from $137.40 to $350.79 and flips from read-dominated to write-dominated. That is the price of a read-optimised schema, and it is entirely on the write side.

Strong consistency costs exactly double on reads and between 1.4% and 24% on the bill. The read multiplier is 2.000 on every workload. What it does to the total is decided by how read-heavy you are: +24.0% on the catalogue, +1.4% on the documents table. So “we made it strongly consistent” is a cheap sentence on a write-heavy table and an expensive one on a read-heavy table, and nobody checks which they have before saying it.

Where a write unit actually goes

17.6% of the write is padding and 65.9% is duplicated bytes. Both are addressable and they are addressed differently: padding by moving the item relative to a boundary, duplication by projecting fewer attributes. Only the hatched block is irreducible, and it is a sixth of what is charged.

So: denormalise, or not?

The closing question, and the whole point of doing this arithmetic before building rather than after. An entity is a header of 400 bytes plus twenty child records of 300 bytes — 6,400 bytes all told. Serve a page from twenty-one items, or from one denormalised item?

Current read shapeNormalised readDenormalised readRead units savedWrite amplificationDenormalising pays above
one range query (sizes summed, rounded once)1.01.007×never
twenty-one point reads, no index10.51.09.57×3.16 reads per write
twenty-one point reads, 2 indexes10.51.09.57×9.47 reads per write
twenty-one point reads, 4 indexes10.51.09.57×15.79 reads per write

The top row is the result worth pinning above a desk. If the read is already one range query, denormalising saves exactly zero read units at any traffic mix, and multiplies every single-field write by seven. 6,400 bytes crosses two 4 KB boundaries whether it arrives as one item or twenty-one, so there was never a read win to collect — and the write cost is real, because an update is billed on the whole item.

The read win people are actually chasing is a win against rounding events, not against bytes: twenty-one point reads round twenty-one times and pay 10.5 units for 6,400 bytes. Where that is the current shape, denormalising does pay — above a break-even that every index pushes further away, because an index multiplies the thing denormalising made bigger. Four indexes take it from 3.16 to 15.79 reads per write, and the answer flips from “obviously yes” to “only for a genuinely read-mostly entity”.

The number I expected to be different

I expected the realised per-byte asymmetry to be the advertised 8×. It is not, and it is smaller in both directions. On the 9 KB documents table it measures 6.89×, because a 4 KB block wastes more of a 9 KB item than a 1 KB block does — so rounding partly offsets the asymmetry. And at the small end it collapses to 2×, because both sides pay their one-block minimum and the ratio becomes the ratio of the minimums rather than of the allowances. The advertised 8× is a limit approached from below by large items, and quoting it at a workload of small items overstates the write penalty by a factor of four. Rounding does not only add cost; it compresses the difference between the two sides, which was the opposite of what I assumed before measuring it.

06

The trade-off: this arithmetic will talk you into a schema you should not want

Everything above prices two line items. It does not price the reasons those line items exist.

Sitting just under a boundary is a fragile design, and the arithmetic makes it look clever. The telemetry table was 14 bytes from doubling its write bill and nobody knew. Optimising toward that state — shaving attribute names to squeeze under 1,024 bytes — buys a real saving and installs a cliff under the schema: the next field, the next locale, the next longer identifier tips it over, and the person who adds that field has no idea they are spending $250 a month. If you do it, the honest version is to write the headroom down in the schema and alert on it, which is not a thing anyone does.

Short attribute names are a legitimate technique and a bad habit. Attribute names count toward item size, so renaming customerEmailAddress to e is real money. It is also a schema nobody can read, in a store with no central schema to look it up in, and the practice long outlives the boundary that justified it. This is a trade against comprehensibility, and comprehensibility is what you spend on-call minutes buying.

Choosing eventual consistency to halve the read bill is choosing a correctness property for a financial reason. The 2× is real and the temptation is real, and it is still backwards. The read bill is the worst input to a consistency decision, because the cost of being wrong does not appear in it at all — and on three of the four workloads above, strong consistency costs under 5% of the total anyway. Decide consistency on the semantics, then let this arithmetic tell you what it cost.

Fewer, larger items reduce rounding events and create contention. Every “pack it into one item” move that saves boundary crossings also enlarges the unit of a write, concentrates traffic on one key, and makes every update rewrite the whole thing — which is where this lesson hands off to hot partitions. The rounding win and the contention loss are both real and they are not in the same units.

Two costs are missing from the model entirely. A secondary index also stores a second copy, which is a per-GB charge this model does not include, so its index numbers are an understatement. And a multi-region deployment multiplies the write side again, per region — the term that turns a write-heavy design from expensive into a decision about geography. Both push the same way as everything above, which is the only reason the omission is safe.

And the rule itself is a vendor’s, stated from memory. The 1 KB and 4 KB constants, the half-unit for eventual reads, the doubling for transactions, the aggregate rounding on a range query — check every one against current documentation before quoting a figure at anybody. What survives being wrong about the constants is the shape: two allowances of different sizes, a ceiling per operation, and a multiplier per index. Every storage system has all three. Most of them do not tell you the numbers.

07

In the wild

The unit rule is published, which is the unusual part. DynamoDB’s developer documentation states the capacity-unit definitions directly: a write unit for an item up to 1 KB, a read unit for a strongly-consistent read up to 4 KB, half a unit for an eventually-consistent one, double for transactional operations, and item sizes rounded up. Almost no other store publishes the two sides separately — an SSD-backed volume sells you IOPS that do not distinguish them, and a managed relational database sells you an instance — so the asymmetry is present everywhere and legible in approximately one place. That is the entire reason this lesson uses those units: not because the vendor matters, but because the arithmetic is visible.

The write side of the asymmetry is a physical fact before it is a billing one. A write must be made durable, replicated to a quorum, and applied to every index; a read touches one replica and returns. B-tree vs LSM is the same story one layer down: a 128-byte row update writes kilobytes to disk because a page is the unit of I/O, which is the identical ceiling applied to a physical block instead of a billing block. A price list that charges 4× more per byte to write is reporting write amplification, and the number it reports is smaller than the physical one.

Single-table design is this arithmetic taken seriously. The DynamoDB practice associated with Rick Houlihan’s re:Invent talks — collapsing several entity types into one table so a single range query returns everything a page needs — is usually explained as avoiding joins. In unit terms it is precisely the rounding-events argument from slot 4: one query over a partition rounds once, and the equivalent fan-out of point reads rounds per item. The practice is right and the usual justification undersells it, because “avoids a join” sounds like a latency argument and it is also a factor of ten on the read bill.

Filesystems have known the distribution result for forty years. Internal fragmentation — a file occupying whole blocks, so the last block is partly wasted — is the same E[ceil(S/B)], and the textbook results are the same two: waste tends to half a block for files large relative to the block, and to a whole block for files smaller than one. Douceur and Bolosky’s large-scale study of filesystem contents (1999) found file sizes to be roughly lognormal, which is exactly why the mean is the wrong statistic there too: a lognormal has most of its count in the small sizes and most of its bytes in the large ones, so the number of rounding events and the volume of data are driven by different parts of the same distribution.

And the practice that gets this right outside computing is parcel banding. Fulfilment operations manage packaging to the gram, not because grams matter but because band edges do, and they forecast shipping cost from a parcel weight distribution rather than from total tonnage. The equivalent discipline here is to keep a histogram of item sizes next to the boundaries, and to alert on headroom to the next boundary rather than on mean item size. Nobody in shipping proposes reducing total tonnage as a cost programme, because the answer is obviously “only if it moves parcels between bands”.

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A write unit covers 1 KB and a read unit covers 4 KB strongly consistent or 8 KB eventually, so per byte a write costs 4× a strong read and 8× an eventual one
  • Units are rounded up per operation, so what you are billed is the expected value of ceil(size ÷ block) and not the mean size divided by the block — the item size distribution decides the bill, not the total bytes
  • The billed block count always lies in a window one whole block wide above mean ÷ block, so the mean pins the bill only to within a whole block per operation
  • The expected block count equals the sum over boundaries of the share of items above each one, so moving a share of items across a boundary costs exactly that share of a unit per operation
  • The first term of that sum is 1 for every distribution, so every operation pays at least one whole unit and a small item is almost entirely padding
  • Strong consistency doubles the read bill exactly and changes nothing else, which makes it a consistency decision with a line on an invoice
  • Every secondary index adds one write of its own projected size and nothing at all to the read path, so a read-optimised schema is a write-cost decision
  • An update is billed on the size of the item, not the size of the change, so denormalising multiplies the cost of every single-field write

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Two tables take identical traffic and hold near-identical volumes of data. One has items of 1,014 bytes and the other 1,034 bytes. What is the difference in the write bill, and why is the mean item size not enough to tell you?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The write bill is exactly double, for 2.0% more data. Write units are charged per operation and rounded up to the next kilobyte, so ceil(1014 ÷ 1024) is one unit and ceil(1034 ÷ 1024) is two. At 400 million writes a month that is $250 against $500. The mean is not enough because what you are billed is the average of a step function of the size, and the average of a step function is not the step function of an average: what decides the bill is the share of items sitting above each boundary. Measured on identical 1,010-byte items the average write is 1.00 units; hold that mean and spread the sizes by ±36% and the average write becomes 1.479 units — 48% more money for the same bytes and the same traffic.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A team wants to cut its storage bill and removes 15% of the bytes from every item. Nothing happens. Then they add one small field back and the bill jumps 20%. What is the shape of the cost function, and what does it imply about where optimisation effort belongs?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A staircase. Cost is flat inside a tread and jumps at a riser, so removing bytes pays only if it moves items across a boundary, and adding bytes is free until it does. Effort belongs at the boundary, not on the bytes: the actionable quantity is the distance from the mass of your distribution to the next boundary, and the diagnostic is the share of items just above each one — because moving a share ε of items across a boundary changes the bill by exactly ε units per operation. It also means the win is fragile: a design sitting two bytes under a boundary has a cliff under it, and the next field pushes it over.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A read-optimised schema adds four secondary indexes so every query is served by an index. What does that cost, where does the cost land, and what has changed on the read path?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Each index is a second write of its own projected item, so four full-projection indexes make every write cost exactly 5× — measured, 2.85 write units becoming 14.23 — and add nothing to the read path, which is unchanged to the last bit. On the measured catalogue workload that multiplies the whole monthly bill by 2.55, from $137.40 to $350.79, and flips it from read-dominated at 39% writes to write-dominated at 76%. Projecting keys only is cheaper and not cheap: each index entry still rounds up to a whole write unit, so four keys-only indexes carrying 14% of the bytes written account for 58% of the write units. The general form is that a read-optimised schema is a write-cost decision, and it is computable from the schema before any of it is built.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You are asked whether to denormalise an entity — a header plus twenty child records — into one item so a page load is a single read. What do you need to know before answering, and which of the two answers is more often right than people expect?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    You need to know how many rounding events the current read performs. If the existing read is one range query over the set, its size is summed and rounded once, so it already costs what the single big item would: 6,400 bytes is 2 blocks either way, and denormalising saves zero read units while multiplying every single-field write by 7, because an update is billed on the item's size. That answer — denormalising buys nothing at any traffic mix — is far more often right than people expect. If instead the read is twenty-one separate point reads, each rounds separately: 10.5 read units against 1, and now denormalising does pay, but only above a break-even of 3.16 reads per write with no indexes, 9.47 with two and 15.79 with four. Every index raises the bar, because it multiplies the thing denormalising made bigger.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    An object storage tier bills a minimum of 128 KB per object and a minimum of 30 days of storage per object, whatever you actually store. You have 40 million objects averaging 30 KB, kept for 5 days. Apply this lesson's arithmetic and say which statistic you need.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is the identical arithmetic on two axes at once, and the block sizes are simply larger. On the size axis you are billed max(128 KB, size) per object, so the quantity is the expected value of that maximum and not 40 million × 30 KB — with a 30 KB mean you are billed at least 4.3× the bytes you store, and the mean is again insufficient because what matters is the share of objects below 128 KB: a workload with the same mean made of 5 KB and 500 KB objects pays much less than one where every object is 30 KB. On the duration axis you are billed max(30 days, lifetime), so a 5-day lifetime pays 6×, and the actionable statistic is the share of objects deleted before day 30. The transferable rule: wherever a resource is sold with a minimum billable increment, the billed quantity is an expectation of a rounding function, and any statistic that averages before rounding is the wrong one. The design responses are the same two as well — pack small objects together to cross fewer boundaries, or keep them on a tier whose increment matches their size.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a resource sold in indivisible increments, charged per transaction and rounded up, so what you pay is decided by how each transaction's size sits against the increment rather than by the total quantity consumed. Name instances outside computing, and say what the wrong statistic is in each.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Legal and consultancy billing in six-minute units is the sharpest one, and the profession has known it for decades: a one-minute phone call bills a tenth of an hour, so a day of many short tasks bills far more than a day of the same hours spent on one long one. The wrong statistic is hours worked; the right one is the number of distinct billable events and their length distribution. Others, each with its wrong statistic: parking charged by the started hour — a 61-minute stay pays for two — where the wrong statistic is total minutes parked and the right one is the distribution of stay lengths against the hour mark; postal and courier weight bands, where total tonnage shipped tells you nothing and the parcel weight distribution tells you everything, which is why fulfilment operations obsess over packaging weight within a few grams of a band; taxi meters that tick per started increment of distance or time, where a route of many short hops costs more than one long trip of the same length; hospital and car-hire billing per started day, where a discharge at 1 am pays for the whole day; and utility connection charges or minimum consumption blocks, where a household using very little pays almost the same as one using the whole block. In every case the two design responses are also the same: make each transaction bigger so it crosses fewer boundaries, or move to a tariff whose increment matches the size of the thing you actually do.
    Did you have that?
