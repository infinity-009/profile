Tier 3 · Storage and state B-tree vs LSM explained well nowhere else

Your 128-byte row update writes 16 kilobytes to disk

Every database picks one of two ways to keep data on disk, and the choice determines whether your workload is fast or hopeless. The usual summary — "B-trees for reads, LSM for writes" — is not wrong so much as missing the number that decides it, which is a property of your hardware and not of either data structure.

Read first — latency-numbersback-of-envelope

01

The disk is doing 400 MB/s and you are writing 3 MB/s of data

A service updates one column on one row, a few thousand times a second. The row is about 128 bytes. So you are asking the database to durably record roughly 128 × 3,000 = 384 KB/s of change.

The drive reports 49 MB/s of writes. That is 128 times what you asked for. Nobody is doing anything wrong, no query is missing an index, and the number is not a monitoring artefact.

Here is where it goes. The row lives in an 8 KB page, and the page is the unit of I/O — the smallest thing the storage engine can atomically read or write. Changing 128 bytes means writing 8,192. That is 64×. Then the write-ahead log records it, and because this page has not been touched since the last checkpoint, Postgres logs a full-page image rather than a delta — because a partially-written 8 KB page is unrecoverable and the WAL is what protects against it. 8,192 more bytes. 128× total.

Then the checkpoint passes, and for a while the WAL logs deltas instead, and your write rate drops by half without anyone changing anything. That sawtooth in your I/O graph is the checkpoint cycle, and it is the first sign that you are looking at an engine and not at a workload.

02

Three engines, four columns, and no winner

The belief this has to break
B-trees are for reads and LSM trees are for writes, and LSM trees have low write amplification.

The first half is a reasonable rule of thumb. The second half is false and it is the one people act on.

At realistic parameters — a 128 GB dataset, a 64 MB memtable, size ratio 10 — a levelled LSM tree rewrites each entry 41 times. A B-tree rewrites it 65–128 times. Those are the same order of magnitude. The LSM is not writing dramatically less; it is writing sequentially, and that only matters as much as your device makes it matter.

On a 7,200 rpm disk, sequential is 195× more efficient per byte and the LSM wins writes by 610×. On NVMe the bandwidth advantage is 1.0× — a saturated link is a saturated link — and the gap falls to 3.1×, which comes entirely from writing three times fewer bytes.

Most of what is written about LSM trees was written for the first machine.

Predict 128-byte rows, 8 KB pages, a 128 GB dataset. How does a levelled LSM's write amplification compare with a B-tree's?
B-tree: 128× (8192/128 page, doubled by the full-page WAL image). Levelled LSM: 1 + T·L = 1 + 10×4 = 41×. So about 3× lower — the same order of magnitude, not the order-of-magnitude win the folklore implies. Everything else the LSM gains comes from those 41 writes being sequential, which is a fact about your drive.
Show it without answering

Four things to do with it.

Press 7.2k HDD, then NVMe SSD. The amplification columns do not change — they are properties of the data structures. The throughput column changes by a factor of 200, and the ranking stays but the margin collapses from 610× to 3.1×. A decision that was obvious in 2005 is a judgement call now.

Drag row size. At 64 bytes the B-tree amplifies 256×; at 8 KB it amplifies 2×. Same engine, same drive, same query — a 128× swing, purchased entirely by making rows fatter than a page fraction. This is the most powerful and least discussed lever in the lesson.

Look at range scan. The B-tree is 1×, always: find the leaf, then walk siblings. Levelled LSM is 5×, tiered is 41×. No Bloom filter helps, because a filter answers a question about an exact key and a scan does not have one.

Drag size ratio T. Write amplification is 1 + T·L where L = log_T(data/memtable), and those two terms fight: large T means fewer levels but more rewriting per level. At T=2 it is 23×, at T=10 it is 41×, at T=50 it is 101×. RocksDB’s default of 10 is a tuned compromise, not an obvious value.

03

The intuition: a filing cabinet and a stack of receipts

The B-tree is a filing cabinet. Every document has one correct place, sorted, and to file something you open the right drawer, find the right folder, and put it in. Reading is excellent — you know exactly where to look, and everything near it is right there. Filing is expensive, because you have to find the place first, and because taking a folder out and putting it back is a whole physical operation regardless of whether you changed one word or rewrote the page.

The LSM tree is a stack of dated receipts. You never file anything. You write what happened on top of the stack and move on, which makes recording nearly free. Reading is worse: to know the current state of something you must check the top of the stack, then the stack below it, and so on, because a newer receipt may supersede an older one. So periodically someone goes through and consolidates the stacks — throwing away superseded receipts and producing one tidy sorted pile. That consolidation is compaction, and it is where all the LSM’s write amplification actually lives. The writes were never cheap; they were deferred, and then done in bulk, in order.

Two consequences fall straight out of the analogy:

  • The stack absorbs a burst effortlessly, and then you owe the consolidation work. Your write throughput is not what the memtable can absorb; it is what compaction can keep up with. Exceed that and the engine stalls you deliberately — which is what RocksDB’s write stalls are.
  • “Is there a receipt for X?” is answerable quickly with an index card per stack saying which names definitely aren’t in it. That card is a Bloom filter. “Show me every receipt between March and April” has no such shortcut — you have to open every stack.

The first of those has an exact number attached, and it is the one that surprises people. On an NVMe SSD writing 3,500 MB/s sequentially, a levelled LSM at 41× write amplification can sustain 85 MB/s of logical writes — about 667,000 rows a second. The memtable in front of it is a memcpy into RAM, so it will cheerfully absorb around 23 times that. The gap between those two numbers is not a tuning problem. It is the deferral, and it comes due:

A B-tree pays the full cost of every write at the moment of the write. An LSM tree defers it and pays in bulk later. Neither is cheaper in total; they differ in when the cost lands and in whether the bytes are sequential.

04

The mechanism: five formulas, and one of them is about your hardware

The B-tree

fanout = page ÷ (key + pointer)  ·  height ≈ log_fanout(leaves) + 1

An 8 KB page with 16-byte keys and 8-byte pointers holds 341 children, so three levels index 39 million rows and four index 13 billion. B-trees are shallow, and that is the entire reason they work — a point lookup on a billion rows is four page reads, of which three are almost always cached.

write amp = page ÷ entry  (×2 with a full-page WAL image)

This is the number from slot 1. It has nothing to do with how many rows you have, and everything to do with how big one row is relative to a page. Long keys hurt twice: they shrink fanout and they leave less room for payload.

Space amplification comes from page fill. Under random insertion a B-tree settles near 70% full, so the data directory is about 1.43× the data.

The LSM tree

L = ⌈log_T(data ÷ memtable)⌉

128 GB over a 64 MB memtable at T = 10 is 4 levels. Ten times the data adds one level. Everything else below is built on this term, and it is small — which is why LSM read costs stay bounded as data grows.

levelled: WA = 1 + T × L  ·  tiered: WA = 1 + L

Levelled keeps one run per level, so merging into it rewrites the level’s contents about T times over the level’s lifetime. Tiered lets T runs accumulate and merges them once, so each entry is written about once per level. That single difference is a ~T× swing in write cost.

point read ≈ 1 + fpr × (runs − 1)  ·  range scan = runs

runs = L for levelled, L×T for tiered, plus the memtable. At a 1% false-positive rate a levelled point lookup costs 1.04 reads. A scan costs 5. Tiered: 1.40 and 41. Bloom filters are the reason the first pair are close and cannot touch the second.

Space: levelled ≈ 1 + 1/T (the bottom level holds nearly everything, so overhead is one level’s worth), tiered ≈ T (up to T overlapping runs of the same keys before a merge).

And the formula that decides between them

sequential advantage = sequential MB/s ÷ min(IOPS × page, sequential MB/s)

HDD at 8 KB: 160 ÷ 0.8 = 195×. SATA and NVMe: 1.0× — the random path saturates the same link the sequential path does, so ordering buys no bandwidth at all. This is the term the folklore was formed under and it is the term that changed.

The one thing that ratio does not capture, and which keeps the LSM ahead on flash: erase blocks. An SSD erases in units far larger than a page, so random 8 KB writes force internal garbage collection that sequential writes largely avoid. That is device endurance rather than throughput, it does not appear in any of the numbers above, and it is a real reason to run RocksDB on write-saturated flash.

When you can ignore all of this. If your working set fits in RAM and your write rate is under a few thousand per second, the engine does not matter and you should choose on features. This lesson starts mattering when the data exceeds memory and the write rate is high enough that amplified bytes approach device bandwidth. Compute it: write rate × row size × amplification versus the drive’s throughput. If you are under 10%, stop reading.

06

The trade-off: three of them, and one you cannot buy your way out of

Read-modify-write versus blind write. A B-tree must read the page before it can update it, so under a random write workload with a cold cache every write costs a read first. An LSM never reads before writing — it appends and lets compaction sort it out. That doubles the B-tree’s disadvantage on writes and is the mechanism behind “LSM absorbs bursts”. It also means an LSM cannot cheaply enforce a uniqueness constraint, because it does not know whether the key already exists without looking, which is why LSM-backed stores tend to offer upserts rather than INSERT that fails on conflict.

Predictable latency versus average throughput. This is the trade that surprises people in production. An LSM’s cost is deferred, and deferred cost arrives in lumps: a large compaction consumes device bandwidth and evicts cache, so p99 write latency has spikes that p50 does not show. A B-tree’s cost is paid immediately and is therefore even — apart from the checkpoint sawtooth. Teams choose LSM for throughput and then discover they bought a tail-latency problem; see percentiles for why the average never showed it.

Space versus writes, with no middle ground you get for free. Levelled and tiered are the same tree at opposite ends of one slider. At T = 10, choosing tiered buys an 8× write improvement for a 9× space cost, and takes range scans from 5 runs to 41. There is no configuration that is good at all three, which is the RUM conjecture restated as an operational fact.

And the one you cannot buy out of: compaction is a background job competing with your foreground. Every byte of write amplification is device bandwidth the LSM spends on itself. If your write rate multiplied by amplification exceeds what compaction can sustain, the tree falls behind, the number of runs grows, reads get slower, and the engine eventually stalls your writes on purpose to stop the situation becoming unrecoverable. That is not a bug, it is the only correct behaviour, and it means an LSM’s real write ceiling is not the memtable’s absorption rate — it is the compactor’s throughput. There is no amount of memory that fixes it, because the work is not memory-bound.

07

In the wild

Postgres full-page writes are slot 1’s second factor, documented as such: full_page_writes defaults to on, and the manual states plainly that turning it off risks unrecoverable corruption on a crash during a page write. The mitigation is not disabling it — it is wal_compression, and increasing checkpoint distance so fewer pages are first-touch. That is a knob whose entire purpose is managing the number in this lesson.

RocksDB’s tuning guide is essentially this lesson written as operational advice: it names write, read and space amplification as the three quantities, gives levelled compaction as the space-efficient default and universal (tiered) as the write-efficient alternative, and documents write stalls as the mechanism protecting against compaction debt.

Facebook’s MyRocks migration is the strongest published evidence for the whole argument. They replaced InnoDB with RocksDB under MySQL for their UDB tier and reported roughly half the storage and a large reduction in bytes written per transaction — a B-tree-to-LSM swap made for space and flash endurance, on the same hardware and the same queries.

Dayan, Athanassoulis & Idreos, “Monkey” (SIGMOD 2017) shows the Bloom-filter story is subtler than slot 4 suggests: allocating filter bits equally across levels is not optimal, because the levels differ in size, and optimal allocation improves lookup cost by up to 80% at no space cost. Worth reading as evidence that even the part of the design that looks settled has a free improvement in it.

LevelDB’s original design note and O’Neil et al.’s 1996 LSM-tree paper are both worth reading for the same reason: they state the disk-seek assumption explicitly, in the open, as the premise of the whole design. The design was correct. The premise changed.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A B-tree's unit of I/O is the page, so updating one row rewrites a whole page — write amplification is page divided by row size
  • Full-page WAL images double that again, which is why B-tree write throughput is spiky across the checkpoint cycle
  • A levelled LSM's write amplification is 1 + T·L, which at real parameters is the same order as a B-tree's
  • The LSM advantage is sequential bytes, not fewer bytes, so it is a property of the device
  • Bloom filters make LSM point lookups cost about one read and do nothing for range scans
  • Levelled costs writes and saves space; tiered costs space and saves writes; the RUM conjecture says you can optimise at most two of read, update and memory

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why does updating a 128-byte row on a B-tree write kilobytes rather than 128 bytes?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the page is the unit of I/O and of torn-write protection, not the row. An 8 KB page holding your row must be written whole, giving 8192/128 = 64×. Then the WAL writes it again — and when the page has not been touched since the last checkpoint, Postgres logs the entire page image rather than the delta so it can survive a torn write, taking the total to 128×. Which of the two you get depends on where you are in the checkpoint cycle, which is why the write throughput of a B-tree is spiky rather than flat.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    "LSM trees have low write amplification." What is wrong with that sentence?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The amplification is not low. Levelled compaction rewrites each entry roughly T times per level, so WA = 1 + T·L; at T = 10 and four levels that is 41×, against a B-tree's 65–128×. Same order of magnitude. What is different is that the LSM's 41× are sequential and the B-tree's are random 8 KB pages at scattered offsets — so the advantage lives in the device's sequential-to-random ratio, not in the data structure.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    On what hardware is the LSM advantage largest, and by how much has it shrunk?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Largest on a spinning disk, where a random write costs a seek plus half a rotation: sequential is ~195× more efficient per byte at 8 KB, and the measured write throughput gap is 610×. On flash the link saturates either way, so the bandwidth advantage is 1.0× and the gap falls to 3.1× — which comes purely from writing three times fewer bytes. LSM trees were designed for the first machine, and most received wisdom about them was formed there.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Why do Bloom filters rescue LSM point lookups and do nothing at all for range scans?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A Bloom filter answers "might this exact key be in this run?" — so a point lookup skips every run whose filter says no, and expected device reads fall to 1 + fpr × (runs − 1), about 1.04 at a 1% false-positive rate. A range scan has no exact key to ask about, so it must open and merge every run that overlaps the range: 5 for levelled, 41 for tiered. That asymmetry is why range-heavy workloads still run on B-trees.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Levelled and tiered compaction differ in one parameter's worth of cost. Name the trade.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Levelled keeps one sorted run per level, so merging rewrites the level's contents ~T times — expensive writes (41×), tight space (1 + 1/T ≈ 1.1×), cheap scans (5 runs). Tiered accumulates T runs before merging them once — cheap writes (5×), terrible space (up to T = 10×, because obsolete versions pile up), terrible scans (41 runs). Same tree, opposite ends of the same slider.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Someone proposes shrinking the B-tree page from 16 KB to 4 KB to cut write amplification. What have they also done?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Cut the fanout by 4×, which makes the tree deeper — fanout is page ÷ (key + pointer), so a 4 KB page with 16-byte keys holds 170 children instead of 682. Amplification improves 4×, point lookups cost an extra level or two of page reads, and each read is now a smaller, less efficient I/O. That is precisely the argument between Postgres's 8 KB default and InnoDB's 16 KB.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    An append-only event log — insert-heavy, queried by time range, on NVMe. Which engine, and what is the one thing that would change your mind?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The workload says LSM (insert-heavy) and the access pattern says B-tree (range scans), so look at the numbers rather than the labels. Sequential insertion is the case where a B-tree packs pages nearly full and appends to the rightmost leaf, so its amplification collapses toward 2× and its range scans stay 5× cheaper — the LSM's write advantage on NVMe is only 3.1×. So: B-tree, unless the insert rate is high enough to matter more than the scans, or the drive's write endurance is the binding constraint. Flash wear is the thing that would change my mind, and it is the argument the throughput numbers do not contain.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: when the unit of modification is larger than the unit of change, cost is set by the container rather than by the payload. Where else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    CPU cache lines — writing one byte dirties 64, which is the whole basis of false sharing. Filesystem block size versus small files. Network MTU versus a one-byte payload, where a TCP ACK costs a full frame. Shipping one item in a pallet-sized box. Git storing whole blobs before packing. The recurring fix is the same in every case: batch changes so the container is amortised, which is exactly what an LSM memtable and a group commit both are.
    Did you have that?
