Tier 6 · Running it in production Distributed job orchestration

Two jobs each needing 6 of 10 slots deadlock at 5/5 under round-robin placement — gang scheduling runs one and queues the other, wasting zero

A resource manager (YARN, the Kubernetes scheduler, Borg) doesn't run jobs — it decides which job gets which slot of a cluster it shares among all of them, and three of its decisions have sharp, closed-form failure modes. A job that needs N workers at once can be handed a partial allocation it holds forever without ever making progress (`simulateGangSchedule`'s 5/5 deadlock below is the same shape as two jobs each wanting 60% of a cluster). A running job can be preempted for a higher-priority one, and whether that's worth it depends on how much work its checkpoint already protects. Batch work that tolerates restart can sit on preemptible capacity — `provisioned-vs-spot.mdx`'s stretch/discount math, reused unchanged. And a job with many parallel tasks finishes when its slowest one does, so `jobCompletionNoSpeculation` prices completion time as a MAX of N, not a mean, and speculative execution as the fix.

Read first — hedged-requeststraffic-shaping-and-hedging

01

A resource manager doesn't run jobs. It decides who gets the cluster.

Every job submitted to a shared cluster — a MapReduce job, a distributed training run, a batch ETL pipeline — competes with every other job for the same finite pool of CPU, memory, and GPU slots. The resource manager (YARN’s ResourceManager, the Kubernetes scheduler, Google’s Borg) is the thing deciding which job gets which slot, right now, and its decisions have failure modes that have nothing to do with any individual job’s own code. Three of them recur across every real system: a job that needs several workers at once can be starved by getting them one at a time; a running job can be forcibly evicted for a more important one; and a job with many parallel pieces finishes only when its slowest piece does.

02

Assume the scheduler placing tasks one at a time is harmless

The instinct: a scheduler that hands out slots as they free up, first-come-first-served, is simple and fair — every job gets a slot the moment one’s available, so nothing sits idle. That instinct is exactly backwards for a job that needs all of its workers simultaneously to do anything useful at all. A distributed training job with 6 workers doing an all-reduce step gets zero training progress from 5 workers — the sixth isn’t “almost there,” the whole allocation is producing nothing until the last worker joins. A scheduler that doesn’t know this hands out workers one at a time anyway, and two such jobs can each end up holding some of what they need while neither ever gets all of it.

Predict A 10-slot cluster gets two distributed-training jobs, each needing exactly 6 workers at once (5 workers = 0 progress). A round-robin scheduler places tasks for whichever job wants more, one slot at a time, with no all-or-nothing guarantee. What happens?
simulateGangSchedule({ totalSlots: 10, jobs: [{tasksNeeded:6},{tasksNeeded:6}] }) allocates one slot at a time, alternating between the two jobs, until all 10 slots are placed. With two equal jobs and an even slot count that lands exactly on 5 and 5 — completedJobs: 0, stuckJobs: 2, wastedSlots: 10. Neither job ever reaches its all-or-nothing threshold of 6, so neither ever runs, and neither ever releases the slots it already holds, because nothing tells a partially-allocated job to give up and free its resources. The entire cluster sits fully occupied and fully useless.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s defaults, round-robin placement splits a 10-slot cluster 5/5 between two 6-worker jobs — 10 slots wasted, zero jobs complete. All-or-nothing placement on the identical cluster runs one job (6 slots) and queues the other cleanly at zero allocation, wasting none. Drag the preemption slider to 500 seconds elapsed with a 300-second checkpoint interval and the model shows a preemption wasting only 200 seconds of progress to save the waiting job 370 — worth it — and flipping “checkpointable” to no turns that same preemption into a net loss. At n=1000 parallel tasks with a small per-task straggler chance, expected completion time with speculative execution runs at roughly a quarter of what it is without.

03

A two-phase-commit shard holding a lock for a transaction that will never complete

The same shape shows up outside scheduling entirely. A cross-shard transaction under two-phase commit asks every shard to “prepare” — take a lock, reserve the write — and only counts as done if every shard prepares and then commits. A shard that has prepared but is waiting on the rest of the group is functionally identical to a training job’s fifth-of-six worker: it is holding a real resource (a lock), doing no useful work, and it cannot safely let go until it learns what happened to everyone else. If the coordinator crashes before telling that shard to abort, the shard sits there — prepared, blocked, and useless — until a recovery protocol restores the missing information. Gang scheduling is the same fix applied one layer up: don’t let a participant commit to holding a resource until you know the whole group can proceed.

04

Three mechanisms, three different levers

GANG SCHEDULING      All-or-nothing placement for jobs that need every worker at once. The lever
(placement policy)   is WHEN a job's tasks get placed — atomically, or as slots trickle in — and
                      the cost of getting it wrong is resources held by jobs that can never finish,
                      not resources merely used inefficiently.

PREEMPTION            Forcibly stopping a running job to free its resources for a higher-priority
(evict and requeue)    one. The lever is WHETHER to preempt right now, and the trade is the work
                       destroyed (bounded by the checkpoint interval, or total if there is none)
                       against how much sooner the waiting job gets served.

SPOT / PREEMPTIBLE     Routing a job onto capacity that can be reclaimed with short notice, in
CAPACITY               exchange for a discount. This is `provisioned-vs-spot.mdx`'s stretch model,
(cost vs interruption) reused unchanged — the resource manager's job is only deciding WHICH queue
                       (preemptible or guaranteed) a batch job belongs in.

STRAGGLERS             A job with many parallel tasks finishes when its slowest task does. The
(speculative exec)     lever is launching a duplicate of any task that looks slow and racing it
                       against the original, at the cost of the cluster capacity the duplicate
                       consumes.

Gang scheduling and preemption both answer “who gets a slot, right now” — one refuses a bad allocation before it happens, the other undoes a good one that’s since been outranked. Spot capacity and stragglers are about a job’s own risk profile: whether it can tolerate losing a machine, and whether one slow task can dominate everything else it’s running alongside.

06

Where each mechanism runs out of slack

Gang scheduling doesn’t fix the cluster running out of room — it fixes what happens when it does. All-or-nothing placement never grows the cluster; a job that needs 6 slots and finds only 4 free still waits. What it prevents is that wait turning into a deadlock where the waiting job is itself holding resources that make the wait permanent. A cluster genuinely too small for its workload is still under-provisioned either way — gang scheduling just makes that failure visible as a queue instead of invisible as a stuck, fully-occupied cluster.

Preemption’s verdict depends entirely on the checkpoint, not on the priority gap. The 500- second, 400-remaining example above flips from worthIt: true to worthIt: false by changing nothing but checkpointable — the priority difference between the two jobs never enters the calculation once the numbers are this close. A scheduler that preempts purely on priority, blind to how much progress it is about to destroy, is optimizing half the trade-off.

The spot-routing decision is a job property, not a cluster property. The same cluster, the same reclaim rate, produces a 1.95× stretch for one job and a 6.63× stretch for another, purely because one job checkpoints and the other doesn’t — which is exactly provisioned-vs-spot.mdx’s finding, reused here rather than re-derived: whether preemptible capacity is a good deal depends on what the job itself can tolerate losing, not on how good the discount looks.

Stragglers get more likely, not less, as a job gets more parallel — and speculative execution’s payoff grows to match. stragglerProbabilityAtLeastOne climbs from 19% at n=2 to 63.2% at n=1,000 for the same per-task rate, because avoiding a straggler at n tasks means every one of the n independent coin flips has to come up clean. Speculative execution’s saving grows right alongside it — at n=2 it roughly halves expected completion time, and at n=1,000 it cuts it by a factor of about four — because the more tasks a job has, the more of them a fixed per-task straggler rate turns into an actual straggler needing a duplicate.

07

In the wild

Google’s Borg is the reference implementation for two of these three mechanisms at once. Verma et al. describe Borg as scheduling both latency-sensitive production services and lower-priority batch jobs onto the same shared cluster, using priority bands so that a running task from a production job can evict a task from a non-production job when the cluster is tight — protecting the higher-priority workload’s resource guarantee while still letting batch work fill the gaps the rest of the time. That is the exact preemption trade this lesson prices, running at the scale of Google’s actual production fleet.

The MapReduce paper is where speculative execution for stragglers was named and justified. Dean and Ghemawat identify a straggler — a machine that unusually slowly completes one of the last few map or reduce tasks — as the most common cause of a MapReduce job running slower than expected, and describe the system’s fix: near the end of a job, the master launches backup (“speculative”) executions of the tasks still in progress, and whichever copy of a task finishes first — the original or the backup — is the one whose result counts. That is precisely jobCompletionWithSpeculation’s race-and-take-first-finisher mechanic, described from inside the system that made it standard practice.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A job that needs N workers simultaneously to make ANY progress (a distributed training job's all-reduce step) gets zero benefit from a PARTIAL allocation — 5 of 6 needed workers produces exactly as much useful work as 0 of 6, but without an all-or-nothing scheduling guarantee a scheduler can still hand it that partial allocation, which it then holds while waiting for the rest
  • `simulateGangSchedule`'s round-robin placement of two jobs each needing 6 of 10 slots splits the cluster 5/5 — neither job ever reaches 6, neither ever releases what it holds, and the entire cluster (`wastedSlots: 10`) produces zero useful work; `simulateAllOrNothingSchedule` on the identical cluster runs one job to completion and queues the other at zero allocation, wasting nothing
  • Preemption is a genuine trade: `preemptionTradeoff` prices the work destroyed (`wastedWorkS` — only the time since the last checkpoint boundary if the job checkpoints, or its ENTIRE elapsed runtime if it cannot) against the time the preemption saves the higher-priority job (`timeSavedForHighPriorityS` — the remaining runtime minus the restart overhead), and the same numbers flip from worth-it to not depending only on whether `checkpointable` is true
  • Batch jobs that tolerate restart are exactly the workload `provisioned-vs-spot.mdx`'s stretch/discount model already prices for spot capacity — this lesson reuses `stretch` and `requiredDiscount` unchanged rather than re-deriving them, applied here as the resource manager's routing signal for which queue (preemptible vs guaranteed capacity) a batch job belongs in
  • A job with n independent parallel tasks completes when its SLOWEST task does — `jobCompletionNoSpeculation` prices expected completion time as the max of n task times under a two-point straggler mixture, and because `stragglerProbabilityAtLeastOne` (`1 - (1-p)^n`) grows with n, a job gets MORE likely to hit at least one straggler exactly as it gets more parallel, not less
  • Speculative execution — launch a duplicate of any task still running past a threshold, take whichever finishes first — is the standard mitigation; `jobCompletionWithSpeculation` prices it as a race between the original straggler and a late-started duplicate, and at n=1000 with a 0.1% straggler rate and a 10x slowdown it cuts expected completion time from about 669ms to about 164ms

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A cluster has 10 slots. Two distributed-training jobs each need exactly 6 workers simultaneously to run their all-reduce step — 5 workers produces no training progress at all. A scheduler with no gang-scheduling guarantee places tasks for both jobs as slots become available, round-robin. What happens?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    `simulateGangSchedule({ totalSlots: 10, jobs: [{tasksNeeded:6},{tasksNeeded:6}] })` splits the 10 slots 5 and 5 — each job gets exactly half the cluster and neither ever reaches the 6 it needs. Because neither job can make progress, neither ever finishes and neither releases the slots it's holding: `completedJobs: 0`, `stuckJobs: 2`, `wastedSlots: 10` — the ENTIRE cluster is consumed producing zero useful work, and it stays that way until something outside the scheduler (an operator, a timeout, an OOM kill) intervenes.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Same cluster, same two jobs, but now the scheduler places each job's 6 workers atomically — it checks whether 6 free slots exist before placing any of that job's tasks at all. Does that fix it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Yes — `simulateAllOrNothingSchedule` on the identical input runs the first job (6 of 10 slots, all at once) and, finding only 4 slots left for the second job's 6, leaves it queued at zero allocation rather than partially placed: `completedJobs: 1`, `queuedJobs: 1`, `wastedSlots: 0`. The second job isn't running, but it isn't holding anything hostage either — 4 slots sit idle rather than 10 slots being actively wasted, and as soon as the first job finishes and frees its 6, the second can start.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A low-priority job has been running 500 seconds when a higher-priority job arrives needing its resource. The low-priority job checkpoints every 300 seconds, would otherwise finish in 400 more seconds, and restarting the high-priority job on the freed resource costs 30 seconds. Is preempting worth it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Yes — `preemptionTradeoff` computes `wastedWorkS` as 500 mod 300 = 200 seconds (only the progress since the last checkpoint boundary is lost) against `timeSavedForHighPriorityS` of 400 − 30 = 370 seconds (how much sooner the high-priority job gets the resource compared to waiting for the low-priority job to finish on its own). 370 > 200, a net benefit of 170 seconds, so `worthIt: true`. Change nothing except `checkpointable: false` and `wastedWorkS` becomes the full 500 seconds — now 370 < 500, `worthIt: false`: the same preemption, at the same moment, flips from a good decision to a bad one purely on whether the job could checkpoint at all.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    `traffic-shaping-and-hedging.mdx` already covers hedging and tied requests — race a duplicate of a slow request, take whichever answer comes back first. Isn't `jobCompletionWithSpeculation` just that same idea with a different name?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The trigger and the shape of the fix are the same — `1 − p` of attempts still outstanding past a threshold get a duplicate, and the job takes whichever finishes first — but the two lessons price different units at different layers. Hedging races individual REQUESTS against a percentile of a latency DISTRIBUTION measured over many independent calls, and its saving (tying, cancelling the loser within one message delay) is a request-level mechanic that doesn't apply to a batch task at all. Speculative execution here races TASKS inside a single JOB against each other, the "slow" outcome is a discrete straggler state (contention, skew, a bad node) rather than a smooth percentile, and what's being minimized is the job's own completion time — the max across all its tasks — not a shared per-request SLA. Spark's `spark.speculation` (cited in `traffic-shaping-and-hedging.mdx`) is the industrial version of exactly this mechanism, which is why that lesson names it directly rather than re-deriving it — this lesson is where the underlying max-of-n-tasks problem it solves actually gets priced.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: when N independent things must ALL succeed simultaneously for any of them to matter, partial success is worth exactly as much as total failure — so a system that hands out "partial credit" toward that requirement without a way to detect and undo it can leave every participant holding a resource that produces nothing, forever. Name an instance outside job scheduling.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A cross-shard distributed transaction under two-phase commit, without a genuine atomic abort. A coordinator asks every shard to "prepare" a write; a shard that prepares has taken a lock and reserved the resource, but the transaction only counts as done if EVERY shard prepares and then commits — one shard voting no, or timing out, makes every other shard's prepared-but-uncommitted state worth precisely nothing. If the coordinator crashes after some shards have prepared but before it can tell any of them to abort, those shards are the training job's stuck 5-of-6 workers exactly: each is holding a lock (a resource), doing no useful work, unable to proceed on its own and unable to safely release what it's holding until something restores the missing "the rest of the group" information — which is precisely why 2PC needs a recovery protocol (or 3PC, or a consensus-backed coordinator) rather than leaving prepared-but-orphaned shards to sit there indefinitely.
    Did you have that?
