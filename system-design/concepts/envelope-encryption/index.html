Tier 7 · Security, privacy, compliance Envelope encryption explained well nowhere else

One number decides your KMS bill, your blast radius and whether you can delete a user at all

A key management service will not encrypt your object — AWS KMS refuses anything over 4 KB — so you encrypt locally with a data key and let the KMS wrap only the key. That leaves exactly one decision: how many objects share a data key. It sets the request bill, what one leaked key exposes, and the smallest thing you can cryptographically delete, and the first two are a conserved product you can only slide along.

Read first — back-of-envelopecache-eviction

01

The encryption worked in staging, and in production the KMS started throttling

The requirement was one line long: encrypt every object at rest, with a key the platform team controls. The implementation was the obvious one — call the KMS to encrypt each object on the way in — and it failed immediately, because AWS KMS Encrypt accepts at most 4,096 bytes of plaintext and the objects averaged a megabyte.

So they did what the documentation says: generate a data key, encrypt the object locally with it, send only the 32-byte key to the KMS to be wrapped, and store the wrapped key — about 200 bytes — next to the ciphertext. One key per object, which any security reviewer would prefer.

At 10,000 objects a second that is 10,000 KMS requests a second. Two things then happened, and only one of them was on anybody’s list.

The bill: 25.9 billion requests a month at $0.03 per 10,000 is about $78,000 a month, for key management alone, on a store whose objects cost a fraction of that to hold.

And the part that actually paged: the shared per-account quota for symmetric KMS operations is on the order of ten thousand requests a second, region-dependent, and it is shared with everything else in the account. Uploads began failing with throttling errors from a dependency nobody had drawn on the architecture diagram, because it was labelled “encryption”, and encryption was assumed to be a local concern.

The fix is to let one data key cover more than one object. The question is how many — and that single number turns out to decide two other things nobody connects to it.

02

Slide the knob and watch the point move along a line it cannot leave

Predict You raise objects-per-data-key from 1 to 1,000. KMS requests fall 1,000×. What happens to the product of KMS request rate and the data one leaked key exposes?
Requests are writeRate/k and exposure is k × objectBytes. The k cancels: the product is writeRate × objectBytes, fixed. So the two are not independent knobs — there is one knob and a frontier, and the only way to move the frontier is to write less data.
Show it without answering

Three things to take from it.

You are not tuning two numbers. The frontier is straight because the product is conserved, and the reason to draw it as one line is that a reader who sees two curves starts looking for a setting that improves both. There isn’t one.

The failure at the left end is not a cost failure. One key per object is the most defensible design on paper and the one that gets throttled. Cost you can argue for; a quota you cannot.

Only the axis labels move. Halve the object size and the whole line shifts left: same requests, half the exposure. That is a real improvement, and it comes from the data model, not from key management — which is the usual shape of a genuine win in this tier.

03

The intuition: a locker key in a sealed envelope

A storage depot keeps its master key in a vault downstairs. The vault is well guarded and slow: you have to queue, and the clerk will only accept something small through the slot.

You cannot post a crate down there to be locked. So you lock the crate with an ordinary padlock, put that padlock’s key in an envelope, and post the envelope down the slot to be sealed with the master key. The sealed envelope is taped to the crate. Anyone with the master key can open the envelope, get the padlock key, and open the crate — nobody else can.

Now the only decision left: how many crates share a padlock?

  • One padlock per crate. Perfect isolation — a stolen padlock key opens one crate. But you queue at the vault once per crate, and the queue has a fixed length per day.
  • One padlock for the whole warehouse. One trip to the vault, ever. And a stolen padlock key opens everything.

Two consequences of one choice, and the trips-to-the-vault you save are exactly the crates you added to the loss. But now the part that makes the whole scheme worth its complexity.

The master key is compromised. You must re-key.

You do not re-lock the crates. Every padlock is still fine; only the envelopes were sealed with the compromised master. So you re-seal envelopes — a few hundred bytes each — and every crate in the depot is untouched. A hundred terabytes of crates, twenty gigabytes of envelopes.

And one more, which is the part nobody sees coming: burn an envelope and lose the padlock key, and that crate can never be opened again — including the copy of it in the off-site archive you have no way to edit. That is how you delete data you cannot reach. It also means the crates that shared a padlock die together.

The data key is a level of indirection, and it is simultaneously three units: the unit of KMS work, the unit of compromise, and the unit of deletion. Choosing it for the first one silently chooses the other two.

04

The mechanism: four formulas, one of them a conservation law

N objects of objectBytes each, written at writeRate per second, k objects per data key.

KMS requests/s = writeRate / k  ·  blast radius = k × objectBytes

One GenerateDataKey per new key on the write path. Reads are the other side and are cacheable — see below.

requests/s × blast radius = writeRate × objectBytes  ·  independent of k

The k cancels. Asserted as an exact equality for every k in keys.test.mjs, because this is the claim the whole lesson rests on: there is one knob, and it moves you along a line.

KMS bill = requests/s × 2,592,000 × $0.03 / 10,000 = $7.78 per sustained request/s per month

Worth memorising as one number. It makes “one KMS call per request” instantly priceable at any scale: 1,000 rps of KMS traffic is about $7,800 a month.

retire the KEK: re-wrap ⌈N/k⌉ × ~200 bytes, not N × objectBytes

The ratio is k × objectBytes / 200. The data keys change wrapper; the data does not change at all, because the data keys themselves are unchanged.

Reads are the asymmetric side. A read needs the data key unwrapped, which is a Decrypt call — unless you have it cached. A DEK cache is an ordinary cache with an ordinary set of bounds (the AWS Encryption SDK’s caching layer takes a maximum age, a maximum number of messages and a maximum number of bytes per cached key, which is the same shape as any eviction policy with a TTL). Two things follow:

  • Read-side KMS load is readRate × (1 − hit ratio), so it is a caching problem rather than a key-management one.
  • Raising k helps reads twice: fewer distinct keys exist, so a fixed-size cache covers a larger fraction of them. At 100M objects and a 10,000-entry cache, one key per object gives a 0.01% hit ratio; one key per 100,000 objects gives 100%.

And one distinction that decides whether the fourth law is ever invoked at all:

What it meansRe-wrapping needed
RotationNew backing key for new encryptions; old versions retained to decrypt old ciphertextNone
RetirementThe old key material must stop being able to decrypt anything⌈N/k⌉ wrapped keys

Managed key rotation is the first row, which is why it can be automatic and free. A compliance requirement, a suspected compromise, or leaving a provider is the second row. They are usually discussed with the same word.

Read the first and third rows together. Going from a key per object to a key per thousand objects removes $78,000 a month and the quota problem, and costs you a blast radius that went from one megabyte to one gigabyte. For most systems that is an obviously good trade, and it is obviously good because it was computed — the same trade is indefensible if the thousand objects are a thousand different customers’ records.

The re-wrap column is worth a second look: even the worst case in the table, 20 GB, is a background job that finishes in an afternoon. The alternative it replaces is rewriting 100 TB, which for most teams is not a job at all but a project.

There is deliberately no proportional figure of that split. A 1,000-object group is a gigabyte of ciphertext and 200 bytes of wrapped key — 5,000,000 to 1 — and a bar chart of it is one full-width band with an invisible sliver labelled 0%. The ratio is the content, so it belongs in a column of numbers.

What to set.

  • Never one key per object at scale, unless writes are slow enough that the request rate is a rounding error. Compute writeRate × $7.78 first; it is one multiplication and it ends most of these discussions.
  • Set k from the deletion requirement first, then check the frontier. Deletion granularity is a hard constraint and blast radius is a judgement, so let the hard one bind. If you owe per-user erasure, a user’s data cannot share a key with another user’s — at any k.
  • Use a hierarchy when the two constraints disagree. A per-tenant KEK with many DEKs beneath it gives one-delete shredding and a bounded blast radius. The shred unit and the blast radius do not have to be the same level.
  • Cache data keys, with all three bounds set — age, messages, bytes. An unbounded DEK cache is a key that lives forever in memory, which quietly undoes the rotation story.
  • Find out whether your requirement says rotate or retire. One is free and automatic; the other is the 20 GB job. They are written with the same word more often than not.
06

The trade-off: one knob, three units, and only one of them is negotiable

Raising k buys request rate and sells exposure, at a fixed exchange rate. This is the frontier, and it is the honest part of the trade-off: both quantities are continuous, both are yours to choose, and the product is fixed by how much data you write. If the frontier is in the wrong place, the answer is a smaller object or fewer writes — not a cleverer key scheme.

Raising k also raises the floor on deletion, and that one is not a trade. Blast radius is a risk you can accept; deletion granularity is a capability you either have or do not, and you discover which after the ciphertext exists. Cryptographic erasure works precisely because it does not require reaching the data — which is the same reason it cannot be made finer retroactively. There is no migration that splits one key’s objects onto two keys without re-encrypting them, at which point you are doing the 100 TB job the whole design existed to avoid.

A key hierarchy is the way out, and it costs an extra unwrap on the read path. Per-tenant KEK, per-group DEK: shredding a tenant is one key delete, and no DEK exposes more than k objects. What you pay is one more level of indirection to resolve on every cold read, and a much more interesting key-management topology to operate — more keys to inventory, more places a key can be orphaned, and a real chance of an object whose KEK was deleted while its DEK survived.

And the thing that is not a trade-off at all: the indirection is nearly free and enormously valuable. Retiring a key-encrypting key touches the wrapped keys — kilobytes to gigabytes — and never reads the data. Almost every property people attribute to “using a KMS” is actually this one structural fact, and it holds at every k.

What you gave up by adopting envelope encryption in the first place is worth naming, since this slot exists for it. The KMS is now on your write path: a dependency with a request quota, a regional failure domain, and an IAM policy that can lock you out of your own data. A wrapped key stored apart from its ciphertext is an object nobody can read, ever, and there is no recovery procedure — which is the same property that makes crypto-shredding work, arriving uninvited.

07

In the wild

S3 Bucket Keys exist because of exactly this arithmetic. SSE-KMS originally made a KMS call per object operation, and AWS’s documented answer was a bucket-level key from which S3 derives per-object keys — reducing KMS request traffic by up to 99% and cutting the request bill accordingly. It is the same move as raising k, done by the storage layer so applications do not have to, and its trade is the same: the blast radius of the derived-key scheme is the bucket.

The AWS Encryption SDK ships a caching layer whose configuration is this lesson’s knob. You set a maximum age, a maximum number of messages and a maximum number of bytes per cached data key. Those three limits are k, expressed in the three units a real system cares about, and the SDK’s documentation is explicit that the trade being made is key reuse against exposure.

Google Cloud KMS and the envelope pattern are documented the same way, with the same payload constraint and the same recommendation to encrypt locally and wrap the key. The convergence is a good sign that the constraint is structural rather than a vendor decision.

Crypto-shredding is the standard answer to erasure at scale, and it is standardised. NIST SP 800-88’s Cryptographic Erase names it: destroying the key renders the ciphertext unrecoverable, including on media you cannot overwrite. This is how “delete my data” is implemented against immutable backups and append-only stores, and it is why per-tenant key design shows up in SaaS compliance architecture long before anyone is worried about cryptanalysis.

Managed rotation not re-encrypting anything is the detail that surprises people. AWS KMS automatic rotation creates new key material and retains previous versions so old ciphertext still decrypts — no re-wrap, no re-encrypt, no application change. It is a genuinely free hygiene measure, and it is also why “we rotate annually” answers a much smaller question than the one a compromise asks.

Model and numbers — every figure computed by src/sim/keys.mjs and asserted in src/sim/keys.test.mjs (10 tests): the conserved product requests/s × k = writeRate as an exact equality for every k, $7.776 per sustained request per second, the 20 GB / 5,000× re-wrap arithmetic, and the DEK cache hit ratio.

AWS KMS Developer Guide — the 4,096-byte Encrypt plaintext limit, GenerateDataKey, request quotas for cryptographic operations (shared per account, region-dependent), and automatic key rotation retaining previous key versions.

AWS Key Management Service pricing — $0.03 per 10,000 requests for symmetric operations, and a monthly charge per key. The $7.78-per-sustained-rps figure in this lesson is derived from it.

Amazon S3 User Guide, “Reducing the cost of SSE-KMS with Amazon S3 Bucket Keys” — the documented reduction in KMS request traffic, and the bucket-level key it is achieved with.

AWS Encryption SDK Developer Guide, “Data key caching” — the caching cryptographic materials manager and its three limits: maximum age, maximum messages encrypted, maximum bytes encrypted, with the reuse-versus-exposure trade stated outright.

Google Cloud KMS documentation, “Envelope encryption” — the same pattern and the same payload reasoning from a second provider.

NIST SP 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization — Cryptographic Erase as a recognised sanitisation technique, which is what makes crypto-shredding a compliance answer rather than a trick.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A KMS cannot encrypt your data — AWS KMS caps Encrypt at 4 KB — so the data key exists because of a payload limit, not because of key hygiene
  • KMS request rate is writeRate/k and blast radius is k objects, so their product is writeRate × objectBytes and no configuration escapes that line
  • One sustained KMS request per second costs about $7.78 a month, so a key per object at 10k writes/s is roughly $78k a month before it is also over quota
  • Retiring the key-encrypting key re-wraps the data keys and never touches the data: 100 TB behind one-DEK-per-object is 20 GB of re-wrapping, 5,000× less
  • Routine rotation on a managed KMS re-wraps nothing, because old key versions are kept to decrypt old ciphertext; only retiring a key forces the re-wrap
  • Deleting a data key destroys its objects everywhere including backups you cannot rewrite, so the data key is also the unit of deletion and cannot be finer than k
  • Reads are the cacheable side: a DEK cache turns Decrypt calls into local work, and raising k shrinks the working set the cache has to cover

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why does a data key exist at all? Answer in terms of a limit, not of good practice.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the KMS will not take the payload. AWS KMS Encrypt accepts at most 4,096 bytes of plaintext, so a 1 MB object physically cannot be encrypted by the service — and even if it could, you would be shipping every byte over the network twice to a rate-limited endpoint. So you encrypt locally with a 32-byte data key and send only that key to be wrapped. Key hygiene is a consequence of the design, not its reason.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Derive the relationship between KMS request rate and blast radius, and say what it implies about tuning them.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Requests are one GenerateDataKey per new key, writeRate/k. Blast radius is k objects, or k × objectBytes of data. Multiply: writeRate × objectBytes — the k cancels, so the product is constant. They are not two knobs. There is one knob and a line, and the only way to move the line is to write less data. Asserted as an exact equality in keys.test.mjs.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A team encrypts each of 100 million 1 MB objects with its own data key, at 10,000 writes a second. What are the two separate things wrong with that, in numbers?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Cost: 10,000 KMS requests a second is 25.9 billion requests a month, about $78,000 at $0.03 per 10,000. Availability: that is at or above the shared per-account request quota for symmetric operations in most regions, so it is not an expensive design, it is a throttled one. The blast radius is excellent — one object per key — which is exactly why the mistake is easy to defend in review.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    The key-encrypting key must be retired after a suspected compromise. How much work is that for 100 TB of objects, and why is it not 100 TB?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    You re-wrap the data keys under a new KEK; the ciphertext of the objects never changes, because the data keys themselves have not changed. At one key per object that is 100M wrapped keys of about 200 bytes: 20 GB, or 5,000× less than re-encrypting. At k = 1,000 it is 20 MB. This is the entire reason the indirection is worth its complexity, and it is the number nobody publishes.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A managed KMS rotates your key automatically every year. How much re-wrapping does that cause, and when does the answer change?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    None. Rotation on AWS KMS creates a new backing key for new encryptions and keeps every old version to decrypt old ciphertext, so existing wrapped data keys stay valid and untouched. The answer changes when you must retire the old key material rather than rotate past it — a compromise, a provider or region exit, a contractual requirement to prove old material is gone. That is when the 20 GB above becomes real work, and it is worth knowing which of the two words a compliance requirement actually used.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Why is the data key also the unit of deletion, and what does that do to a right-to-erasure request?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because destroying the key destroys every object it wrapped, wherever the ciphertext is — including in snapshots, replicas and backups you cannot rewrite. That is crypto-shredding, and it is how erasure is actually implemented at scale, since finding and overwriting every copy is not tractable. The consequence is a hard constraint: you cannot shred finer than k. If one data key covers a thousand users' rows, deleting one user is impossible, and you learn this after the data is written.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A multi-tenant SaaS wants per-tenant crypto-shredding and picks one data key per tenant. Their largest tenant writes 40,000 objects a second and their smallest writes four a day. What breaks, and what is the fix that keeps the shredding property?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Nothing breaks on the KMS side — one key per tenant is one GenerateDataKey per tenant, not per object, so request load is trivial. What breaks is blast radius: the big tenant's single key now exposes its entire dataset, and the key is held in memory on every host serving that tenant. The fix keeps the tenant as the outer boundary and adds a layer inside it: a per-tenant KEK, with many DEKs beneath it. Shredding a tenant means destroying its KEK — still one delete — while no single DEK exposes more than k objects. The general move is that the shred unit and the blast radius do not have to be the same level of the hierarchy, and treating them as one is what forces the bad choice.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a level of indirection converts an expensive bulk operation into a cheap operation on the pointers, and the granularity of the pointers then silently becomes the granularity of everything else. Name an instance outside cryptography.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A filesystem's inodes: deleting a file unlinks a pointer rather than scrubbing blocks, and the block size then dictates minimum allocation and fragmentation. Also: a database's indirection through a page table means moving a row updates one pointer, but page granularity becomes lock granularity; a CDN's cache keys make invalidation cheap, and the key granularity becomes the smallest thing you can purge; library book barcodes make re-cataloguing cheap and the shelf becomes the smallest thing you can relocate; shipping containers make transhipment cheap and the container becomes the smallest thing you can inspect. The tell is always that a choice made for efficiency quietly fixed the resolution of policy.
    Did you have that?
