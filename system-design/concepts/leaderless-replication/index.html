Tier 3 · Storage and state Leaderless replication

A key nobody reads never converges under read repair, and a Merkle tree finds a divergent key 250× cheaper than comparing every one

Give up the leader entirely: any of N replicas can take a write or serve a read, through a coordinator that is a stateless proxy, not a decision-maker. `quorums.mdx` already derives and prices the durability guarantee this buys — R + W > N — so this lesson does not repeat that arithmetic. It builds the rest of the picture: the two mechanisms, read repair and anti-entropy, that keep replicas converging with no leader to force order between them, and what each one costs when it works and when it doesn't.

Read first — quorums

01

A customer's address was updated once, in 2024, and never agreed with itself again

A customer changes their shipping address during checkout. The write lands on two of three replicas — the third is mid-restart — and the coordinator reports success, because two out of three is the write quorum. The customer never updates that address again, and nobody ever reads it from the one replica that missed the write, because ordinary traffic reads from whichever two replicas answer fastest, and it’s almost always the same two.

Eighteen months later, an audit script reads from all three replicas directly and finds two different addresses for the same customer. Nothing crashed. No write ever failed. The stale replica has been sitting there the entire time, and nothing in the system was ever going to notice on its own.

02

Assume 'eventually consistent' means it happens on its own, eventually

The belief this has to break
A leaderless store is eventually consistent, so given enough time every replica catches up on its own.

“Eventually consistent” describes an outcome, not a mechanism, and the outcome does not happen by itself. Something has to notice a replica is stale and something has to copy the fresh value to it. In a leaderless store — no leader, no replication log flowing in one direction — that “something” is exactly two mechanisms, and they trigger on different conditions:

Read repair fires only when a client reads the key and the read happens to touch both a stale and a fresh replica. If nobody reads the key, read repair never runs for it, ever.

Anti-entropy fires on its own background schedule, independent of reads, and is the only one of the two that eventually reaches a key nobody has looked at. “Eventually consistent” is really a claim about anti-entropy’s schedule, not a property of the system doing nothing.

Neither mechanism is optional. A store with only read repair leaves cold keys stale forever. A store with only anti-entropy pays its full bandwidth and CPU cost even for hot keys that would have self-corrected on the very next read.

Predict N=5 replicas, R=2, one stale. Read repair needs about 2.5 reads on average to touch and fix it. A key read once a minute converges in about 2.5 minutes. A DIFFERENT key, same setup, is read once a week instead. How long does IT take to converge?
The number of READS needed to converge doesn't change with how often the key is read — it's still ~2.5 on average, because that number comes from the quorum math (R=2 of N=5, one stale replica), not from the clock. What changes is how long those 2.5 reads take to arrive: at one read per minute, 2.5 reads take about 2.5 minutes; at one read per week, the same 2.5 reads take about 2.5 weeks. Read repair has no clock of its own — it only has whatever clock the key's own read traffic gives it.
Show it without answering

Four things the rig is built to show.

The ring has no special node. Any of the five circles could have taken the write first; the diagram deliberately draws them identically, because that absence of a distinguished node is the entire structural difference from leader-follower-replication.mdx.

“Simulate a read” is read repair’s whole mechanism, not a simplification of it. Pick R replicas at random; if the pick includes a stale one, fix it. That is genuinely all read repair does — there is no additional step being skipped for the demo.

The expected-reads readout is the model, live. It calls readDetectionProbability(n, r, staleReplicas) on every draw, which is 1 − staleReadProbability(n, r, staleReplicas) from quorum.mjs — reused, not reimplemented — because “does this read’s quorum touch the stale replica” is the same combinatorial question as “does this read’s quorum touch the fresh replica,” just asked about the other side of the same partition of N nodes.

The Merkle bar is nearly flat because tree depth grows logarithmically. Drag dataset size from a thousand keys to a billion — the naive bar grows a millionfold. The Merkle bar barely moves, because depth only grows from about 10 to about 30.

03

The intuition: a household shopping list nobody owns

Three housemates keep the household shopping list as a photo on their own phone. Nobody’s phone is the “real” one — any of them can update it after a grocery run, and they text the new photo to the group chat. Following the group’s own rule, an update only counts once at least two of the three have acknowledged it (a 2-of-3 write quorum), and checking the list only counts once you’ve compared with at least one other person’s copy (a 2-of-3 read quorum). That rule is exactly quorums.mdx’s R + W > N, and this lesson is not about re-deriving it — it’s about what happens next.

One housemate’s phone was off when an update went out. Her copy is now stale, and nothing about the group chat rule fixes that by itself — texting only reaches phones that are on.

She opens her phone a week later and, out of habit, compares her list with a housemate’s before heading to the store. They notice the mismatch immediately and she updates her copy on the spot. That’s read repair: it fixed her copy the moment — and only because — she happened to check.

If she’d never opened the list again, her copy would still be wrong today. Nobody’s job is to notice a phone that never gets used. That’s the gap read repair cannot close by construction.

The household also does a monthly “compare all three photos side by side” night, whether or not anyone’s list has actually changed. That’s anti-entropy: slower, and it runs whether or not it’s needed, but it’s the only one of the three habits that would have caught her stale phone even if she’d never opened it again.

Read repair fixes what gets used. Anti-entropy fixes what doesn’t. A system that only has the first quietly accumulates cold, wrong data forever — and nothing about the system looks broken while it does.

04

The mechanism: one guarantee reused, two mechanisms new

durability guarantee = quorums.mjs’s own R + W > N, unchanged

overlaps(n, r, w), guaranteedOverlap(n, r, w), staleReadProbability(n, r, w) — imported directly from quorum.mjs, not rederived. At N=3, W=2, R=2 the rule holds and the guaranteed overlap is 1 node; at N=3, W=1, R=1 it’s broken on purpose and 66.7% of reads can miss the write, exactly as quorums.mjs already computed. The one leaderless-specific failure mode is a sloppy quorum: if the designated W replicas are unreachable, a Dynamo-style coordinator writes to W reachable nodes elsewhere instead, which keeps the write available but means the reader’s designated read set never touches those nodes at all — the overlap guarantee doesn’t just weaken, it stops applying. (Recovering from that is hinted handoff, a mechanism named here and not built out further — it’s not on this lesson’s roster.)

P(a read detects a stale replica) = 1 − staleReadProbability(N, R, staleReplicas)

readDetectionProbability(n, r, staleReplicas) in leaderless-replication.mjs. Reuses staleReadProbability from quorum.mjs by treating the staleReplicas count as if it were the “fresh” count w in that formula — the two questions (“does this read miss all the fresh nodes” and “does this read touch at least one stale node”) are complements of the same combinatorial event. Verified against brute-force enumeration of every read quorum for N up to 7 in leaderless-replication.test.mjs.

E[reads to first repair] = 1 / P(detect)  ·  wall-clock = that ÷ read frequency

readRepairConvergenceTime(readFrequencyPerSecond, n, r, staleReplicas). A geometric distribution’s mean is the reciprocal of its success probability — the number of reads needed doesn’t depend on the clock at all, only on N/R/staleReplicas. The clock only converts that fixed read count into wall-clock time, which is exactly why a hot key and a cold key need the same number of reads but can differ by a factor of thousands in how long that takes to happen. At readFrequencyPerSecond = 0 the function returns Infinity — not a large number, the actual mathematical consequence of dividing by zero frequency, and the precise statement of “an unread key never converges under read repair alone.”

Merkle cost = differingKeys × depth × branchingFactor  ·  naive cost = totalKeys

merkleTreeComparisonCost and naiveComparisonCost. Depth is ⌈log_b(totalKeys)⌉ — merkleTreeDepth — so for a fixed number of differing keys, Merkle cost grows by one level (a constant additive step) every time the dataset doubles, while naive cost doubles outright. The formula is a deliberate upper bound: it charges a full comparison at every level for every differing leaf without deduplicating ancestors two nearby differing leaves might share, and leaderless-replication.test.mjs checks that an actual tree walk that DOES deduplicate shared ancestors never costs more than this formula says.

Six orders of magnitude of data cost the Merkle tree three extra levels (10 → 30) and 3× more comparisons (200 → 600). The naive comparison, over the same range, costs a millionfold more. This is the entire, computable argument for using a Merkle tree specifically — not “it’s more efficient” as an assertion, but a depth that grows additively against a scan that grows multiplicatively.

At N=1,000,000 with 100 keys actually differing (0.01% of the dataset): Merkle costs 4,000 comparisons against 1,000,000 naive — a 250× reduction. That advantage isn’t unconditional — past a crossover point (25,000 differing keys at this dataset size, 2.5% of it) enough of the tree has to be walked that the tree stops being cheaper than just comparing everything.

06

The trade-off — neither mechanism is free, and neither is sufficient alone

Read repair’s cost is nearly zero and its coverage is incomplete. It piggybacks on traffic that was going to happen anyway — a read that already touched R replicas gets a free comparison and, on a mismatch, one extra write. But its entire domain is “keys someone reads,” and the formula above states precisely how bad the blind spot is: an unread key’s expected convergence time is not large, it is undefined in the sense that no finite amount of waiting produces it.

Anti-entropy’s cost is real bandwidth and CPU, paid on a schedule regardless of need, and its coverage is complete — it eventually compares every key, hot or cold. A Merkle tree is what makes that cost tolerable: without it, “compare every key between every pair of replicas” is the naive linear cost in the table above, repeated on every anti-entropy pass, against the entire dataset, whether or not anything actually diverged. With it, a pass that finds nothing wrong costs essentially one root-hash comparison, and a pass that finds K things wrong costs K × depth × branchingFactor — cheap specifically because divergence is normally rare.

Running only one of the two is a real, common misconfiguration, not a strawman. A store with read repair but a disabled or infrequent anti-entropy pass looks fine under normal load — hot keys stay converged — and silently accumulates wrong cold data that an audit eventually finds, as in slot 1. A store with only anti-entropy pays its full background cost continuously, even for data that a single read would have fixed for free. Dynamo-style systems run both because they solve different halves of the same problem, not the same half twice.

07

In the wild

Dynamo (SOSP 2007) is the canonical leaderless design, and it is also where the R/W/N vocabulary that quorums.mdx covers came from. The same paper’s mechanism for keeping un-contacted replicas current — comparing hash trees between replicas rather than transferring raw data — is the Merkle-tree anti-entropy this lesson models; DDIA’s account of it (below) is the more precise and directly cited source for the specific mechanics used here.

Riak and Cassandra both implement read repair and anti-entropy as named, separate features — Cassandra’s is nodetool repair for anti-entropy and a per-query read_repair_chance for the reactive path; Riak’s active anti-entropy runs continuously in the background specifically because its authors observed that read-triggered repair alone left cold keys diverging indefinitely — the exact failure mode slot 1 describes.

DDIA, ch. 6, “Catching up on missed writes” (printed p. 231) states both mechanisms in the same two paragraphs this lesson is built around: “Read repair… works well for values that are read often,” and anti-entropy as the background process that catches “values that are never read and therefore not handled by read repair” — the book’s own framing of exactly the gap this lesson quantifies. The same chapter (p. 237, “Detecting Concurrent Writes”) is where concurrent writes and version vectors are covered — a related but separate problem this lesson does not re-open, because leaderless conflict resolution and the two convergence mechanisms here are independent concerns.

DDIA, ch. 10, p. 412 notes that Dynamo-style quorums can be made linearizable by having a reader perform read repair synchronously, before returning — at a real performance cost — and that Cassandra does this on quorum reads while Riak does not, for exactly that reason. It’s the sharpest illustration in the book that “read repair” names a family of designs with different consistency/latency trades, not one fixed behavior.

Model and numbers — every figure computed by src/sim/leaderless-replication.mjs and asserted in src/sim/leaderless-replication.test.mjs (12 tests): the quorum-overlap functions checked for identity against quorum.mjs, read-detection probability against brute-force enumeration of every read quorum for N ≤ 7, and Merkle-tree cost against an actual tree walk that deduplicates shared ancestors.

Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed. — ch. 6, “Catching Up on Missed Writes” (printed p. 231): read repair, hinted handoff and anti-entropy, named and distinguished exactly as this lesson uses them; p. 237, “Detecting Concurrent Writes”; ch. 10, p. 412 on synchronous read repair and linearizable Dynamo-style quorums.

DeCandia et al., “Dynamo: Amazon’s Highly Available Key-value Store”, SOSP 2007 — the leaderless design this lesson’s terminology comes from, and the paper that introduced comparing replicas via Merkle trees rather than transferring raw data. PDF

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Leaderless replication removes the one structural feature single- and multi-leader systems both keep — a designated node that accepts a given write first — and replaces it with: any replica can take a write or a read, usually via a stateless coordinator that only proxies the request and holds no authority over ordering
  • The durability guarantee leaderless replication buys is not new — it is exactly `quorums.mdx`'s R + W > N, applied unchanged: at least one replica in any read quorum is guaranteed to hold the latest write, and a sloppy quorum (writing to W nodes elsewhere when the designated replicas are unreachable) is the one documented way that guarantee goes silently missing
  • Read repair is reactive and read-triggered: a read that touches both a stale and a fresh replica detects the mismatch and pushes the correct value to the stale one right then — which means it only ever fixes a key that someone happened to read
  • An unread key's convergence time under read repair alone is unboundedly long — nothing forces anyone to read it — while a key read regularly converges in a small, computable number of expected reads regardless of how fast or slow the clock ticks
  • Anti-entropy is proactive and read-independent: a background process compares replicas on its own schedule and is the only one of the two mechanisms that reaches a cold key read repair will never touch
  • A Merkle tree turns "which keys differ between two replicas" from a linear scan of the whole dataset into a walk that only descends into subtrees whose hash disagrees — cost scales with the number of differing keys times the tree's depth, and depth grows only logarithmically as the dataset grows
  • Read repair and anti-entropy are not substitutes for each other — one is fast and narrow (whatever gets read), the other is slow and complete (everything, eventually) — and a leaderless store runs both because neither alone converges the whole keyspace

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why does read repair only fix a key that "gets read", and what does that imply for a key that's written once and never looked at again?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Read repair's entire mechanism is a read: a client's read touches several replicas, notices one returned a stale value, and pushes the fresh value to it as a side effect of that specific read. There is no read, there is no comparison, there is no repair. A key that's written once and never read again has its convergence time determined by `readFrequencyPerSecond = 0`, and the formula gives exactly what that implies: infinite expected convergence time. Not "eventually" — never, unless something else (anti-entropy) intervenes.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A Merkle-tree anti-entropy scan over 1,000,000 keys with 100 keys actually differing costs how many hash comparisons, and how does that compare to comparing every key?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    With a binary tree, depth is ⌈log₂(1,000,000)⌉ = 20 levels. Each of the 100 differing keys forces a comparison at every level of its root-to-leaf path, two sibling hashes at a time: 100 × 20 × 2 = 4,000 comparisons. Comparing every key directly costs all 1,000,000. That is a 250× reduction — computed by `merkleTreeComparisonCost` and `naiveComparisonCost` in `leaderless-replication.mjs`, not estimated.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `quorums.mdx` already proves that R + W > N guarantees a read quorum overlaps a write quorum. If that's true, why does a leaderless store need read repair or anti-entropy at all — doesn't the overlap already mean every read sees the latest value?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The overlap guarantees a read quorum touches a replica that took the write — it does not guarantee every replica the read touches agrees, and it says nothing about what happens to the replicas the read didn't touch. A write that reached W of N replicas leaves N − W replicas stale until something updates them; R + W > N only ensures a reader can't be unlucky enough to miss the fresh copy entirely, at the moment of that one read. The stale replicas are still stale afterward. Read repair and anti-entropy are what actually shrinks that stale set over time — the overlap rule is a per-read guarantee, not a convergence mechanism, and conflating the two is exactly the gap this lesson exists to close.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system with no central authority stays approximately correct through two mechanisms that trade off completeness against triggering condition — one that acts only when something is actively used, and one that sweeps everything on its own schedule regardless of use. Name an instance outside distributed storage.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A library's shelving errors, fixed by patrons versus fixed by inventory audits. A patron who pulls a misshelved book off the shelf and can't find its neighbor might report it to a librarian, who re-shelves it correctly on the spot — but that only happens for books someone actually goes looking for. A book nobody has requested in three years stays misshelved indefinitely under that mechanism alone, exactly like an unread key. The library also runs a periodic full-shelf inventory — slow, expensive, touches every book whether or not anyone wanted it — which is the only process that ever finds and fixes the book nobody asked for. Neither replaces the other: patron reports are fast for popular books and blind to unpopular ones; the audit is complete but runs on a calendar, not on demand.
    Did you have that?
