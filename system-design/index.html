TIER 0 Why anything is hard at all 28/30

The tier that does not exist anywhere else. Every other resource opens at "add a load balancer", which answers before the question has been asked.

Operational vs analytical orphan 1 rig One database can accept orders and answer reports, until the report becomes the workload. The first architectural fork is not which database to buy; it is which data is authoritative, which data is derived, and where each kind of work is allowed to run. Latency numbers 2 rigs Jeff Dean's table of latency numbers is on every system-design cheat sheet, and almost nobody can use it — because nine orders of magnitude do not fit in a human head. The ratios do, and they are the part that decides your design. One request end to end orphan 1 rig A request passes through two kernel queues before any line of your code runs, and both of them drop connections without producing a single application-visible event. At 20,000 new connections a second the modern default accept queue absorbs 204.8 ms of silence — the historical default absorbed 6.4 ms. This is the vocabulary the rest of the tier assumes. Back-of-envelope orphan 1 rig Estimation is not about being right. It is about being able to reject a claim in two minutes with arithmetic anyone can check — and about discovering, before you build, that one unstated input decides the entire design. Byte budgets orphan 1 rig Everyone estimates storage from the payload and is wrong by an order of magnitude, systematically and in the same direction. The multipliers — row overhead, indexes, compression that does not apply, rendition ladders, replication — are each individually unsurprising and they compound. Worse, the smallest rows have the worst overhead ratio, so the table you thought was cheap is the expensive one. Little's Law 1 rig L = λW is the only law in this curriculum that assumes nothing — no arrival pattern, no service-time distribution, no queue discipline. It turns a latency into a headcount, runs backwards to catch a latency metric that is lying to you, and hands you a confidently wrong answer in exactly two situations. Queueing & utilisation orphan 3 rigs Nothing broke, no bug shipped, the CPU is not at 100% — and your service is five times slower than it was yesterday. This is the first thing to understand about systems at scale, and it happens on a single machine. Throughput vs latency orphan 1 rig Bandwidth is a capacity, throughput is an achieved rate, latency is a duration for one request, and the three get used as synonyms. They are not reciprocals — `throughput = concurrency / latency`, so a third variable breaks the link — and they routinely move in opposite directions, which is why every batch size is a defensible answer. The number worth carrying: a 5 ms latency budget lets you use 87.7% of a machine, and the last twelve points cost a tenfold budget. Write path / read path orphan 1 rig An index, a cache, a materialised view, a denormalised column, a precomputed timeline — these are taught as six techniques and they are one decision made six times: how much of the derivation happens eagerly at write time versus lazily at read time. The work is moved, not removed. And the optimum is a corner: across six decades of plausible read/write ratios, the middle is best in under one per cent of them. One machine's ceiling 1 rig CPU, memory, disk and network all run out eventually — but only one of them runs out first, and the other three are money you have already spent and cannot reach. Knowing which one gives out is the whole skill, and the CPU graph will not tell you. Event loops & C10K orphan 1 rig The two reasons everyone gives for choosing an event loop — threads eat memory, context switches eat CPU — are both off by two orders of magnitude on a modern machine. The reason that is real never gets stated: a loop's whole advantage is that it never blocks, and one synchronous call in one handler takes 11× the throughput off eight cores. Blocking vs async orphan 1 rig A blocked thread costs memory and zero CPU. A retry loop costs CPU and zero memory. An async completion costs a queue entry, a share of one wakeup, and a per-operation fee the other two never pay — so below a computable concurrency, and above a computable ready fraction, "make it async" is a rewrite that ends with more CPU per operation than it started with. Amdahl & the USL orphan 1 rig Tripling a fleet from 32 machines to 96 can leave you with less throughput than you started with — and 20 machines would have matched all three of those numbers. Amdahl's Law explains the flattening; Gunther's Universal Scalability Law explains the decline. Vertical vs horizontal orphan 1 rig Vertical and horizontal scaling are not two settings of one dial — they raise N against different terms of the same equation. One machine of 50 units and 50 machines of one unit do exactly the same amount of work, and nine machines of 5.6 units do 2.7 times more from identical hardware. Performance vs scalability orphan 1 rig Performance is how long one unit of work takes for one user. Scalability is whether that number survives more load or more data. They are separate axes, all four quadrants are populated, and the interesting designs are the ones where buying one sells the other. The number worth carrying: put 1.2 ms of an 8 ms request inside a lock and you have set a ceiling of 833 requests a second that no amount of hardware moves. Why averages lie orphan 1 rig Your dashboard says 26 milliseconds and it is not lying. Four requests in five are faster than that number, one in a thousand takes three hundred, and the figure on the dashboard is not a percentile of anything. Then somebody averages the p99s across ten machines and the last honest number on the page is gone too. Availability math 1 rig Series multiplies availability down and parallel multiplies failure down, and the two feel like they belong to different universes. They do not: adding a required dependency and adding a second replica move a downtime budget by exactly the same number of minutes, always, at every component availability. The third replica moves it by a thousandth of that, and the fourth buys thirty-one milliseconds of uptime a year. Seven three-nines services in a row are two nines, not three. MTBF and MTTR orphan 1 rig Availability is a duty cycle — MTBF over MTBF plus MTTR — which means it depends on the two means only through their ratio. So one four-hour outage a year and fifty-two five-minute outages a year are the same percentage and nothing like the same system, and a thing that fails every single day but recovers in twenty seconds beats both. Halving repair time and doubling reliability buy exactly the same availability; they do not cost remotely the same. The three guarantees orphan 1 rig Availability, reliability and durability are not three grades of the same thing — they are measured in three different units, so a number in one says nothing about the other two. Availability has no time argument at all, which is why one SLO figure covers systems whose chance of an incident-free year differs by 136 orders of magnitude. And durability has no repair term, which is why eleven nines of it sits beside four nines of availability in the same published spec sheet, honestly. The tail at scale orphan 1 rig One hundred shards, each answering in 10 milliseconds, each slow only 1% of the time. Every shard dashboard is green. And 63% of user requests take more than a second, because the user does not wait for the average backend — the user waits for the worst one. This is multiplication, not a bug. Hedged requests orphan 1 rig A hedge fires only when the first attempt has already crossed the 95th percentile, so it costs 5% more requests rather than double — and once it has fired the leg is the minimum of two draws, so the tail gets squared. Both halves stop being true at the same moment: when the slowness has a shared cause, or when the extra 5% is what saturated the system. The break-even is 1/(1 + 2e), which is 91% utilisation for a p95 hedge. Stop-the-world pauses orphan 1 rig A garbage collector, a hypervisor stealing a vCPU, a cgroup CPU quota running out mid-slice and a writeback storm all do the same thing: the process stops for a while and does not know it stopped. That produces a latency bug with a cliff in it — cross 1% of requests and your p99 is the pause length, whatever your code does — and a correctness bug that is worse, because a paused process is indistinguishable from a crashed one and its peers will give its lease away while it still believes it holds it. Failure is normal orphan 1 rig Every reliability figure you are given is per component and per year. Every question you have is about the whole fleet and a much shorter interval. The conversion is one multiplication and it changes what a failure *is*: at Backblaze's published 1.4% annualised rate, a rack of 480 drives loses one every 54 days and a hundred thousand drives lose one every 6.3 hours. Then the harder finding — the failure rate is not constant with age, and a constant-hazard model calibrated on your own first year understates the next five by 3.02×. Single point of failure orphan 1 rig The phrase is easy to define and almost impossible to apply to your own system, for a structural reason: the redundancy you build is in the data plane, where the boxes on the diagram are, and the single points that survive are in the control plane, which is not on the diagram at all. A config service, a service registry, a secret store, one DNS zone, one certificate job, one identity provider. Each is required by everything and drawn by nobody — and giving them a second instance makes availability *worse*. Correlated failure orphan 1 rig Redundancy multiplies only if the failures are independent. Put one per cent of shared cause into a pair of three-nines replicas and they are eleven times more likely to fail together than the arithmetic promised — and past the third replica, adding more buys literally nothing, because the shared term does not shrink. Worse, six of the ten shared causes are immune to placement diversity, which is the only fix the industry has seriously engineered. Maintainability orphan 1 rig Maintainability loses every planning meeting for a structural reason rather than a cultural one: the feature is a benefit now and every maintainability property is a cost avoided later. The fix is arithmetic. All three properties the word covers are separately computable, two of them have dates on them — a team at 20% toil on a fleet growing 40% a year is a pure operations function in 4.8 years — and the module boundary everyone reaches for is worth 15× when it seals and 1.1× when a fifth of it leaks. The microsecond regime orphan 1 rig Below about a hundred microseconds, latency stops being about how fast your work runs and becomes about how many layers your work passes through. Every technique here removes a layer rather than speeding one up — and most systems should not pay what that costs. Client-perceived latency orphan 1 rig Every percentile on your dashboard is computed over requests that arrived. The user's percentile is computed over intents — and the two diverge twice over. Once because time-to-usable is a chain of round trips your server contributes 2.6% of, and again because the users who gave up never sent the beacon that would have put them in your data.

TIER 1 The network 32/34

TCP, TLS, HTTP/3, and the real-time menu — only the parts that change a decision.

DNS and TTLs orphan 1 rig DNS is a naming system with caching, and almost everyone uses it as a control plane. Caches in series add their TTLs rather than sharing one, negative answers get a TTL sixty times longer than the record you were publishing, and an RFC-compliant resolver may serve an expired answer for a week — precisely when your nameservers are the thing that broke. Record types and the apex orphan 1 rig A CNAME says "this name is somewhere else", and RFC 1034 forbids anything else from sharing that name. A zone's apex must carry its SOA and its NS records. So of the 512 combinations of the nine standard record types, 64 are legal at an apex and none of them contains a CNAME. Every provider's ALIAS is a workaround with a bill: an extra resolution, a TTL they choose, and the CDN's geo-routing answered from their network instead of yours. Routing policies orphan 1 rig Weighted, latency-based, geo and health-checked DNS are sold as global load balancing. They divide resolutions, not requests, and nothing in the loop ever observes a queue. At 8,000 requests per second behind a 300-second TTL, one routing decision commits 80,001 requests — and the realised split's standard deviation depends on the resolver population, not on your weight. You would need 2,401 equally-sized resolvers to land within two points. Anycast orphan 1 rig One IP, announced from every PoP, and each router picks the shortest AS-path to it — a count of peering hops that reflects who sells transit to whom, not distance. On a network with realistic peering density that sends a majority of vantage points to a PoP that is not their nearest, by a detour bigger than the trip itself. And when the catchment changes, nothing tells the TCP connection riding it: a withdrawal reaches half the network in about two minutes at the classic 30-second MRAI, and a session that long survives that change only as often as `exp(−t/MTBF)` says it will. Connection setup orphan 1 rig A request is not one round trip. A cold HTTPS request is four, three of which carry no application data at all — so for a small response the answer arrives when distance says it can, and a wider link changes it by a rounding error. The number worth knowing is how big a response has to get before the bytes cost more than the introductions. The layers, briefly orphan 1 rig Seven layers exist so each one can be reasoned about, and changed, without touching the others — but almost every real decision only touches two or three of them. This lesson puts a number on the two places that separation actually bites: header overhead, which is 73% of a 20-byte VoIP sample and under 1% of an 8 kB file, and path MTU, where adding a tunnel silently eats bytes at layer 2 or 3 while every symptom shows up as a mysterious timeout at layer 7 — three layers and, often, a different team away from the change that caused it. IP, subnets, NAT orphan 1 rig Splitting a block into equal subnets is not free — every new subnet needs its own reserved network and broadcast address, so a /24 split four ways loses 6 usable hosts to bookkeeping it didn't need before. One layer up, a carrier-grade NAT shares one public IP's 64,512 usable ports across every subscriber behind it: at a realistic subscriber count, that block can be smaller than the concurrent connections Little's Law says one person's browsing needs — a hard exhaustion nobody's application code will ever see coming. And the reason both exist at all: 86.6% of a 32-bit address space was never going to cover billions of devices, while a single standard IPv6 subnet holds 4.3 billion IPv4 internets' worth of addresses. Packet loss and retransmit orphan 1 rig Fast retransmit needs three duplicate ACKs, which needs three packets to arrive after the lost one — a packet lost near the end of a burst has no followers to generate that evidence, so it waits out the retransmission timer instead. That timer is floored at one full second by RFC 6298 regardless of how fast the network actually is, and a lost SYN before any RTT sample exists starts there and doubles on every retry: six retries is 63 seconds before a connection attempt gives up, on a formula anyone can compute, not a mystery hang. Congestion control orphan 1 rig A sender is not told its share of the network; it has to guess, and every algorithm guesses from a different measurement. Guess from dropped packets and you need a full buffer to learn anything, so you build the delay you then report. Guess from delivered rate and you keep 93% of a link that 1% loss reduces the other sender to under 1% of. And a short transfer never gets far enough into any of this to find out, because it spends its whole life doubling. Bandwidth-delay product orphan 1 rig One connection's throughput is a window divided by a round-trip time. That makes distance a divisor and bandwidth only a ceiling — so an intercontinental 10 Gbps link carries a single stream at a thousandth of its rate, and the fix is never a faster link. The 40 ms mystery orphan 1 rig Nagle will not send a small segment while anything is unacknowledged. Delayed ACK will not acknowledge until it has data to piggyback on or a second full segment to cover. Put them on opposite ends of one connection and each waits for the other, so the latency is decided by a timer — 40 ms on Linux, 200 on Windows, 500 by the letter of the RFC. Both algorithms are correct. Neither is broken. And the escape is not a big first write, it is a remainder of zero. UDP, and when you want it orphan 1 rig This isn't "TCP is sometimes too slow for real-time media" — it's categorical. RFC 6298 floors TCP's retransmission timer at a full second no matter how fast the network is, and no real-time jitter buffer can afford to hold a second of audio or video waiting to find out if a packet is coming. UDP drops the late packet and moves on; the gap gets concealed, not fixed. And the same protocol that can't wait for anyone has a second unrelated superpower: multicast delivers the identical stream to any number of receivers for the cost of one send, where unicast — TCP's only option — costs exactly proportional to how many are watching. Port exhaustion orphan 1 rig A connection is identified by five things, four of which are fixed when you talk to one backend, so the only free variable is the source port — and there are 28,232 of them. A closed socket holds its port for another 60 seconds, which turns a count into a rate: about 470 new connections a second to any one destination, forever, no matter how fast the requests are. A port pool has no waiting room, so the failure is Erlang-B, and the first failures appear 0.8% below the ceiling. There is no degraded region to alert on. Connection pooling orphan 1 rig A pool is a queue with a server count you chose by hand, so its size is set by Little's Law and the wait in front of it is Erlang-C shaped. That makes both mistakes look identical from the outside — too few connections and callers wait while the backend idles, too many and the queue moves inside the backend where you cannot time it out — and it makes the number the database actually sees a product nobody configured. Head-of-line blocking orphan 1 rig HTTP/2 put forty requests on one connection and deleted the blocking that HTTP/1.1's six-connection workaround existed to hide. It did not delete the other one: TCP hands the application a single byte order, so one lost segment stalls every stream behind it — including the thirty-two that lost nothing. HTTP/3 moves the blocking below loss recovery. It cannot move the coupling nobody can remove, because one connection is still one congestion window. TLS on the wire orphan 1 rig The previous lesson counted the round trips. This one opens them. A server's first TLS flight is overwhelmingly one message — the certificate chain — and its size is set by your certificate authority's hierarchy and your key algorithm, not by your protocol version. On QUIC that size decides whether a cold handshake takes one round trip or two. TLS 1.3, 0-RTT orphan 1 rig Turning on session resumption without 0-RTT is a common, well-intentioned change that saves nothing: a resumed handshake still runs a full round trip of TLS messages before the server will accept ordinary application data, identically to a fresh one. Only the `early_data` extension — 0-RTT — removes that round trip, by letting the client attach a request to its very first flight. And that's exactly why it's the one mode with no freshness guarantee: a captured first flight can be replayed by anyone holding a copy of it, up to 1.8 million times inside one day's ticket lifetime at a typical round trip time, unless the server pays for a dedup cache or restricts 0-RTT to requests a repeat can't damage. mTLS orphan 1 rig Asking the client to prove its identity too is not "TLS, but twice." The added CPU is small and landed almost entirely on the server, because a chain-verify is expensive and a signature is cheap. The real bill is operational: every client now needs a certificate before it can connect at all, and a client fleet is usually orders of magnitude bigger than a server fleet. Revocation orphan 1 rig Revoking a certificate and a client actually refusing it are two different moments, and the gap between them is a deliberate configuration choice, not a network glitch. A CRL is stale until its next scheduled update; a stapled OCSP response is stale until the server refreshes it; and a live OCSP query — the only one of the three with no built-in staleness — is also the only one that silently stops checking anything at all the moment its responder is unreachable. HTTP semantics orphan 1 rig Safe, idempotent, and cacheable are not three descriptions of the same good behavior — they are three independent, RFC-fixed properties, and a method or a status code either has one or it doesn't regardless of what any specific API author intends. What that buys is concrete: retrying an idempotent request after a timeout is provably harmless, while retrying a non-idempotent one is a measurable, nonzero risk that doesn't depend on which non-idempotent method you picked. And a cache is allowed to guess freshness for a response with zero caching headers — but only for a short, fixed list of status codes, error pages included. HTTP caching semantics 1 rig A 304 Not Modified is a request. It costs a whole round trip, plus the entire connection handshake if the connection is cold, and it removes only the body — so on a small resource revalidation saves half the bytes and 0.2% of the time. The strategy that wins does not answer the freshness question faster; it renames the file so there is nothing to ask. HTTP/2 multiplexing orphan 1 rig HTTP/2 added its own flow control on top of TCP's, and its default window of 65,535 bytes is smaller than the bandwidth-delay product of essentially every path you care about — a gigabit connection at 100 ms is short by 191×, and a 1 ms LAN still caps gigabit at half. There is a second window shared across streams, where one reader that stops reading leaves exactly zero bytes for the other thirty-nine. And HPACK makes the streams order-dependent above the transport, which is why QUIC cannot remove that coupling either. QUIC and HTTP/3 orphan 1 rig A TCP connection *is* its 5-tuple, so any event that changes an address ends it. A QUIC connection is identified by a connection ID it carries, so it survives a Wi-Fi-to-cellular handover that would kill TCP outright. That is real, it is the one QUIC feature with no TCP equivalent — and it removes about two-fifths of what a network change costs, because both transports still have to climb out of slow start. Then a load balancer takes even that back. Transport compression orphan 1 rig Compressing a response costs time to save time, so there is a bandwidth above which it is a straight loss — and it is `(1 − 1/ratio)` over the harmonic sum of compress and decompress throughput. That puts brotli-11 at 5.6 Mbps, gzip-6 at 180, and zstd-1 at 1.8 Gbps. Compressibility barely enters it: the saved fraction varies by 1.2× across every codec here while their throughput varies by 522×. And the level you should pick is not a fact about your content — it is a fact about whether you can cache the result. Pagination orphan 1 rig `OFFSET k LIMIT s` cannot jump. The engine walks k rows and throws them away, so page depth is a cost and a full walk is quadratic — which is why a table growing 3.3× turned a 37-minute export into a 7-hour one. The second problem is worse and almost never named: an offset is a count into a list that is still being edited, so a reader scrolling a live feed is shown rows they have already seen and never shown rows that were there when they started. Past one threshold they make no progress at all, and every request returns 200 with a full page. REST & versioning orphan 1 rig "Versioning" sounds like one decision — v1, v2, a header, a URL — but the number that actually matters is a policy choice hiding behind it: does a new version have to stay compatible with the ONE before it, with EVERY version ever shipped, or with nothing because nothing was ever removed. Those three policies cost 0, N, and N² compatibility checks respectively, the same pairwise- agreement shape this course already found in team coordination. And a deprecated version doesn't vanish when you announce end-of-life — it decays on a curve, and halving the time it takes to reach 99% migrated needs the migration rate to more than double, not just double. GraphQL N+1 orphan 1 rig GraphQL's most infamous performance bug is the identical tree-fanout arithmetic this course already derived for authorization graph traversal, wearing a different name. A naive resolver issues one query per node in the response tree — the same f-ary sum as walking a group-membership graph — and the fix is the same fix: batch every request at one LEVEL into a single round trip, so total queries becomes a function of depth alone, never fanout. Two further findings ride on top: batching across concurrent requests, not just within one, is Little's Law; and sending a hash instead of a query body is a bandwidth trade with a protocol-defined, one-round-trip tax on a miss. gRPC and protobuf orphan 1 rig "Protobuf is 3–10× smaller than JSON" is a true sentence about a comparison nobody ships. Almost all of the win is field names going away, a compressor already removes repeated field names, and on a 50-row page the 3.13× you measure uncompressed is 1.18× on the wire. The parts of protobuf worth having are elsewhere: a field number that makes a rolling upgrade safe, and unknown fields that survive a hop — until a proxy re-serialises them away. Webhooks 1 rig A webhook sender retries on a schedule it controls, against a receiver it does not — the receiver's dedup window, its signature-verification code, and its own downtime are all opaque to the sender. This lesson computes what that opacity actually costs: how many retries can outlive a dedup window the sender can only guess at, how much cheaper a naive signature compare is to defeat than the signature's own length promises, and how deep a sender's own retry queue grows while it waits for a receiver that isn't answering. The real-time menu orphan 1 rig Polling, long polling, server-sent events, WebSocket. The feature tables compare them on bidirectionality, which decides nothing. The decision is one curve — compare the staleness you will accept against 1 ÷ (update rate + 1/hold) — and on one side of it there is no trade-off at all. Push does not remove the poll; it moves it and shrinks it. Proxies 1 rig A forward proxy and a reverse proxy are the same box shape pointed in opposite directions — one serves a client population talking to many destinations, the other serves many clients talking to one destination — and that direction, not the protocol, is the entire difference. This lesson computes what concentrating traffic at either one actually costs (CPU, connections, ports) and what it exposes if that one box is compromised. Service mesh 1 rig A service mesh's sidecar is nothing new — it's the forward-proxy/reverse-proxy box this course already costed, doing the mTLS this course already costed. What's actually new is what happens once there are N services instead of one proxy in front of one backend: an all-pairs topology to maintain, two extra hops that compound on every single call, and a control plane whose pushed configuration can be stale by a number this lesson computes exactly the way a DNS TTL already is.

TIER 2 Scaling the stateless tier 20/25

The easy half. Load balancing, health checks, autoscaling, and the traps in each.

Stateless discipline 1 rig Stateless doesn't mean "no state anywhere" — it means no state that only one process remembers: no session held only in an instance's memory, no local file a later request depends on, no routing rule that requires request N+1 to land back on the same box as request N. This lesson computes what breaking that discipline actually costs: how many sessions a single restart erases when they're pinned to memory, why scaling the fleet out doesn't change how many forced logouts happen a year — only their size — what moving that state to an external store costs on every request whether or not an instance ever fails, and why sticky routing quietly disables the load balancer sitting right underneath it. Load balancing orphan 2 rigs One backend in your fleet of eight gets four times slower. It does not crash, and its health check still passes. Round robin keeps feeding it and you start rejecting 9% of all traffic. Two of the five standard routing policies survive this untouched, and the cheaper of the two needs to look at exactly two servers. Health Checks 1 rig A shallow health check (does the process accept a connection?) and a deep one (does the real dependency work?) answer different questions, and of five catalogued failure modes a shallow check catches only one. That gap is the first half of this lesson. The second half is the trap a correct deep check sets: pulling the backends it correctly fails concentrates the same total load onto fewer survivors, and if that pushes them past their own queueing limit, they fail the identical check — not from the original bug, but from the fleet's own remediation. Connection draining 1 rig A backend told to shut down is still carrying whatever it was carrying a moment before — that population is fixed by Little's Law, not by how politely anyone asks it to leave. The load balancer needs its own time to notice and stop sending new work, which eats into the drain window before any draining happens at all. This lesson computes what's left once that lag is subtracted, what fraction of the in-flight population is still running when the process is finally killed, and what buying a higher completion rate actually costs every rolling deploy that ever takes a backend down. Thundering herd 1 rig Nobody sent more work than the service can handle. They just sent all of it in the same millisecond, and the requests that suffer most are the ones that had nothing to do with it. Sticky sessions 1 rig `stateless-discipline.mdx` already computes what a hash-pinned, Zipf-skewed session population does to a fleet. This lesson asks what's left once that skew is removed entirely: sessions handed out by a plain round robin, none of them any busier than another. Even then, the busiest instance runs measurably over the fleet's own mean, purely because sessions arrive and leave on independent clocks — and once an instance IS overloaded, sticky pinning has already taken away the load balancer's one real tool for it. This lesson computes both: how far pure arrival/departure timing pushes one instance above the pack, and how long even the fastest possible response takes to bring it back, compared with the zero seconds a stateless backend needs for the identical overload. Autoscaling 1 rig Reactive, predictive, and scheduled autoscaling are not three different mechanisms — they are one mechanism (detect load, then add capacity) with one shared, additive lag, and the three names describe how much of that lag a strategy avoids by acting ahead of a live metric instead of waiting for one. This lesson computes the lag itself, what the existing — undersized — fleet does to its own queue for the whole time that lag is running, and the backlog and drain time left behind once new capacity finally shows up. Warm-up & cold start 1 rig A health check tells you an instance is UP. It says nothing about whether the instance is WARM — whether its JIT has compiled the hot paths, its connection pools are populated, and its caches hold anything. This lesson puts a number on the gap: how much slower a fresh instance actually is, how long that lasts, and what handing it 100% of traffic immediately costs in aggregate queueing time compared to ramping it in. Serverless model 1 rig A provisioned instance costs the same per hour whether it serves one request or ten thousand. A serverless function costs (near) nothing at zero traffic and rises linearly with request rate — two cost curves, one flat and one rising, that cross exactly once. This lesson computes that crossing point, the "cost cliff" the roster names, and a second, unrelated ceiling every serverless platform enforces that a provisioned fleet never has to: a hard limit on concurrent executions, past which requests are throttled outright rather than merely queued. Containers vs VMs 1 rig A container and a VM both promise "your process, isolated from everyone else's." They deliver that promise through completely different mechanisms — one shares the host kernel, the other virtualizes a second one — and that mechanism difference is the entire reason one packs a host far denser and starts far faster than the other. This lesson computes the density a fixed memory budget actually buys under each, and prices the isolation the denser option gives up to get it. K8s reconciliation 1 rig A Kubernetes scheduler places a pod exactly once, against its REQUESTS — a reservation made now. A pod's LIMITS are a separate, later promise, and the scheduler never checks whether limits summed across a node even fit, which is why a node can be "overcommitted" and still be doing nothing wrong. This lesson computes the moment that stops being true: when actual usage, not requests and not limits, crosses physical capacity — and why the two resources fail in completely different ways when it does, and why the controller that fixes it runs as a loop with its own measurable delay rather than a callback that fires the instant something breaks. Deployment Strategies 1 rig Rolling, blue-green, canary, and shadow traffic are not four unrelated techniques — they are four schedules for the same variable: what share of a fleet, and of real traffic, is running a new version that might be bad. This lesson shows that detecting a bad version needs a fixed number of real exposures no matter which strategy is running — a one-sample statistical test that doesn't care about your deployment tooling — and that the strategies differ only in how fast wall-clock time gathers that number and what fraction of live users is exposed while it does. It computes that number, the wall-clock cost of reaching it four different ways, and the capacity bill blue-green and shadow traffic pay to change the trade. Feature flags 1 rig A feature flag's whole promise is that shipping code and exposing it become two separate, independently reversible decisions. This lesson computes what that promise actually costs. A percentage rollout is a hashing problem — get the key wrong (a coin flip instead of a stable user id) and the SAME user flickers between old and new on almost every request. Get the key right and a second problem appears anyway: the rollout percentage itself lives in a snapshot that refreshes on a timer, and two evaluations of one user's session — one at the start of a request path, one further along it — can read that snapshot at two different ages and disagree. Add flags that outlive their purpose and a flag service that can itself be slow or down, and a deploy primitive sold as pure upside turns out to have three measurable costs of its own. Multi-AZ vs multi-region 1 rig Multi-AZ and multi-region are sold as the same feature at a bigger radius: replicate outside one failure domain. They are not the same trade. An AZ boundary and a region boundary stop genuinely different failures, at genuinely different odds — and the distance between two AZs is small enough that synchronous replication barely costs anything, while the distance between two regions is large enough that the same synchronous replication costs a write's entire latency budget. This lesson computes both gaps: what each boundary actually protects against, and the exact point — much closer than either concrete region pair it uses — where "just replicate synchronously, but further" stops being an option at all. Active-active vs passive 1 rig Cold, warm, hot and active-active are usually taught as one ladder of "how much readiness do you want," as if climbing it is strictly good. It isn't a single trade — it's two independent ones, stacked. Climbing the ladder collapses recovery time (RTO), which this lesson computes stage by stage. It does NOT automatically reduce what a standby wastes: warm and hot standby pay close to a full site's bill for a site that serves nothing, and active-active only escapes that waste if its capacity can actually be used — writes partitioned by key, or conflicts resolved, across both sites. Get that part wrong and active-active isn't the free upgrade it looks like: it's a hot standby that costs the same and happens to answer some reads. (Replication lag and RPO are `multi-az-multi-region.mdx`'s model, reused unchanged here — this lesson's own contribution is RTO and the idle-capacity bill.) Cell-based architecture 1 rig Multi-AZ and multi-region replicate the same system outside a bigger failure domain. Cell-based architecture cuts a different way: it PARTITIONS one region into N independent, fully self-contained slices — each with its own compute, its own data store, its own queues — and routes every tenant to exactly one slice, forever. The payoff is a blast radius that is structurally 1/N instead of 100%, and this lesson computes exactly when that payoff shows up: for a plain cell failure, always; for a bad deploy, only if the rollout is staged cell-by-cell rather than pushed everywhere at once. It also computes the two things that arithmetic doesn't hand you for free — a router that fronts every cell and is therefore the one part of the design that isn't divided by N, and an operational bill (and a sizing risk) that grows right along with it. Shuffle sharding 1 rig Isolating tenants by giving each one a fully separate cell works, and it means running N times the infrastructure. Shuffle sharding gets most of the same isolation from ONE shared pool: instead of pinning a tenant to a fixed resource, give it a random, almost-unique COMBINATION of resources drawn from the pool. This lesson computes exactly how much isolation that buys — as a binomial coefficient, not a slogan — and why it gets better, not worse, as the pool grows. Multi-tenancy & noisy neighbours 1 rig Every tenant sharing a pool — CPU, disk I/O, a database, a queue — is exposed to every other tenant's usage, whether or not either one did anything wrong. `cell-based-architecture` and `shuffle-sharding` answer this by dividing the pool up so tenants stop sharing it. This lesson states the problem precisely and computes the cheaper answers that don't require dividing anything: a hard per-tenant quota, and fair-share scheduling — which turns out to buy the same protection as the quota, plus flexibility the quota structurally cannot offer. Gray Failure 1 rig A gray-failed node is invisible to any health check — shallow or deep — that exercises a cheaper, unaffected code path than the one real traffic actually takes. `health-checks.mdx` already showed a deep check misses whatever it never asks about; this lesson shows a check can ask exactly the right question and still miss the fault, because "deep" (calls the real dependency) is not the same claim as "as slow as the real request path." The only signal a gray-failed node cannot fake is what real requests actually experience — a slower, noisier signal than a binary probe, and this lesson computes exactly how much slower, how much noisier, and how much worse it is to have no such signal running at all. Edge isolates & WASM 1 rig An edge platform (Cloudflare Workers, Fastly Compute@Edge) runs a request handler behind neither a container nor a VM but a third, cheaper primitive: a V8 isolate or a WASM sandbox sharing not just the host's kernel but its own already-running process. This lesson extends `containers-vs-vms.mdx`'s density-and-startup ladder down one more rung, computes what that rung actually buys and what it costs, and separates the isolate's real advantage — startup, overwhelmingly, and density only at the small app sizes an edge function actually has — from what it gives up to get it: an OS-enforced boundary, traded for a runtime's own bookkeeping.

TIER 3 Storage and state 71/82

The hard half, and the biggest tier. Storage engines, indexes, isolation, replication, sharding, consistency.

B-tree vs LSM orphan 1 rig Every database picks one of two ways to keep data on disk, and the choice determines whether your workload is fast or hopeless. The usual summary — "B-trees for reads, LSM for writes" — is not wrong so much as missing the number that decides it, which is a property of your hardware and not of either data structure. Consistent hashing orphan 1 rig `hash(key) % N` is correct, obvious, and one line long. It also relocates almost your entire dataset the first time N changes — and the bigger your cluster, the worse that gets. The fix is a circle, and the circle has a price nobody mentions. Hot partitions orphan 1 rig Your shard map is perfectly balanced. One node is at 300% of a fair share and falling over. Both of those sentences are true, because a partitioner balances keys and your users request traffic — and a key is the smallest thing you are allowed to split. Quorums orphan 1 rig The rule everyone can recite is a constraint, not an answer. It guarantees exactly one thing — that your read set and write set share a node — and leaves latency, availability and fault tolerance completely undetermined. Four configurations satisfy it and behave nothing alike, and one of them is less available than no replication at all. Replication lag orphan 1 rig Lag is not a delay, it is a queue, so it obeys 1/(1−ρ) and not intuition. A replica with 5% headroom takes 200 seconds to clear a 10-second stall; with 1% headroom it takes 1,000. And the utilisation that decides this is not on the leader's dashboard — a sixteen-way leader saturates a single-threaded replica at 6% of its own capacity. Isolation anomalies orphan 1 rig The isolation levels you can name are not the levels you get. Locking REPEATABLE READ and snapshot isolation are incomparable — each prevents an anomaly the other permits — so "at least REPEATABLE READ" is not a safety requirement. And the anomaly snapshot isolation cannot see commits both transactions cleanly, raises no error, and moves no metric. Heap files & page layout 1 rig A table's rows live on fixed-size pages whether anyone thinks about it or not — a page header, a slot array, and packed row data. This lesson counts what naive division gets wrong about how many rows fit, and shows why the same range scan costs a sequential handful of pages on one physical layout and a random fetch per row on another, with nothing about the query or the index changing at all. Row vs column storage 1 rig A row-oriented table packs one row's columns together; a column-oriented table packs one column's rows together. That single layout choice decides how many bytes a query reads off disk — by a factor that swings from "23 times fewer" to "worse than the alternative" depending on how much of the row you actually need — and it is also the reason a column store can process data in tight, branch-free batches instead of one row at a time. Compression techniques 1 rig Dictionary encoding, run-length encoding and delta encoding are each a bet on one specific shape a column can have — how few distinct values it has, whether the same value recurs next to itself, and whether consecutive numbers are close together — and a general-purpose LZ/entropy codec is the shape-blind fallback that catches a little of each bet without ever winning any of them outright. Applied to the wrong shape, a specialized technique doesn't just fail to help: dictionary encoding on a near-unique column and RLE on a column with no runs both make the column bigger than storing it raw. This lesson computes exactly when each one wins, by how much, and what it costs to guess wrong. WAL & fsync 1 rig Every durable system keeps a write-ahead log for one mechanical reason: appending sequentially is vastly cheaper than the random, whole-page rewrite a B-tree needs for the same change, and replaying the log after a crash reconstructs whatever was lost. But the log only helps if it is actually on the device — and calling `write()` does not put it there. This lesson computes the gap between the two, what group commit trades to close it cheaply, and the honest data-loss window a system accepts depending on exactly when it tells a client "committed." Shared-nothing vs shared-disk 1 rig A shared-nothing node bundles compute and local disk into one purchase, so scaling one always drags the other along, wanted or not. A shared-disk architecture — Aurora, Snowflake, Neon, ClickHouse-on-S3, Kafka's tiered storage — unbundles them into two independently billed, independently scaled layers, and pays for that split with a network round trip on every access the local-disk version never had to make. This lesson prices both halves of that trade: the stranded capacity a bundled node forces you to buy, and the cache hit rate a network-backed storage layer needs to stop that round trip from being the whole story. Object storage durability 1 rig Object storage's API has no call to seek into a live object and write just the changed bytes — PUT replaces the whole object, by key, every time. That single missing call is why every system built on top of an object store ends up append-only. This lesson prices what the missing call costs as an object grows, and shows the one primitive an object store DOES offer in place of a lock — the conditional write — actually preventing a lost update that a naive read-modify-write race would silently suffer. Replica placement 1 rig Replication factor N says how many copies of your data exist. It says nothing about WHERE those copies sit — and where turns out to dominate durability as a cluster grows. Naive random placement draws every item's copyset from a pool of combinations that grows combinatorially with cluster size, so a correlated failure of just a few nodes gets more and more distinct ways to land squarely on one item's entire copy set — even though the replication factor and the FRACTION of the cluster failing never change. This lesson computes that rise, computes the bounded-copyset scheme that stops it, and separately computes the simpler question of whether one item's own copies share a single rack or AZ at all. Erasure coding 1 rig Split the data into K pieces, compute M extra parity pieces, and any K of the K+M survive-together. That single trick is why cold storage at Facebook, Azure, and every major object store runs erasure-coded instead of triple-replicated — the storage bill drops by multiples. The bill nobody puts next to that saving is what happens when a disk actually dies: the same K that shrank the storage cost is exactly what it now costs, in nodes touched and bytes moved, to get one fragment back. Checksums & integrity 1 rig A disk's own checksum only proves the disk read back what it itself wrote. TCP's own checksum only proves the packet wasn't damaged in a way its 16-bit sum happens to catch — and that sum was chosen in the 1980s for cheap arithmetic, not strong detection. Neither layer can vouch for corruption introduced by the bus, the RAM, the controller, or a bit that flips after every check already ran. This lesson computes what closes that gap — an end-to-end checksum, verified by the application itself — and quantifies the two failure modes that motivate it: how a checksum can be blind to a whole class of real corruption, and how a scrub schedule is a race against silent, independent rot that shrinking the interval wins by more than the interval shrank. What is an index 1 rig Every later lesson in this subsection — clustered vs. secondary, composite and covering, selectivity, EXPLAIN plans — assumes one thing is already obvious: what an index actually IS, mechanically, and why having one changes a lookup's cost class at all. This lesson answers that, with nothing left implicit: an index is a second, separate structure, built from just the key plus a pointer back to the row, kept in sync with every write to the table it serves — and that is exactly why it is not free. Clustered vs secondary 1 rig A table has exactly one physical row order, so exactly one index can match it — the clustered index, whose leaf pages ARE the rows. Every other index on that table is secondary: sorted on its own key, but pointing back at a row it does not physically sit next to. This lesson prices what that pointer actually costs — a point lookup that doubles from one tree traversal to two, and a range scan that can collapse from a sequential read into one random fetch per matching row, past a crossover this lesson computes rather than guesses at. Composite & covering indexes 1 rig A composite index over `(a, b, c)` is one physical structure, sorted by `a`, then by `b` within each `a`, then by `c` within each `(a, b)` — a phone book sorted by (last name, first name), not three indexes glued together. Which queries that single sort order can serve efficiently depends entirely on the ORDER the columns were declared in, not on which columns happen to appear in the query. This lesson computes exactly which of several realistic query shapes a given column order serves cheaply, which it serves at a partial cost, and which it cannot serve at all — plus the separate question of a covering index, which stores enough extra columns to skip the base-table hop `clustered-vs-secondary-indexes` already priced. Index write cost 1 rig `what-is-an-index.mdx` stated the basic rule and stopped there on purpose: N indexes roughly multiply a write by (1 + N). This lesson is the part it deferred — the honest mechanics that make the real number worse than that flat multiplier in one direction and better than it in another, depending on which column actually changed, and depending on whether each index's own write lands as a cheap sequential append or an expensive random one. Partial & functional indexes 1 rig Every index so far in this subsection has covered the whole column, for every row, exactly as written. This lesson is about two refinements that make an index cheaper by covering LESS — deliberately, precisely — rather than more: a partial index that only indexes the rows a WHERE clause names, and a functional index that indexes the result of an expression instead of the raw column. Both buy a smaller, cheaper-to-write index. Both also come with a sharp edge: stray one predicate or one expression off the index's own definition and the database gives that query nothing at all, not partial credit. Selectivity & cardinality 1 rig "The table has an index" and "the query will use it" are not the same fact — a query planner compares two real costs and takes the cheaper one, and past a small, computable share of the table matching, a plain sequential scan wins every time. This lesson names the property that decides it (selectivity: how much of the table a condition matches; cardinality: how many distinct values a column has) and reuses `clustered-vs-secondary-indexes.mjs`'s own crossover to find exactly where that flip happens — not "most rows," a single-digit percent. Reading EXPLAIN plans 1 rig `index-selectivity` computed the crossover a cost-based planner uses to choose between an index scan and a full scan — and that math is never wrong. What it quietly assumed is that the SELECTIVITY fed into it is current. A real optimizer doesn't measure selectivity per query; it reads whatever its last `ANALYZE` recorded, on a fixed schedule, while the table itself can change the instant a bulk update runs. This lesson prices the gap between those two clocks — how long a correct decision keeps running after it stops being correct, and the three-number readout (`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`'s access method, estimated rows, actual rows) that is how you catch it happening. Inverted index 1 rig `what-is-an-index.mdx` already showed what a sorted or hashed structure buys a structured column: a lookup that costs 3 page reads instead of 1,000,000 row-reads. Full-text search needs the same trade against unstructured text, and the structure that buys it has its own name and its own mechanics: the inverted index — the term→documents mapping that flips a document collection's natural document→terms shape, so a search for a word becomes one posting-list lookup instead of a scan of every word of every document. This lesson builds that flip, the merge-walk that answers a multi-term query against it, and the compression a sorted posting list gets almost for free. Bloom filter 1 rig `btree-vs-lsm.mdx` already charges an LSM point lookup `1 + fpr × (runs − 1)` and moves on, treating the false-positive rate as a given constant. This lesson is where that constant stops being asserted and starts being derived — from a bit array of size M and K independent hash functions — and where the one-sided guarantee that makes it safe to use at all (it can never, ever, produce a false negative) gets its own proof and its own number. Skip lists, tries, sparse index 1 rig A B-tree's height is a formula: plug in the row count and the page size, get one deterministic number, every time. Three other structures give that up on purpose, each trading the guarantee away for something else — a skip list trades it for no rebalancing logic at all, a trie trades per-key memory for a lookup cost that never depends on how many keys exist, and an LSM's sparse index trades index completeness for a footprint 1/K the size. This lesson puts a number on each trade, reusing `btree-vs-lsm`'s own cost model rather than re-deriving the B-tree side of it. Dense vs sparse indexes 1 rig `skip-lists-tries-sparse-index.mdx` showed one instance of this: an LSM SSTable's in-memory index holding only every Kth key. This lesson pulls the same property up to where it actually lives — dense versus sparse is a choice available to ANY index over ANY sorted data, B-tree or SSTable alike, and it is only available at all when the underlying data is sorted by the indexed key. This lesson prices the general trade-off: how index storage shrinks with the sparseness factor K, how the resulting local scan grows, and the real sweet spot in between — plus why an unsorted heap table can never use it, stuck at K=1 forever. Geospatial indexes 1 rig Every index this course has built so far assumes a key has one natural sort order: numbers sort by magnitude, strings sort alphabetically, and "nearby" in the sorted order means "nearby" in the data. A pair of coordinates breaks that assumption outright — sorting by latitude alone scatters points that are metres apart in longitude, and vice versa. This lesson surveys the four families that answer "what's nearby" anyway: geohash fakes a 1D key out of 2D space (and pays for it at cell boundaries), quadtree and R-tree give up on a fixed key order entirely, and S2/H3 fix the specific distortion a naive lat/lng grid has near the poles. Join algorithms 1 rig A query planner never has "the" way to compute `A JOIN B` — it has three structurally different algorithms, each with its own cost shape, and it picks whichever one is cheapest for the specific tables in front of it. Nested-loop pays per outer row times an inner cost that a good index can crush to a handful of page reads. Hash join pays once per row on each side, as long as the smaller side's hash table fits in memory. Sort-merge pays to sort whatever isn't already sorted, then walks both sides once. This lesson computes all three costs for the same pair of tables and shows exactly where each one wins — not by rule of thumb, but by the crossover a real cost-based planner is doing the arithmetic for. Scatter-gather 1 rig Any query without the shard key in its WHERE clause can't be answered by one shard — it has to be scattered to every shard in parallel and gathered back by a coordinator. The query's latency is set by the slowest of those N parallel replies, not their average, and the same tail-amplification arithmetic this course has already used for generic fan-out applies here without a single new assumption: more shards touched means a near-certain straggler, even when every shard is almost always fast. Materialized views 1 rig A materialized view precomputes and stores a query's result so a read is a lookup instead of a re-run. The read speedup is never free — someone has to keep the stored result in sync with the base table, and how that maintenance is done changes its cost by two orders of magnitude. This lesson prices full refresh against incremental maintenance, shows exactly which aggregate shapes earn a cheap incremental update and which don't, and puts a number on the staleness a fixed refresh cadence leaves a reader exposed to. Vertical partitioning 1 rig A wide row packs a table's frequently-queried columns and its rarely-touched or oversized ones together, and a row store's unit of I/O is the whole row — so a query that only needs the small hot columns still pays for every byte of the large cold one, on every page it touches. This lesson quantifies that waste, splits the cold column into its own table to fix it, and shows Postgres's TOAST mechanism doing the same separation automatically, per oversized value, with no schema change at all. Lock taxonomy & granularity 1 rig A database enforces "don't let two transactions touch the same data unsafely" at row, page, or table granularity — the same protection, three different price tags. This lesson computes those prices directly: the lock-object overhead and unrelated-row blocking at each granularity for one fixed workload, why a granularity hierarchy needs intent locks to stay cheap to check, why a plain row lock cannot stop a phantom insert into an empty gap, and the exact row-lock count at which an engine gives up on fine-grained locking and blocks a table's worth of transactions at once. Normalization & normal forms 1 rig Normalization is not rule-following for its own sake — it is a disciplined way of arranging a schema so a fact is stored exactly once, which is what makes update, insert, and delete anomalies structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely. This lesson prices those anomalies, walks 1NF through BCNF against one concrete schema apiece, and then reverses course honestly: denormalization is the same trade-off, made on purpose once its cost is actually understood. Schema-on-read vs schema-on-write 1 rig A relational table checks every field against its schema before a write is accepted; a document store or event log accepts whatever arrives and leaves the checking to whoever reads it later. Both are real engineering positions, not one correct answer and one mistake — this lesson prices what each one actually costs: a per-field validation tax paid on every write, a detection delay that is exactly 0 on one side and can run to years on the other, and a parsing tax every reader pays instead of the writer paying it once. It also separates a distinct, often-conflated idea: data locality, which is about where related data physically sits, not when its shape gets checked. Dimensional modelling 1 rig Normalization asks how to arrange a schema so a transaction never corrupts. Dimensional modelling asks a different question of a different workload: how to arrange a schema so an analyst's aggregate query — revenue by category, by quarter, by region — stays fast across millions of rows. This lesson prices the three answers side by side: a star schema (one join hop to any attribute), a snowflake schema (normalize the dimensions too, for less storage and more hops), and one big table (flatten everything, for zero hops and the most storage). Graph query languages 1 rig A relational join can express any FIXED number of hops — "friends of friends" is a 2-way self-join, "friends of friends of friends" is a 3-way one. What it cannot express is "however many hops it takes": a variable-depth traversal has no fixed arity, so no fixed number of JOIN clauses can write it. SQL's late answer is `WITH RECURSIVE`, which runs the same join once per hop and, naively, has to re-check every new row against everything already visited — a check that gets more expensive every additional hop. A purpose-built graph engine skips all of that: every node already stores direct pointers to its neighbors, so following an edge is one O(1) pointer read, full stop. This lesson computes both costs as depth grows and shows exactly why the gap between them widens rather than staying flat. Unique ID generation 1 rig Generating a value that has never been used before sounds like it should be free — call a function, get a number. But that number becomes a primary key, and a primary key's VALUE decides where every insert lands in a B-tree, not just whether it's unique. This lesson prices four real ID schemes — UUIDv4, UUIDv7, ULID, Snowflake — on the two axes that actually differ: how many bytes each one costs to store, and what its value layout does to insert locality, reusing the page-split model this course already built rather than re-deriving it. ACID vs BASE 1 rig ACID is usually taught as four letters to memorize. This lesson prices three of them — atomicity's WAL, isolation's locks-or-retries, durability's fsync — as one per-commit latency number, and is honest about the fourth: consistency isn't a mechanism with a cost at all, and it is not the same word CAP uses, which is the single most common confusion this topic produces. BASE is the other side of the same arithmetic: a distributed system that declines to pay this tax, on purpose. MVCC 1 rig Snapshot isolation says a transaction sees the database as it stood at one fixed moment. This lesson is about the machine that makes that true without a reader ever waiting on a writer: every write creates a new row version instead of overwriting the old one, and a reader picks the version its own snapshot can see. The mechanism is nearly free per read — and it has a bill, paid in versions that pile up for as long as the oldest open snapshot in the system stays open. 2PL, SSI, deterministic execution 1 rig Serializable execution has three real, shipping mechanisms, not one — a database can enforce "runs as if transactions ran one at a time" by making transactions wait (two-phase locking), by letting them run free and aborting one at commit time if it turns out unsafe (serializable snapshot isolation), or by refusing to let it be ambiguous in the first place (deciding the execution order before anything runs). This lesson prices all three the same way — cost per transaction against one shared variable, how often transactions overlap — and finds the contention level where the optimistic bet stops paying off. Deadlocks 1 rig Two transactions each hold a lock the other needs, and each is waiting for the other to release — neither can proceed, and without intervention neither ever will. This lesson computes the three practical answers: detecting the cycle in an actual wait-for graph and aborting a victim, preventing it structurally with a consistent lock order, and what the application owes once a victim is aborted. `lock-taxonomy-and-granularity.mdx` covers what a lock IS; this is what happens once two of them collide. Optimistic vs pessimistic 1 rig Two philosophies answer "what do I do about another transaction that might touch the row I'm about to change" — take a lock first and make it wait, or don't take a lock at all and check for damage when you're done. This lesson is the application/query-level version of that decision: `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` and `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` as pessimistic concurrency's actual SQL, `WHERE id = ? AND version = ?` as optimistic concurrency's actual SQL, and the numbers that say which one to reach for. Long transactions & bloat 1 rig `mvcc.mdx` proved that the reclamation horizon — the age of the oldest open snapshot in the system — sets a floor on dead-version count with no vacuum term in the formula. This lesson turns that floor into bytes: how many pages a table and its indexes actually occupy once a transaction has been held open, the exact write rate past which VACUUM falls permanently behind no matter how long that transaction stays open, and why letting the fixed-width transaction-id counter wrap is not a performance problem but a data-corruption one. Leader–follower replication 1 rig A leader can acknowledge a write the instant it hits its own disk, the instant the fastest follower confirms it, or only once every follower has — and those three choices are one dial, not three features. This lesson puts a number on all three arms of that dial: the latency each one adds, the writes each one risks on a leader crash, and the probability each one simply refuses to write at all. Session consistency guarantees 1 rig `replication-lag.mdx` measured the lag distribution; `leader-follower-replication.mdx` measured what an ack policy costs to shrink it. Neither one asks what a single USER experiences across a sequence of their own reads and writes — that is a session-level question, and it splits into exactly three nameable guarantees. This lesson computes each one: the routing window that makes read-your-writes hold, the probability random routing shows a comment then un-shows it, and why a causally-related pair of writes on separate partitions has a coin-flip chance of appearing backward the instant they're written — not a rare edge case, the DEFAULT case at zero gap. Multi-leader replication 1 rig A single leader has exactly one place a write can land, so two writes to the same key can never disagree about which one happened first — there is only ever one leader to ask. Give every region its own leader instead, and that guarantee is gone: two leaders can each accept a write to the same key before either one hears about the other. This lesson computes how often that happens as a function of write rate and inter-leader delay, what last-write-wins actually costs when it does, and the write availability multi-leader buys during a partition that this new risk is the price of. Leaderless replication 1 rig Give up the leader entirely: any of N replicas can take a write or serve a read, through a coordinator that is a stateless proxy, not a decision-maker. `quorums.mdx` already derives and prices the durability guarantee this buys — R + W > N — so this lesson does not repeat that arithmetic. It builds the rest of the picture: the two mechanisms, read repair and anti-entropy, that keep replicas converging with no leader to force order between them, and what each one costs when it works and when it doesn't. Failover & split brain 1 rig Promoting a follower when the leader goes silent sounds like the easy half of replication — until you have to decide HOW silent counts as dead, WHICH follower to trust, and what happens to the writes the old leader never got to send. Get any of the three wrong and you don't get a slow recovery, you get two leaders that both believe they're the only one, accepting writes that will never agree with each other again. Chain replication 1 rig A third replication topology, next to the leader-follower star and the leaderless quorum: nodes form a straight line, writes enter at the head and walk it node by node, and only the tail's reads are ever served — which buys strong consistency without a single vote. The bill is a write latency that sums every hop instead of racing them in parallel, and this lesson puts the exact gap on that bill as the chain gets longer. Why you shard 1 rig Replication already solved reads — more copies of the whole dataset, spread across machines that each hold everything. It does nothing for a dataset too big for one disk, or writes too fast for one leader. Partitioning is the other axis: each machine holds one copy of a slice of the data, not a copy of all of it, so throughput and storage genuinely scale with partition count. This lesson computes that win and the bill that comes with it — the same scatter-gather tail-latency arithmetic already proved for shards, applied to how much of the linear scaling win a partitioned store actually keeps once some fraction of queries can't stay on one shard. Range vs hash partitioning 1 rig `why-you-shard.mdx` established that splitting data across partitions is worth doing. It did not say HOW a key decides which partition it lands in — and that one decision, made once when the table is created, is close to a one-way door. Range partitioning keeps sorted-order neighbors together, which is exactly what a range query wants and exactly what a monotonically increasing key (a timestamp, an autoincrementing id) turns into a permanent hot partition. Hashing fixes the hot partition and breaks the range query instead — this lesson computes both sides of that trade with the same tools `hot-partitions.mdx` and `scatter-gather.mdx` already built. Rendezvous hashing 1 rig Consistent hashing earns its minimal-disruption property with a ring: sorted points, virtual nodes, a binary search. Rendezvous hashing gets the *same* property — add a node, move only about 1/N of the keys — by scoring every node from scratch on every lookup and keeping whichever scored highest. No ring, no virtual nodes, no shared structure to maintain. The price is that "score every node" really does mean every node, and past about ten of them the ring's binary search is already cheaper. Rebalancing cost 1 rig Consistent hashing, range partitioning and rendezvous hashing all answer the same question — which partitions move when a node joins, leaves or the assignment scheme changes. None of them answer what moving those partitions actually costs while it is happening. This lesson computes that cost: a migration duration set by one node's density, a live-traffic penalty that is not linear in how much bandwidth you give the migration, and a wall past which "go faster" stops being a trade-off and starts being an outage. Partitioned secondary indexes 1 rig Once data is partitioned, a query on the primary key routes to exactly one partition — that's the whole point of `why-you-shard`. A secondary index has no such luck: the row it matches could be on any partition. This lesson prices the two genuine ways to handle that — an index local to each partition, which makes every read a scatter-gather, or an index partitioned by its own indexed value, which makes every cross-partition write pay for a distributed transaction or accept staleness — and computes the exact read:write ratio where one stops beating the other. Cross-shard queries 1 rig Sharding solves storage and write throughput by giving every row exactly one home — but a query or a join that needs rows from more than one shard gets none of that benefit for free. This lesson prices the four ways a team actually resolves that: ask every shard and merge (scatter-gather), choose the partition key so both sides of a join already share a shard (co-location), replicate a small reference table onto every shard (broadcast), or scatter-gather anyway but do the merge outside the query engine (an application-side join) — and shows that three of the four are the identical cost curve wearing different names. Directory-based sharding 1 rig Range partitioning, hash mod N, a consistent-hash ring, rendezvous hashing — every scheme this curriculum has covered so far answers "which shard owns this key" by computing it from the key itself. This lesson covers the one that doesn't: write the answer down in a table, and look it up. That buys a property no formula can match — move any key to any shard for any reason — and it costs a property every formula gets for free: the table itself now has to be found, trusted, and kept up, on every single request. Federation vs sharding 1 rig Six lessons back, this subsection built one mechanism: split one schema's rows across machines by a key, and throughput scales with partition count. That mechanism has been called "partitioning" the whole time — and it is also routinely called "sharding," a term that quietly adds a connotation the general word doesn't: independent database instances, one per shard. Federation is not a third name for the same thing. It splits by FUNCTION — a users database, an orders database, an inventory database, each its own schema — the way microservices split code, not the way this subsection split rows. This lesson untangles the three words with a real decision procedure, then quantifies the practical reason federation and sharding are usually combined rather than compared. CAP & PACELC 1 rig Almost every explanation of CAP treats consistency, availability and partition tolerance as three properties a system picks two of, forever. That is not what the theorem says, and it is not how any real system works — a partition is not a design choice, it is a network failure that happens whether anyone wants it to or not. This lesson states CAP correctly, prices the real choice it forces using `quorums.mdx`'s own R+W>N math, and then adds the trade-off CAP leaves out entirely: PACELC's "else" branch, priced using `leader-follower-replication.mdx`'s own sync/async math — the latency-versus-consistency choice that is live on every write, partition or not. Consistency models lattice 1 rig `session-consistency-guarantees.mdx` named three CLIENT-facing guarantees a session can get from a replicated system. `cap-and-pacelc.mdx` names the SYSTEM-facing trade a partition forces. This lesson is the map connecting them: the full lattice of system-wide consistency models, strongest to weakest — linearizable, sequential, causal, eventual — with each one's real coordination cost computed from three already-built lessons, and the one genuinely load-bearing result: causal consistency is not an arbitrary stop on the way down, it is the provably STRONGEST model that never has to block during a partition. The outbox pattern 1 rig A service writes a row to its own database, then separately publishes a message about it. There is no transaction spanning the two, so a crash between them either loses the message or invents one. At this lesson's checkout-scale operating point that is about 43,240 events lost or invented a day — not a rare accident, a computable rate. The outbox pattern makes both writes land in the same local transaction instead, which drops the loss to exactly zero — and charges for it in publish latency instead: about half a second, mean, at a 1-second poll. CRDTs and operational transform 1 rig `multi-leader-replication.mdx` priced last-write-wins (a coin flip that silently deletes a write) and named application-level merge (domain-specific code, e.g. union two carts) as the better alternative — then pointed forward to CRDTs as "the general-purpose, principled version of exactly that idea." This lesson delivers on that pointer, and puts the other production answer next to it: operational transform, the mechanism behind Google Docs. Both automate merge without a lock and without one write overwriting the other. Neither is free — this lesson computes what each one actually costs. Three-phase commit 1 rig Two-phase commit has exactly one serious flaw: if the coordinator dies after some participants have voted yes but before it sends the final decision, those participants are stuck — locked, waiting, unable to safely guess. Three-phase commit was built to fix precisely that, by adding a phase whose whole point is letting participants finish the job themselves. It works — under an assumption the protocol needs and cannot enforce: that the network stays synchronous enough for a timeout to mean "dead" rather than "partitioned." Under a real partition, the fix can make DIFFERENT groups of participants decide differently on the same transaction, which two-phase commit's design cannot even do. Timeliness vs integrity 1 rig `cap-and-pacelc.mdx` and `consistency-models-lattice.mdx` both use "consistency" the way CAP does — an ordering property — and both flag, without developing it, that the word is doing at least two different jobs. This lesson is that untangling: timeliness (when you see a value, relative to when it was written) and integrity (whether the data is ever corrupted or made nonsensical) are two independent axes, not one dial, and most of what people fear about "eventual consistency" is a timeliness worry wearing an integrity costume. OLTP vs OLAP 1 rig OLTP and OLAP are not two kinds of database — they are two workload shapes, and row-vs-column-storage.mdx's own crossover formula already says which layout wins for either one. This lesson runs that formula on a mix instead of a single query, then asks the honest question the roster demands: does HTAP actually escape the trade-off, or does it just move where you pay for it, and does zero-ETL fix the underlying mismatch or only automate the plumbing between two systems that stay separate. Content-defined chunking 1 rig Content-addressed storage names a chunk by a hash of its own bytes, not by where it sits — so identical content anywhere in a store collapses to one copy, which is the entire mechanism deduplication depends on. The naive way to produce those chunks, splitting a file every fixed N bytes, quietly destroys that mechanism the moment a file is edited: one inserted or deleted byte shifts every later boundary, so no downstream chunk's hash matches what it used to be. This lesson computes exactly how much that costs, what a rolling hash buys back by cutting on content instead of position, and the real CPU and metadata price of that fix. Schema migration, expand-contract 1 rig A live database cannot swap its schema atomically while readers and writers keep running old code — so a safe migration is four separate phases, not one. This lesson prices each one: the backfill's duration as a direct function of batch size, the batch-size lever's other side (a bigger batch finishes sooner and pins more dead row versions per batch while it runs), what a shadow-read verification step buys against a silent backfill bug before it reaches production, and what "drop the old column before every reader migrated" actually costs when a straggler is still reading it. Backward vs forward compatibility 1 rig Backward compatibility means new code can read old data; forward compatibility means old code can read new data — two independent properties, not two names for the same caution. Both matter at once because of one fact neither direction alone explains: data outlives code. A live database holds records from every schema version it has ever written, in shrinking-but-nonzero proportion, while the code reading it is at most one or two versions at a time. This lesson computes how many historical versions a single reader actually has to tolerate, classifies six real schema changes against both properties, and quantifies what a strict "unknown field is an error" parser costs versus a lenient one. Binary schema encodings 1 rig Protobuf, Thrift, Avro and MessagePack are all "binary and schema-based," and that sameness hides three genuinely different mechanisms for the one property they all chase: compact on the wire, and survivable across a schema change. Protobuf and Thrift number every field and never repeat its name. Avro deletes the field markers entirely and makes the reader fetch the writer's exact schema to know how to parse the bytes at all. MessagePack keeps the field names, exactly like JSON, and saves nothing on repetition. This lesson builds an actual byte-size model of the same record under all three mechanisms plus JSON as the non-binary baseline, and reuses this course's own backward/forward-compatibility classifier against Avro's very different resolution rule. SQL vs NoSQL 1 rig "SQL vs NoSQL" is usually argued as a tribe you join, with "scalability" doing the work of a reason. It isn't one axis and there is no winner — there are eight storage families, each built to make one SHAPE of access pattern cheap at the expense of every other shape. This lesson builds the actual decision framework: a scoring model that takes a workload's join complexity, consistency need, write pattern, query flexibility, and schema variability, and computes which family's mechanical cost is lowest — reusing this course's own join-cost and traversal-cost math to show the number behind "relational" or "graph" instead of asserting it. Query-first data modelling 1 rig A relational schema is designed once, normalized, and queried however you like later — a JOIN is always available. Cassandra and DynamoDB remove that option: there is no ad-hoc join, and no efficient WHERE on a non-key attribute. So the design process inverts. You enumerate every access pattern FIRST, then build one denormalized table per pattern, keyed so each one is a single-partition lookup. This lesson prices what that buys on reads, what it costs on writes, and reuses `hot-partitions`' own math to show the one thing this discipline does not exempt you from: choosing a partition key with enough cardinality. Blob storage vs database 1 rig A large or rarely-queried binary payload — an image, a video, a PDF, a big JSON blob, model weights — can live two places: inline in the database row next to the record that owns it, or in cheap object storage with only a pointer left behind. Both are "correct." This lesson prices the difference: what every replica and backup copying that payload for free actually costs per month, what an inline blob does to a scan that never even reads it, and the byte-size threshold past which externalizing stops paying for a fixed extra round trip and starts paying for itself.

Clocks, causality, consensus, fencing — and why "is it dead?" is undecidable.

Two generals & FLP 1 rig Two generals coordinating an attack over messengers who might be captured, and a formal 1985 proof about consensus algorithms, turn out to be the same fact told twice. Neither is a bug waiting for a cleverer protocol — both are proofs that certainty is unreachable over an unreliable, unbounded-delay channel. What's computable is how close you can get: confidence climbs toward 100% with every extra confirmation and never touches it, and a consensus attempt under real delay can only ever be safely stuck, never safely wrong. Failure detection orphan 1 rig Every distributed system asks "is that node dead?" and no system can answer it. Silence is the only evidence you get and both causes produce it, so a timeout is not a measurement but a bet with two ways to lose. What sets the odds is not your network's average delay — it is the shape of its tail, and that is why production timeouts look absurd until you compute them. System models taxonomy 1 rig Two already-shipped lessons in this tier make claims that look contradictory until you notice they are scoped to different worlds. FLP says no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus terminates. Raft guarantees it constantly, in production. Neither is wrong, because neither is talking about the same assumption on message delay. This lesson names the three formal assumptions — synchronous, asynchronous, partial synchrony — that every distributed algorithm's proof is scoped under, classifies six claims already made by this tier, and quantifies what happens to a failure detector's accuracy when the real network stops honoring the bound it was tuned to assume. Raft 1 rig Raft is the practical answer to a proof that says perfect agreement is impossible: it stays provably SAFE at all times and provably LIVE almost all of the time, by picking exactly which guarantee to give up and when. This lesson computes the three numbers that make that trade concrete — how often an unrandomized election timeout collides versus a randomized one, how long a committed entry actually takes to commit as a function of cluster size, and how long a cluster is unavailable after a leader crashes. Total order broadcast 1 rig Every node applies the same sequence of writes in the same order: that single guarantee is total order broadcast, and it turns out to be exactly as hard as consensus itself — solve either one and the other falls out for free. This lesson names the primitive `raft.mdx` already built without naming it, shows the equivalence as two small concrete constructions rather than a claim to take on faith, and computes the one number that follows from serializing every write through a single agreed sequence: a throughput ceiling nothing about cluster size can buy back. Byzantine faults 1 rig Every consensus lesson so far — Raft, quorums, failure detection — assumes a node either works correctly or stops. Byzantine faults break that assumption: the node is up, reachable, and answering, and it is lying, possibly a different lie to each peer. This lesson computes exactly how much bigger a cluster has to get to survive that (3f+1 nodes, not 2f+1) and exactly why that specific size forces any two quorums to still share an HONEST node — plus the honest reason most systems never need any of this. Per-key ordering 1 rig `total-order-broadcast.mdx` priced the expensive, general answer to "what order does everyone see messages in": serialize every message through one agreed sequence, and pay a throughput ceiling nothing about cluster size buys back. Almost no production queue actually does that. Kafka, Kinesis, SQS FIFO, and Pub/Sub ordering keys all make a narrower, cheaper promise — messages sharing a key arrive in order, full stop, with no promise at all across keys — and this lesson computes why that narrower promise is normally exactly enough, what it costs when it isn't, and what fixing that costs in the parallelism given up. Safety vs liveness 1 rig Every correctness claim this tier has made so far — "never two leaders," "eventually a leader is elected," "3PC can decide wrong under partition" — is one of exactly two kinds of promise, and the two kinds fail in opposite ways. This lesson names them, builds a function that classifies six claims already made by four shipped lessons, and reuses raft.mjs's own election simulation to compute the split directly: the safety invariant holds on every one of thousands of simulated trials with zero violations, while an adversarial timing pattern delays the liveness half of the identical mechanism for exactly as many attempts as it is given. Fencing tokens orphan 1 rig A distributed lock cannot stop a stale writer, because the thing enforcing the lock and the thing receiving the write are different systems and only one of them knows a lock exists. The corruption rate is one line of arithmetic — mean pause over lease TTL — and at ordinary settings it is one bad write every ten seconds. The fix is not a better lock. It is moving the check to where the write lands. Locks, leases & Redlock orphan 1 rig Redlock asks a majority of independent Redis nodes to agree on a lock instead of trusting one — a real fix for one problem, a single point of failure. It is not a fix for the other problem, and the arithmetic shows why: a client that pauses past its lease resumes with no way to know time passed, and no number of Redis nodes it consulted beforehand changes that, because nobody re-checks it. A lease is exactly a lock with a clock built in, and the same clock is the whole of the argument. Barriers & semaphores 1 rig A mutual-exclusion lock has exactly one holder; a barrier and a semaphore are the other two shapes built from the same clocks and quorums. A barrier is a rendezvous — nobody proceeds until everybody arrives — and that "everybody" is why it is the one distributed pattern that gets WORSE as N grows: one crash and it wedges forever, unless a failure detector bounds the wait. A semaphore is a lock generalised from one holder to K, and the number worth computing is how fast its wait time collapses once K passes the arrival rate's real concurrency need. Object storage as coordination 1 rig A conditional PUT — write this key only if it doesn't already exist, or only if the ETag still matches — is a compare-and-swap on an object key. That single primitive is enough for leader election, distributed locking, and job claiming, built entirely on a store nobody runs or patches, because whoever's conditional PUT lands first simply wins. It inherits the same clock-dependency fragility a TTL-based Redis lock has, and it costs an order of magnitude more per attempt than a dedicated coordination service — which is exactly why it belongs to infrequent coordination and nowhere near a busy per-request lock. Gossip & membership 1 rig No node broadcasts to the whole cluster and no coordinator keeps the authoritative member list — instead every node just tells a few random peers what it knows, once per round, the way a rumor spreads through a crowd. This lesson computes what that buys and what it costs: how few rounds it actually takes to reach nearly every node (it grows with log N, not N), why the very last node always takes longer than the smooth curve promises, and the bandwidth a cluster pays every round for having no one place to ask instead. Service discovery 1 rig Service A needs to call service B, and B runs as multiple, constantly-changing instances — scaling up, scaling down, crashing and getting replaced, each on an IP address that didn't exist an hour ago. A needs a CURRENT, WORKING instance of B, and there are exactly two places that "current" can be decided: in the calling client, or in a component the client always talks to instead. This lesson prices both, prices the registry both of them depend on, and computes the one number that makes "current" a lie: how often a client gets routed to an instance that has already crashed but the registry hasn't heard about it yet. Leader election 1 rig `raft.mdx` already elects a leader — randomized timeouts, term numbers, a majority vote — but that mechanism is welded to a consensus protocol that also replicates a log. Leader election is also a standalone problem, older than Raft and solvable without consensus at all: Bully lets the highest surviving id always win, at a message cost that can be quadratic in cluster size; Ring gets the same answer with a message cost that never moves off linear; a lease turns the whole question into one conditional write, at the price of trusting a clock. This lesson computes all three, and the one number that actually explains why production systems avoid Bully: how a single flaky failure-detector signal reruns its worst case for free. Paxos and why Raft won 1 rig Paxos was proven correct first, published first, and is still older than Raft by over a decade — and Raft is what production systems actually run. This lesson computes why, and the answer is not speed: once a Multi-Paxos leader is stable, its steady-state commit latency is the identical formula as Raft's, not merely close to it. What Paxos never specified was HOW to get a stable leader in the first place, and that gap — filled a different, incompatible way by every real implementation — is the actual reason adoption went to the algorithm with one unambiguous spec instead of the one proven first. Zab and Viewstamped Replication 1 rig Two more consensus/replication protocols, neither of which needs Raft's mechanism re-explained: Viewstamped Replication (1988) is Raft's closer structural ancestor than classical Paxos ever was, and Zab (ZooKeeper's own protocol) adds one guarantee neither Raft's nor VR's spec names — FIFO client order. This lesson draws the family tree all four protocols actually belong to, shows their steady-state cost is the identical formula once a leader exists, and computes what breaks, concretely, without Zab's extra guarantee. Clocks and skew orphan 1 rig A wall-clock reading is an interval, not a point, and last-write-wins compares two of them as though they were exact. The rate at which that discards data is not set by how good your clocks are — it is set by the ratio of clock skew to the gap between writes, so the same clocks are fine for a cold key and catastrophic for a contended one. And the losses are directional: whichever machine runs fast wins every conflict. Logical clocks orphan 1 rig Replacing wall clocks with logical ones fixes the reply-before-the-message bug completely and exactly. It does not fix conflict detection, and almost every explanation implies it does — a Lamport timestamp assigns an order to pairs that have none, and those invented orderings are indistinguishable from real ones. Half of them, typically. Detecting concurrency needs a vector, a vector costs one counter per participant, and that cost is why the modern answer is a hybrid. Idempotency orphan 1 rig A retry can duplicate an effect three independent ways, and an idempotency key as usually built stops the one nobody was losing money to. The rate of the worst one is set by the client's timeout, not by anything on your server — at a one-second timeout against a two-hundred millisecond endpoint, 3.6% of requests retry while the original is still running. Two of the three guards are exact. The third can only ever be a bet. Delivery semantics 1 rig There are exactly three answers to "the sender is in doubt whether a message got through — what does it do?", and vendor marketing has invented a fourth name for one of them. This lesson computes what each real answer costs: at-most-once loses messages at exactly the underlying failure rate, at-least-once trades that loss for a genuine wire-level duplicate rate, and "effectively-once" is at-least-once's identical wire-level duplicate rate run through `idempotency.mjs`'s own guards — a mechanism that changes what a duplicate does, never whether one was sent. The end-to-end argument 1 rig TCP has a checksum. So does the disk. So, usually, does whatever's in between. Every one of those checks can pass, on every hop, while a bit that flips somewhere none of them was watching rides all the way to the application untouched. This lesson computes why that residual risk COMPOUNDS the more hops a lower-layer check has to cover, why one end-to-end check — run once, by the endpoint that actually needs the data right — collapses it back down by orders of magnitude instead, and why the honest verdict from the 1984 paper that named this isn't "rip the lower-layer check out": at 64 hops, removing TCP's own retransmission (kept purely as a performance optimization) costs 327× more bytes moved for a file that arrives exactly as correct either way. Consistent snapshots 1 rig Ask every node in a distributed system "what's your state right now?" and combine the answers, and you can get a global state that never existed — a message recorded as received on one side and never recorded as sent on the other. Chandy and Lamport's 1985 algorithm fixes this with one extra message type and zero coordination: a MARKER that rides the same channels application traffic already uses, and turns out to be enough to carve a provably consistent cut through a system that never stopped moving. Distributed tracing 1 rig A single click can trigger calls to eight different services, and no one service's logs show the whole picture — a trace is what stitches those eight views back into one. This lesson computes the two costs that come from actually running tracing at scale: how much storage a sampling rate costs as a direct function of traffic, and the probability that rate would even have caught the one rare bug you needed it for — then contrasts deciding what to keep at the START of a trace against deciding at the END, which is cheap to decide and expensive to hold. Sharded counters 1 rig `hot-partitions.mdx` named the fix for a write-hot single row and moved on: split the key into subkeys, write to a random one, sum on read. This lesson is that fix, worked out as a number. `why-you-shard.mjs`'s own linear throughput law gives the write side; `scatter-gather.mjs`'s own order-statistic law gives the read side; the two combine into one exact break-even ratio — and a G-Counter CRDT turns out to already be doing the read side's arithmetic, for a different reason. Coordination avoidance 1 rig This course has already built four expensive ways to coordinate — locks (`lock-taxonomy-and-granularity`), consensus (`raft`), synchronous replication (`leader-follower-replication`'s sync mode), and serializable transactions (`2pl-ssi-deterministic-execution`) — and one way to skip it (`crdts-and-operational-transform`). This lesson names the actual decision procedure that tells you which one an operation needs: Bailis et al.'s invariant confluence, a real test with a real answer, not a judgment call. It applies that test to four concrete operations, and prices the gap between the two answers using numbers this course already computed. Txn vs. eventual consistency 1 rig This course has already built every mechanism this decision routes to: sagas (`saga-vs-transaction`), 2PC/3PC (`three-phase-commit`), the CAP/PACELC availability trade (`cap-and-pacelc`), and the actual test for which operations need coordination at all (`coordination-avoidance`). This lesson does not re-derive any of that. It puts the two options side by side — a distributed transaction's all-nodes-up requirement against eventual consistency's single-node requirement — quantifies the gap in both latency and availability, and then applies coordination avoidance's own classifier to four realistic operations to show that half of them never needed a distributed transaction in the first place. Determinism as a design tool 1 rig A replicated log, a CRDT merge function, and this course's own seeded test suite all lean on the identical property: feed the same input to two independent processes and they produce byte-for-byte the same output, no matter when or in what order they run. That property has a name — determinism — and once it holds, replication and recovery stop needing coordination at every step; they only need to agree on the ORDER of the input, once. This lesson names the tool directly, prices what skipping it costs in coordination overhead, and points at the seeded `rng` already threaded through every model in this repo as a live example rather than a hypothetical. Stateful operators 1 rig Some stream operators only ever need the event in front of them — a filter, a map — and a crash costs them nothing, because there was nothing to remember. MapReduce and its legacy 1 rig Dean and Ghemawat's 2004 paper gave large-scale data processing a shape simple enough to teach in three verbs: map transforms each record on its own, shuffle regroups every result by key across the whole cluster, reduce folds every value sharing a key into an answer. Idempotent, replayable pipelines 1 rig `idempotency.mjs` already prices what one non-idempotent operation costs when a retry duplicates it. `determinism-as-design-tool.mjs` already prices what one non-deterministic operation costs when a replica replays it. `queue-vs-log.mjs` already prices whether a source can hand back history at all. Lambda vs. kappa 1 rig A pipeline that needs a fast, approximate answer right now AND an eventually-correct exact answer has two classic architectural answers. Kappa architecture runs only ONE stream pipeline, and answers "reprocess the past" by replaying the same log through the same logic instead of maintaining a second implementation. This lesson computes what each answer actually costs: lambda's bill is a fixed engineering premium for keeping two implementations of the same logic in sync as it evolves, modeled as a duplicate-implementation cost plus an expected rate of "drift defects" only two independent copies can produce. Kappa's bill is different in kind — it pays nothing extra to implement the logic once, but its "just replay it" answer only works when the log's retention still reaches back far enough AND the stream engine can replay that much history inside whatever window still makes the result useful, reusing `append-only-log-mechanics.mjs`'s retention math directly for the bytes a reprocessing job has to redo. The shuffle primitive 1 rig Between map and reduce sits the step every treatment of MapReduce names and prices only in passing: shuffle takes every mapper's output, sends each key to the one reducer holding its partition, and turns "process this dataset" into a fully-connected mapper-to-reducer exchange. This has nothing to do with *shuffle sharding* -- a load-balancing technique for isolating tenants across a shard pool, covered in `shuffle-sharding.mdx` -- beyond the word itself; this lesson's shuffle is a distributed sort. Three costs follow from that. Every mapper talking to every reducer means the transfer count scales as M times R even though the total bytes moved does not -- a cost axis most treatments never separate from data volume. A reducer's partition that outgrows its memory spills sorted runs to disk and pays to merge them back. Serving derived data from batch 1 rig `mapreduce-and-its-legacy.mdx` already covers what happens INSIDE a batch job; `cqrs.mdx` already covers splitting a read model from a write model in general, kept in sync by an incremental projector that updates on every write. Standing queries and CEP 1 rig A database stores DATA persistently and treats QUERIES as transient: a query arrives, gets matched against data that's already indexed for fast lookup, and is forgotten the moment it returns an answer. A STANDING QUERY — the mechanism underneath complex event processing (CEP), streaming-SQL continuous queries, and saved-search alerts like "notify me when a new listing matches my search" — inverts that completely: the query is registered once and persists indefinitely, while each event flows through once and is gone. Checking a new event against every registered query, one by one, is the naive move, and it doesn't survive contact with scale. The fix is to index the queries themselves, the way a database indexes its data — but how much that buys depends entirely on how selective the average registered query's filter actually is. Open table formats 1 rig `etl-vs-elt.mdx` used "data lake" as a place raw files sit, waiting to be shaped; DDIA's own "Table format" section names Apache Iceberg and Databricks's Delta format as the thing that turns that pile of files into something a query engine can treat as a real table -- but only names time travel, GC, and transactions as features, not how any of them work. Lineage & Catalogs 1 rig Two questions every pipeline in this section has been quietly assuming an answer to: "if I change or drop this column, what breaks downstream" and "where does this table's PII actually live." This is not `isolation-tiers`'s boundary — that lesson prices the container-vs-microVM boundary around untrusted CODE; this one prices the boundary around independent, mutually-untrusting tenants' DATA, and the two are easy to conflate precisely because they rhyme. `relationship-authorization` prices the per-request check that is supposed to stop a query from reaching another tenant's row; this lesson prices what the blast radius actually is on the day that check has a bug, and the answer turns out to depend entirely on which of these three tiers the row lived in. `database-per-service` already priced this exact lever — pooling versus partitioning, blast radius versus a cost that rises with count — on the service-boundary axis, and `multi-tenancy-noisy-neighbours` already prices what a quota or fair-share scheduling buys inside one shared pool; both are reused here unchanged, on the tenant-boundary axis, next to the one genuinely new number: a migration-and-instance bill that grows in a straight line with tenant count and eventually pushes teams back down the ladder they climbed. Second factors & passwordless 1 rig authn-vs-authz.mdx settles what a second factor is FOR — proving who is asking, not deciding what they may do — and this lesson does not re-litigate that split. password-hashing.mdx prices what happens after a password database is stolen; this lesson never touches a database, because a live relay-phishing attack steals a credential at the moment of use, before it is ever hashed or stored anywhere. What follows prices four "second factors" against that live attack and finds three of them cost a phishing-kit operator the identical price to defeat as no second factor at all — then prices the one that doesn't, and exactly why not. Network isolation layers 1 rig `ip-and-nat.mdx`'s `subnetPlan` already prices what a subnet split costs in addresses and how a CGNAT port block is sized — this lesson takes a VPC's host topology as given and asks a question ip-and-nat never asks: once an attacker has landed on one host inside it, how many of the others can they actually reach, as a function of which security boundary is real rather than left at its cloud-provider default. `zero-trust-and-mtls.mdx` already prices blast radius one layer up, at the service-mesh identity layer — an authorization policy's allowed out-degree between application identities; this lesson is the layer below that, where "compromised" means an attacker can open a TCP connection at all, before any application identity is even asked. Security groups (stateful, per instance) and NACLs (stateless, per subnet, both directions independently) are two enforcement points on the identical question — which hosts can this packet reach — and a WAF is a third, unrelated question: not which hosts a request reaches, but whether the payload it carries is allowed to. All three ship with a permissive default a team has to actively replace. Data residency and sovereignty 1 rig `why-you-shard.mdx` prices what a partition key buys — throughput that scales linearly in partition count — and what a query pays when it can't stay on one partition. It never asks what field the key is built from, because for pure load balance any high-cardinality field spreads writes just as well as any other. This lesson prices the field load balance is indifferent to but the law is not: which jurisdiction a row's data must physically remain in. It does not re-derive why-you-shard's throughput or scatter-gather math — it prices a different axis of the same key choice, and names the real cross-border-transfer mechanisms (GDPR's adequacy decisions and Standard Contractual Clauses, PIPL's security-assessment and standard-contract routes) without pretending to be legal advice. EU AI Act as design constraint 1 rig `audit-logging-tamper-evidence.mdx` already prices two properties of a security-relevant log: that a sampled record can't be trusted to survive, and that a stored record can't be trusted to be unedited. This lesson does not re-derive either — it prices a third, independent constraint Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 adds on top of both, for a "high-risk" AI system (Annex III — e.g. recruitment screening or remote biometric identification): Article 12(1) anchors a log's required survival to the system's own remaining time in production, not to a fixed number of days a storage-cost calculation was free to choose once. Article 19(1) and Article 26(6) then floor that duty at six months for provider and deployer alike, each bounded by "the extent such logs are under their control." And Article 50 is not a logging requirement at all — a system can satisfy every article above in full and still owe an end user a disclosure it never made. Doing the right thing 1 rig `audit-logging-tamper-evidence.mdx` already prices whether a compliance log's mandated records survive a sampling policy; this lesson reuses that exact probability, unchanged, for a different completeness question — whether the decision trail a bias investigation needs (which model version, which patrol allocation, which training snapshot) survived being logged like routine telemetry. `pii-classification-tokenisation.mdx` already prices the asymmetric cost of a classifier missing a sensitive field against over-flagging an ordinary one; this lesson reuses that exact cost function, unchanged, on a different kind of field — a proxy for a legally protected trait (a postal code, an IP address) that is not personally identifying at all but needs the identical classification discipline DDIA names directly. What neither sibling models, and what this lesson does, is the one mechanism DDIA's own final chapter argues from without pricing: a predictive system's output becoming the next model's training data, and how fast that compounds a bias small enough to look like rounding error.

TIER 8 Architecture and patterns 28/38

Monolith to services, decomposition, sagas, outbox, and the distributed monolith.

When to split orphan 1 rig The decision to split one process into several is made almost entirely with adjectives, and it goes wrong for a structural reason: the costs of splitting are measurable and the benefits mostly are not. So the costs get computed, the benefits get asserted, and whoever speaks last wins. This lesson computes the costs precisely — availability, latency, amplification — and then states the benefits honestly, which means admitting that exactly one of the four usual ones produces a number. By capability vs by subdomain 1 rig `when-to-split` answers whether and when to split a process into services. This lesson assumes that question is settled and asks the one it deliberately left alone: once you're splitting, how do you draw the lines? The two dominant answers — organize around what the business DOES (ordering, payments, shipping) or organize around a DDD subdomain (a concept with its own model, wherever that cuts) — only disagree where a concept is shared by more than one capability. That disagreement is not a matter of taste. It is a bipartite graph with a number on each side: cut through the concept and duplicate it, and risk silent drift; cut around it and centralize it, and pay in the runtime availability `when-to-split` already priced, one hop per caller. Bounded contexts & DDD 1 rig "Customer" means a billing account with a payment method and a dunning state inside Billing, and a person with a sentiment and a ticket history inside Support. Both models are correct. Neither is correct outside its own boundary, and the word is spelled identically in both, which is the entire trap — nothing in the code flags the difference. A bounded context is the boundary inside which one model is valid; this lesson prices what happens at the seam between two of them, where letting one context read the other's raw representation of a shared term costs something very different from routing that read through one explicit translation. The distributed monolith orphan 1 rig "You have a distributed monolith" is used as an insult, which is why nobody agrees they have one. It is a measurement, and the measurement is not about runtime — it is how many services must ship together for an average change. If that number is above one you are paying for k services and collecting the independence of k divided by it. Splitting further makes it worse. Database per service 1 rig Database per service buys two things a shared database structurally cannot give you: a schema each service can migrate on its own clock, and a failure that stays inside one service instead of taking the whole platform with it. Both are real, and neither is free. A shared database's one connection pool absorbs every service's bursts with slack another service is not using at that instant — split that exact capacity into per-service reservations and blocking rises by two orders of magnitude at eight services, for no more total capacity than before. A cross-service ACID transaction, free while everyone lives in one database, becomes a saga or a CQRS read model the moment they do not. This lesson prices both sides with closed forms this course already built for pooled resources and fleet-wide failure rates, and turns up a third cost nobody quotes: the rate at which the shared instance breaks AT ALL rises with every service attached to it, with no change to any one service's own reliability. Saga vs transaction orphan 1 rig A saga replaces one atomic transaction with a sequence of local ones plus an undo for each. The guarantee it offers is real and it is not atomicity: either everything committed, or a prefix committed and was undone. Read that for what it does not say. The intermediate states are visible — that is the whole point, because releasing locks is what makes them visible — and some undos are not unreliable but impossible. Gateway vs. BFF vs. federation 1 rig A single "API gateway" gets asked to do two different jobs, and conflating them is why teams argue past each other about it. The first job is CROSS-CUTTING: auth, rate limiting, routing, TLS termination — concerns every client needs, better implemented once at one choke point than duplicated in every backend service (`distributed-rate-limiting.mdx` already prices WHERE in the topology that choke point should sit; this lesson does not re-derive it). The second job is SHAPING a response for one specific client — a mobile screen wants one small denormalized payload, a web client wants to compose several resources itself, and a single generic endpoint serving both ends up wrong for one of them. A backend-for-frontend (BFF) answers the shaping job with one dedicated backend PER client; a federated GraphQL gateway answers it with one shared schema composed across every backend service instead. Both cost something real, in different currencies. A BFF's cost is engineering effort that scales with how many distinct client shapes exist — N independently maintained implementations of the same composition logic. A federated schema's cost is schema-composition overhead that scales with how many backend services exist (a pairwise conflict check on every schema change) plus a query-planning tax that scales with how deep and how branchy a composed query gets — the exact N+1 problem `graphql-n-plus-one.mdx` already diagnosed, paid one layer up the stack. This lesson computes both currencies from first principles and finds the one number that actually decides between the first two: at a fixed service count and schema-change rate, exactly how many client shapes it takes before hand-rolled BFFs cost more, in raw annual maintenance alone, than one shared schema. Strangler fig and ACL 1 rig A strangler fig migration is not a rewrite strategy, it is a SCHEDULE — the same fractionOnNew(t) idea `deployment-strategies` already priced for a rollout, stretched from seconds to months. The anti-corruption layer is what makes that schedule safe to run: the boundary that lets a bounded slice of legacy entities move without coupling the new system's model to the old one. This lesson prices the schedule itself and lands on an uncomfortable finding — going slower shrinks the size of any ONE bad step, but not the expected cost of a defect across the whole migration, which does not move at all. The reason to go slow is bounding a peak, never lowering an average. Sidecar, ambassador, adapter 1 rig Sidecar, ambassador, and adapter are one mechanism — a second process on the same host as the main container — doing three different jobs, and the job's name does not decide the cost: whether the main container can proceed without it does. This lesson computes the real overhead of that hop (a rung between an in-process call and a cross-host one this course hadn't named yet), the availability it compounds when it can't be skipped, and the redeploy exposure it removes when it's used to keep N inconsistent interfaces out of the main container entirely. Event-driven architecture 1 rig `queue-vs-log.mdx` settled what a broker keeps and `pub-sub-vs-point-to-point.mdx` settled how many consumers receive each message. This lesson assumes both are settled and asks what changes about the SYSTEM once it is actually built this way: a producer that publishes and does not call anyone, consumers that react and never talk back. The benefit is real and countable — a producer publishing to a topic needs zero code changes as consumers are added, ever, where a direct call needs one integration and one redeploy per consumer. The cost is the same decoupling turned around: a synchronous call chain is trivially traceable, because a call stack (or one propagated trace context) carries causal history structurally, in one place, whatever its depth. An event chain has no call stack — each hop is a separate publish into a separate log, and nothing forces a consumer to copy a correlation id into whatever it publishes next. This lesson computes exactly what that costs: how many independent logs a human must correlate to reconstruct one causal chain (linear in the chain's depth, against a flat 1 for a direct call), how fast the odds of that reconstruction succeeding automatically decay with depth, and what it costs to rebuild the synchronous answer request/response gave you for free. Orchestration vs choreography 1 rig Orchestration puts one coordinator in charge of a multi-step workflow. Choreography lets every service react to events from the others, with nobody in charge. Both can implement the exact same saga — this is the coordination-STYLE axis, orthogonal to `saga-vs-transaction`'s atomicity axis, and a saga can be built either way with identical guarantees. The concrete difference is what a coordination-relevant change costs: under orchestration it is at most two edits, no matter how large the workflow gets, because the sequencing logic takes no such input. Under choreography it is the same geometric fan-out that makes a schema change ripple through chained pipeline stages — and the two styles cost the same only in the single-listener case that a real system never stays in for long. Layered, hexagonal, clean 1 rig This tier's other lessons draw boundaries between services. This one draws a boundary inside a single one, and it is a boundary between the same two things every time: a business rule, and the infrastructure that rule needs to run. Layered architecture never answers what a rule is allowed to depend on, so in practice it depends on whatever concrete class sits below it. Hexagonal architecture answers it once — a rule may depend only on interfaces it owns — and clean architecture is that same answer, named and given a ring number. What is usually sold as "better design" turns out to be exactly one measurable thing: how much of the tree beneath a rule has to be built, or faked, before a test can touch it. Peer-to-peer vs. client-server 1 rig Client-server puts one participant in charge of state AND authority: every client trusts the server, depends on it, and the server's operator decides what is and is not allowed. That single point of control is, identically, a single point of failure and a hard capacity ceiling that does not move no matter how many clients show up. Peer-to-peer removes that one participant: an operation now needs a QUORUM of peers rather than one server being up, which buys denial-resistance that keeps climbing with every peer added — and, under full replication, buys almost no extra throughput at all, because the quorum a peer must clear grows exactly as fast as the fleet that could clear it. Thick vs. thin client is the orthogonal question this lesson does not compute: not who has to agree, but where the logic runs. Offline-first and sync engines 1 rig A sync engine treats "no network" as a normal state, not an error: writes land in a local store first, are acknowledged immediately, and a background process reconciles that store with the server whenever a connection happens to exist. Going offline for a while is really two different questions bundled into one word. The first — did the SAME record get edited on both sides while neither could see the other? — is a conflict-resolution question this lesson deliberately does not answer; a sibling lesson owns the merge mechanism in full. The second — given the client was offline for T seconds, how much of a full state reconciliation does the engine actually have to do? — is this lesson's whole scope, and it has a real, closed-form, and non-obvious answer: an offline device is simultaneously a producer (its own write queue) and a delayed consumer (everything it hasn't pulled yet), and which of three real strategies is cheapest — replay every operation, pull each touched record's current state once, or give up and re-download everything — flips as a function of offline duration, at a point this lesson computes rather than guesses. Idempotent consumer 1 rig `idempotency.mdx` already builds the HTTP half of this: a client-generated key, inserted atomically with the effect, sized by a TTL because the store only ever holds one row per key. A message consumer reading off a broker is not that shape. At-least-once delivery means a redelivery can arrive with no key attached at all — it is the same message id, again, on its own schedule — and the consumer has to remember every id it has seen across a WHOLE WINDOW at once, not one row per request. That is a cost that scales with throughput times window, not with how long one row lives, and it is why this is its own lesson rather than three more lines in idempotency.mdx. Three mechanisms answer "have I seen this," at three different prices: remember every id (exact, but the window is a bet), remember one offset per partition (exact forever, if and only if delivery is strictly ordered), or approximate the set with a Bloom filter (smaller, but it now drops real messages too). Replica vs. API composition 1 rig A product page needs Catalog's name, Pricing's price, Inventory's stock count and Reviews' rating — four services, four databases, no shared join. There are exactly two honest ways to answer that one read. API composition queries all four live, in parallel, and merges the answers at the gateway: always consistent with each service's current state, because there is no second copy of the data to go stale, but its latency is the MAXIMUM of four different services' own tails and a slow or down dependency means a partial answer, not a clean failure. A command-side replica pre-joins the same four services' data ahead of time, off the write path — `cqrs.mdx`'s read model, fed by four sources instead of one — into one queryable store: a read is a flat lookup regardless of how many services fed it, paid for with `cqrs.mdx`'s own staleness. This lesson prices both sides in the same units and reconciles them against one latency SLA and one staleness tolerance, including the state worth being able to name: neither pattern clears the bar, and the fix is fewer/faster dependencies or a faster projector, not a pattern choice. Microservice chassis 1 rig Every new service has to answer the same four boring questions — how does it log, how does it expose a health check, how does it load its config, how does it emit metrics and traces — before it does anything domain-specific at all. `health-checks.mdx` already priced getting the health check right; `structured-logging-and-sampling.mdx` and `metrics-logs-traces.mdx` already priced logging and observability's own storage mechanics. None of them asked the fleet-wide question: what does it cost when EVERY service answers those four questions on its own, against one shared chassis every service depends on instead? A chassis is not a free win — it trades an N-copies cost for a different one, a version-propagation tax on every dependent — and this lesson computes both sides and the fleet size where the trade actually pays off. Contract testing 1 rig Integration testing across N independently-deployed services has an honest default: verify against everyone, because nobody has a machine-checked map of who actually calls whom. That default is the full N-choose-2 mesh, and it is quadratic in N. Skipping it instead means a real consumer-provider pair can drift silently incompatible, and nothing catches it before production. Contract testing is a third option: a consumer's expectations of a provider, checked against the provider's actual behaviour, independently, with neither side's process running — priced against the real dependency graph, which is linear in N. This lesson prices all three regimes on the same axis and turns up the one number teams rarely quote: the shared "everything up" environment a full integration run needs is itself far less reliable than the one provider a contract check needs. Conway's law 1 rig `the-distributed-monolith` measured a change touching f functions scattering across k misaligned services: k(1 − (1−1/k)^f) of them, rising with k rather than falling. That formula never used the word "service" for anything except which bucket a change lands in — so run it one layer up the org chart and it prices something else entirely: how many TEAMS a change drags in, when service ownership is scattered across teams instead of drawn around what changes together. Splitting the org into more, smaller teams fails the identical way splitting into more services does. And team boundaries add a cost service boundaries alone do not: a request that lands on another team's backlog waits for that team's own cadence before anyone even starts it. That is calendar time, not effort, and it is the second half of this lesson. Dual-write, shadow read, cutover 1 rig "Migration strategy" is not one technique, it is three, and only one of them is this lesson's own math. Dual-write — writing the same record into the old system and the new one — is not a new hazard: it is `dualwrite.mjs`'s database-plus-broker partial-failure model, reused unchanged, with "old system" and "new system" written where that file says "database" and "broker". Gradual cutover — shifting an increasing share of READ traffic — is not new either: it IS `strangler-fig-and-acl.mjs`'s fractionOnNew(t) schedule, cited rather than re-derived. What is actually new is shadow read: reading from both systems but serving only the old one's answer, comparing the two for validation before any cutover begins. This lesson prices how much of that comparing is enough — and shows that the number it produces is exactly the input the strangler fig's own cost model has been assuming rather than measuring. Build vs buy vs managed 1 rig Build, buy, and managed are usually argued in adjectives — "more control", "faster to ship", "less to maintain" — and adjectives don't compose, so whoever states theirs most confidently wins the meeting. This lesson prices the one part of the decision that DOES have a closed form: not which mode is cheapest today, but how fast today's cheap choice turns into tomorrow's expensive one. Every month you integrate deeper with a vendor, the cost of leaving grows; the cost of having built the thing yourself does not. There is always a month where those two cross, and it is a specific, computable month — not a vibe about "lock-in." Tier 9's `build-vs-buy-dollars` prices this same decision in dollars and net present value; this lesson prices it in a currency that shows up before the invoice does — engineer-weeks you would spend extracting yourself, and how many months of ordinary usage it takes to spend more of them than building from scratch would have cost. Durable execution as a primitive 1 rig `saga-vs-transaction.mdx` already prices what a hand-rolled saga costs at runtime — a state space that is 3n wide and a stuck rate that is quadratic in step count, f·g·n(n−1)/2 — and `idempotent-replayable-pipelines.mdx` already cites Temporal's own documented determinism constraint (no direct clock reads, no unseeded randomness, no unordered map iteration) as one example of its own replay-safety classifier. Neither prices what it costs, in lines of code, to BUILD the recovery machinery in the first place. This lesson does: a hand-rolled saga step needs one compensating action, written and independently tested; a durable-execution step needs that one determinism constraint, respected, because the runtime — not the developer — reconstructs the workflow's state by replaying its event history. The two totals turn out to be exactly proportional to step count, so their ratio never changes with workflow length even though the saga's own operational stuck rate, one layer up, keeps getting worse. Actor model & distributed actors 1 rig `event-driven-architecture.mdx` already prices what changes once components talk by publishing messages instead of calling each other directly — zero producer coupling, a reconstruction cost that grows with hop count. This lesson does not re-derive any of that; it names the one specific, addressable-identity shape async messaging takes inside the actor model — mail sent to one actor's identity, not broadcast to a topic, drained by exactly one thread that never runs two messages for that identity at once — and prices the two things that are specific to it. That single-threaded mailbox is exactly an M/M/1 queue with one server, so its depth and wait blow up on the same hyperbola every queueing system does as arrival rate nears the actor's own ceiling. And because no other actor can ever be touching this one's memory, a crash costs a bounded, isolated restart — where the identical fault loose in shared mutable state costs a blind window's worth of every request that arrived before anyone noticed. Unbundling the database 1 rig `database-per-service.mdx` unbundles a platform by SERVICE ownership boundary — one team's data in one instance, so a migration or an outage stays inside one team's blast radius. This lesson unbundles by ACCESS PATTERN instead, potentially fanning ONE service's own data out to many specialized stores — a search index, a cache, a materialized view, a data warehouse — each wired to that one service's change stream by the CDC mechanism `cdc-and-log-tailing.mjs` already built, not re-derived here. What is new: pricing the two ways a database can serve K different access patterns — maintain K internal structures itself, synchronously, and pay in write latency (`index-write-cost.mjs`'s own write-amplification math, reused unchanged), or maintain none of them and pay in each store's own CDC-propagation freshness lag instead. Neither `materialized-views.mjs`'s incremental-maintenance cost nor `oltp-vs-olap.mdx`'s row-vs-column argument for why a warehouse doesn't belong on the primary is repeated here; both are cited for what they already settled. Designing around dataflow 1 rig `event-driven-architecture.mdx` already prices what publish/react costs to RECONSTRUCT — how many independent logs a causal chain scatters across, and how fast automatic reconstruction's odds decay with hops. This lesson assumes that same publish/react decoupling as the transport and asks a different question: what happens once the APPLICATION itself is built as a chain of derived views reacting to it, rather than as handlers that query current state on demand — the shift Kleppmann calls turning the database inside out, applied here one layer up from where `unbundling-the-database.mdx` already applies the identical idea to storage engines. The concrete consequence: a value's end-to-end staleness, from a producer's write to the last derived view a consumer actually reads, is the SUM of every stage's own lag — linear in how many stages the value crosses, not the p^hops decay `event-driven-architecture.mdx` derives for a different question entirely. `command-side-replica-and-api-composition.mdx`'s own alternative — fan several sources into ONE materialized store instead of chaining derived views — is cited for its already-priced staleness math and its already-stated fact that composition itself carries none, not repeated here. And the two failure shapes differ in kind, not just in size: a downed request/response dependency returns an answer no caller can mistake for data; a stale derived view returns a row that looks exactly like a fresh one, because nothing in a materialized view's shape carries a flag saying otherwise. Loosely interpreted constraints 1 rig DDIA states the choice directly: an airline can run a synchronous, cross-channel capacity check before confirming every booking — a distributed uniqueness/capacity check, coordinated across every sales channel that can sell a seat — or it can sell optimistically against a local, possibly-stale view, oversell on purpose, and bump the rare excess passenger with compensation. The instinct is to price that choice as "coordination is expensive versus an occasional apology." This lesson prices both sides in dollars and shows that instinct is aimed at the wrong number: the coordination tax is a rounding error. What actually makes strict enforcement expensive is the revenue a "never violate the constraint" policy guarantees it forfeits. Golden paths & platform teams 1 rig `microservice-chassis.mdx` priced ONE infra decision — the shared service template — and found a shared copy cheaper past a fleet-size threshold. This lesson prices the organizational bet one level up: a platform team absorbing an entire CATALOG of infra decisions at once, where the chassis is a single row. The real total without a platform is services × decisions-per-service; the real counter-cost with one is a platform team's own headcount, paid whether or not a single service adopts what it ships; and the real risk is that a paved road which does not fit an edge case sends a team back to building it themselves anyway — so the organization can end up paying twice. This is the organizational/economic case. It does not re-derive the chassis's own build-vs-adopt math, which stays exactly where that lesson left it. Cloud-native as an input 1 rig `when-to-split.mdx` prices three costs of splitting — availability, latency, capacity — and never asks what it costs, in operational engineering, to actually hit the per-service availability its own `perServiceRequired` demands. This lesson prices exactly that and only that, reusing `decomposition.mjs`'s functions unchanged rather than inventing a parallel model: a service's real incident profile (a crashed container, an overloaded fleet, a dead database primary — each timed from a sibling lesson's own number) against the downtime budget composition allows at a given k. A cloud-native platform absorbs three of those four incident classes automatically and cuts expected downtime by three quarters — and it moves the operationally affordable split depth from 0 to 2 services at this lesson's defaults, not to the 9 a "we have Kubernetes now" argument assumes, while leaving `serialAvailability`, `maxChainDepth`, and `splitBreakEvenRatio` completely untouched.

TIER 9 Cost, capacity, economics 13/13

Cost per million orphan 1 rig Every system-design resource sizes a system in requests per second and bytes, and then stops — which leaves engineers optimising the one term that is a twentieth of the bill. Decompose a million requests into dollars and the dominant term is almost never compute, the thresholds are ratios of prices rather than prices, and the cheapest fleet is the one with the worst tail. Storage cost tiers orphan 1 rig Every pricing page shows one ladder: storage falls by four orders of magnitude from RAM to deep archive, and the obvious move is to climb down it. The page does not show the second ladder hiding under the first — the price of touching a byte, which rises just as many orders of magnitude in the opposite direction — or that a per-object fee and a minimum commitment can make the climb lose money before a single byte is ever read again. Cross-AZ traffic orphan 1 rig Availability zones exist so that one datacentre failing does not take you down, and the bill for that is invisible in the architecture diagram — because the diagram draws a replica as a box and the charge is on the arrows between them. Replication, the line everyone blames, is 1.5% of it. Cost of replication factor orphan 1 rig "RF=3 costs 3x and buys 3x the safety" is two separate wrong sentences glued together. Storage is the one line where the first half is exactly right. The bill's other line — cross-zone replica traffic — is capped by the zone count, not the replication factor, and durability isn't priced in dollars at all: it rises fast per copy and then hits a ceiling no copy count can cross. Cache economics orphan 1 rig A cache pays when its hit rate clears a break-even set by the ratio of two costs — and that break-even rises as you make the cache bigger. Hit rate grows like a logarithm, rent grows like a straight line, so the size that maximises savings sits an order of magnitude below the size that maximises hit rate. Past a certain point a 94.7% hit rate saves exactly nothing. Egress costs orphan 1 rig Almost every line on a cloud bill is priced per unit of work — an instance-hour, a request, an operation. Egress is priced per byte, and response size appears in no capacity model, no load test and no autoscaler. Worse, the same byte is billed on four or five boundaries on its way out, only one of which has the word egress on it. And the fix everyone reaches for — a CDN — turns out to be a price arbitrage that clears its cost at a 23% hit ratio, or cannot clear it at any hit ratio at all, depending entirely on your response size. Cost attribution orphan 1 rig [Egress costs](/concepts/egress-costs) prices a $31,035-a-month transfer bill and ends on a warning: none of that arithmetic is executable without dividing the bill by a team. This lesson is that division, priced. Showback and chargeback are not the same mechanism at two prices — one moves a real number on a real budget and the other does not, and that difference is worth six times as much incentive per dollar correctly attributed. Building the pipeline that can defend a bill line costs real engineering hours regardless of mechanism, and on the bill above, chargeback justifies building it down to the tenant while showback only ever justifies the free tier — anything finer loses money, no matter how cheap cloud storage and query pricing get, because the number that decides it is an incentive nobody's budget will produce. Read/write asymmetry orphan 1 rig A write unit covers 1 KB. A read unit covers 4 KB, or 8 KB if you can live with eventual consistency — so per byte a write costs four to eight times a read, and asking for strong consistency doubles the read bill exactly. Then the units are rounded up per operation, which moves the bill off your total bytes and onto your item size distribution. The mean is the wrong statistic, and the right one is computable before you build. Provisioned vs spot orphan 1 rig Three prices for identical hardware, and only one of them is a cost. Preemptible capacity is 60-90% off and can be taken back with a couple of minutes' notice, so its real cost is its price times the wall clock it wastes — and the discount it has to beat turns out to equal that waste exactly. The waste has a minimum at a checkpoint interval of sqrt(2c/λ), which nobody computes. The committed half is one line and nobody computes that either: a 40% discount needs 60% utilisation. Reserved capacity lock-in 1 rig Provisioned-vs-spot's break-even is exact and it is also silent about time: a discount d beats on demand above utilisation 1 − d, full stop, no term length in it. But a commitment is signed once and a workload's shape does not hold still for one to three years — it gets re-architected, downsized, or moved to a new instance family, and none of that un-signs the term. Model the time until that happens as a single number, a mean lifetime, and the deeper 60%-off three-year discount that needs only 40% utilisation turns out to be exposed to three times the term length a shift can land inside — so there is a real band of workload lifetimes where the shallower, safer one-year commitment wins and the deeper one already loses. Cost of observability 1 rig `metrics-logs-traces.mdx` and `structured-logging-and-sampling.mdx` already own WHAT drives observability's cost: cardinality, log volume, and sampling rate. This lesson's only job is PRICING those levers, in real dollars, on two named vendors' published 2026 rate cards — and the fourth lever neither tier-6 lesson prices at all: how many days you keep it, which moves a gigabyte-billed vendor and an event-billed vendor by completely different amounts. Build vs buy, in dollars 1 rig Tier 8's `build-vs-buy-vs-managed` prices this same three-way decision in engineer-weeks of switching cost against a flat rebuild figure — one crossover, because a rising line only ever meets a flat one once. This lesson prices it in dollars of total cost of ownership, and the finding is that the three modes are not shaped alike, so the crossover is not one month, it is a sequence: build is a big cost on day one plus a shallow slope, buy is a flat per-seat line that never had a day-one cost to begin with, and managed is priced per unit of the thing that actually grows when the product succeeds — which makes its bill a parabola, not a line. A line with an intercept, a line through the origin, and a parabola through the origin cross each other in a specific order, and which option is cheapest changes twice before month 30, in a worked example built entirely from real, dated seat and usage prices. Cost-driven redesign orphan 1 rig Every other lesson in this tier holds scale fixed and varies something else — a hit rate, a utilisation, a retention window, an item size — and finds its own break-even along that one axis. This lesson holds all of those fixed and varies the one thing none of them do: how much traffic the system actually sees. Two architectures each have their own dollar curve as a function of traffic, the curves are straight lines, and two straight lines cross at most once. On one side of that point an architecture is the right call; on the other side, with nothing about correctness, data model, or team size having moved, it is the wrong one. The trigger for the redesign is the crossing, not a defect.

LLM serving, KV cache, HNSW, RAG, GPU economics. Zero coverage in any existing repo.

Prefill vs decode orphan 1 rig One API call, two workloads with opposite bottlenecks. Reading the prompt is compute-bound and emitting tokens is bandwidth-bound, and at a batch of one the second uses a third of one percent of the chip you paid for. Almost every technique in LLM serving is one move along one axis, and the axis is not the one on the datasheet. KV cache orphan 1 rig Once memory rather than compute decides how many users a card serves, the serving engine is a memory allocator — and the obvious allocator throws away 90% of the cache without leaking a byte. Not a tuning problem: a consequence of reserving space for a length nobody knows yet. Ten times the memory buys 59 users. Changing the allocator buys 61 on the hardware you already have. GPU memory orphan 1 rig llm-inference-phases prices the weights term and kv-cache prices the KV-cache term's allocator behaviour — 320 KB per token for the shape both files use, quoted directly here rather than re-derived. Neither says where the rest of an 80 GB card actually goes before either term gets a byte; in both files that gap is a single unexplained guess, `reserveGB = 2`. This lesson opens that guess into the two real terms inside it — a slice of the card no engine ever offers at all, and one activation tensor large enough to matter — and finds that the "room for two" both siblings quote for a 70B model at FP8 on one H100 was already too generous. Continuous batching orphan 1 rig llm-inference-phases already fixed the cost of one decode step: it reads every weight in the model once, for the whole batch, regardless of how many of that batch's slots are still generating anything. This lesson is about who gets to hold those slots, and for how long, when replies finish at wildly different lengths — a scheduling decision, not a memory or arithmetic one. It leans on kv-cache's distinction between a contiguous reserve-to-maximum allocation and paged, per-slot memory without re-deriving either, and treats gpu-memory-arithmetic's account of what a card's memory leaves for concurrent sequences as already settled. TTFT vs tokens/sec orphan 1 rig llm-inference-phases derives why prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound; this lesson does not re-derive that roofline, it asks what a human does with the two durations it produces. A streamed reply is felt as two separate numbers at two separate moments — the dead air before the first token, and whether the text keeps flowing once it starts — and they answer to different levers: a cached prefix can only move the first, a lower weight precision can only move the second, exactly and provably rather than just usually. A benchmark that quotes one number while silent on the other has told you half of what you paid for. Speculative decoding orphan 1 rig A small draft model proposes several tokens; the large target model checks all of them in one batched pass and keeps whatever variable-length prefix survives. This lesson does not re-derive why that pass is nearly free below the ridge point — llm-inference-phases.mdx already showed that a weight read serving many positions at once costs the same wall-clock time as serving one — it spends that fact on a lever batching cannot touch: more tokens per weight-read without adding a second user or losing a bit of precision. What it prices instead: the exact expectation for tokens produced per verification round as a function of acceptance rate and speculation depth, the GPU-seconds spent computing logits for tokens nobody keeps, and the VRAM a resident second model claims on the very card kv-cache.mdx already spent down to a handful of gigabytes. Quantisation orphan 1 rig gpu-memory-arithmetic already prices a card as weights, plus a KV cache that grows with concurrency and context, plus a fixed reserve — this lesson does not re-derive that formula, it only shrinks the weights term and asks what the shrinking costs. On one 80 GB H100, a 70B model's weights alone do not fit at FP16, fit with room for two 8,000-token sequences at FP8, and sixteen at INT4 — the identical formula, three bit counts. What that formula has no opinion on is price: OPT-175B's own published WikiText2 perplexity moves 0.4% at four bits done with a calibrated method and 14.6% at the identical four bits done by naive rounding, and at three bits the naive version does not merely get worse — its LAMBADA accuracy is reported as exactly zero. Quantisation is neither the free compression trick it is often sold as nor a fixed tax either way: it is a design decision whose price is set far more by the method than by the bit count. Prefill/decode disaggregation orphan 1 rig llm-inference-phases established that prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound in the same request, and stopped there. This lesson asks the deployment question that observation forces: if both phases run on one shared pool of GPUs — the naive thing to build, and the only thing continuous-in-flight-batching's own scheduler needs to make work at all — a newly arrived prompt's prefill queues behind every decode iteration already in flight, because the scheduler protects tokens already streaming to a connected user. Splitting the phases onto two independently-sized pools removes that queue entirely, at the cost of a GPU-equivalent that neither pool would need alone, and a KV-cache transfer over the network between them. GPU scheduling orphan 1 rig gpu-memory-arithmetic prices how much of a GPU a job's weights and KV cache need. This lesson starts one level up, from a job that has already been sized, and asks which physical GPUs a scheduler hands it and when — a question with two independent, compounding answers. One is timing: a job needing N GPUs *simultaneously* cannot start on N − 1, which turns "is the cluster busy" into a combinatorial question rather than a linear one. The other is geography: once running, a synchronous job's throughput is set by its slowest network hop, not its average one, so the placement decision that ended the wait can also be the one that halves the throughput. GPU autoscaling orphan 1 rig A GPU replica's "boot time" is not a container starting — it is a 70 GB, or 405 GB, file landing on a card, and this lesson prices that transfer at a real bandwidth instead of treating it as a config knob. It reuses `autoscaling.mdx`'s stacked-lag and M/M/1-backlog machinery unchanged rather than re-deriving it, and adds only what a GPU changes: a boot term large enough to dominate the whole stack, and a scale-up threshold that — against a traffic ramp rather than an instant step — decides how much backlog has already built before that fixed lag even starts counting. The payoff is the comparison the stateless-web version never needed: pricing idle, pre-warmed replicas against the exact backlog a reactive scale-up leaves behind. Inference gateway tier 2 rigs api-gateway-and-bff settled that a choke point should exist and priced centralising a cross-cutting policy at it. This lesson is about the second gateway tier that appears once the thing behind the choke point is a model server, and about the single fact that makes it a different tier rather than the same one with new plugins: a request is no longer a unit of work. Measured on production agentic traffic, two turns that count identically to a requests-per-minute limiter differ by 43.1x in the tokens a limiter would charge, and by 45.2x in prompt tokens alone — which is the term prefill FLOPs are linear in. That one fact breaks four things simultaneously. It breaks rate limiting, because the quantity you want to limit is not knowable until generation ends — so every real token limiter reserves an estimate and reconciles later, and LiteLLM issue 18730 measured that hole at 6.6x overshoot. It breaks load balancing, because the replicas are not interchangeable: one card holds this prompt's KV blocks and the others do not, which is the difference between a 0.6-second and a 4.3-second first token. It breaks cost attribution, because a cached prefix read costs a tenth of an input token, so the bill is not linear in tokens. And it breaks cost-quality model routing, because switching model throws the prefix cache away — above a computable 81.0% cached prefix, routing DOWN to a model 3.3x cheaper on both input and output costs MORE per turn, and production agentic traffic sits at 95.4%. Embeddings & similarity orphan 2 rigs Every later lesson in this tier — ivf-pq, hnsw (already shipped), vector-db-vs-pgvector, hybrid-search, rag-end-to-end — assumes "distance between two vectors" is a settled question and gets straight to indexing it. It is settled, but not the way a 2022-era tutorial states it: cosine similarity and dot product are the same ranking only after normalizing, dimensionality is now a runtime dial rather than a fixed model property, and "more dimensions" has a real, measurable cost in exactly the corpus this repo's other retrieval lessons already index. IVF + PQ triangle orphan 1 rig hnsw already prices one point in the ANN design space: a graph index, graded by recall@k against exact search. This lesson prices the other point — partition-then-compress — and the reason it is a genuinely different shape, not a worse version of the same one: hnsw has one dial (efSearch). This family has two, and they trade against DIFFERENT costs. nprobe trades recall against latency. Compression bits trade recall against memory. Neither dial can buy what the other one buys, which is why "just compress harder" cannot fix a latency problem and "just probe more lists" cannot fix a memory problem. HNSW orphan 1 rig Exact nearest-neighbour search is slow because it reads every vector, not because it multiplies them — a 10M × 768 float32 scan moves 30.7 GB and does 15.4 GFLOP, so it is memory-bound by 10× and more compute cannot help. HNSW escapes it by being wrong on purpose. The amount it is wrong by is called recall, it is a measurable number, and a latency figure without it specifies nothing: the fastest possible index returns random results instantly. Embedding drift & migration orphan 1 rig embeddings-and-vector-similarity settled what a vector IS; this lesson is about what happens the day the model that PRODUCES vectors changes. An embedding-model upgrade is not a schema change — it moves the vector space itself, so a query embedded by the new model has no meaningful similarity to a document still indexed under the old one. The same shape as schema-migration-expand-contract, but the two states are not compatible in the interim the way two schema versions of the same table usually are. pgvector vs. vector DB orphan 1 rig hnsw.mdx priced what a graph index costs; embeddings-and-vector-similarity priced what a vector costs. Neither says WHERE to run either. As of 2025-2026 that choice is no longer "pick a vector database" by default — native vector types now ship inside Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB, Oracle and every major search engine, and the real fork is a RAM number, not a vendor preference. Hybrid search (BM25 + vector) orphan 2 rigs embeddings-and-vector-similarity gave dense search a way to find paraphrases; BM25 still finds exact keyword matches dense search misses entirely. Running both and combining their rankings is the current default — but the two retrievers' scores are not comparable numbers, and how a system fuses them despite that is the actual design decision, still unsettled in 2025-2026. Multi-stage retrieval & ranking orphan 2 rigs hybrid-search-bm25-and-vector fused two retrievers into one ranked list. This is the general shape that ranked list sits inside — feeds, search boxes, ad systems and RAG all run some version of candidate generation, then a cheap coarse pass, then an expensive final reranker — and the question this lesson prices is how wide each stage has to be before the next one can actually help. RAG end to end orphan 1 rig multi-stage-retrieval-and-ranking priced the funnel's widths; this is one query's actual trip through it — chunk, retrieve, rerank, assemble, generate. Semantic caching orphan 1 rig Embed the incoming query, find the nearest cached query, serve its answer if similarity clears a threshold. The mechanism is genuinely simple and genuinely useful. What gets misrepresented is every number attached to it — the hit rate, the safety of a single threshold, and whether a similarity score can detect the failure that actually bites in production. MCP's specific contribution is assembling every ingredient in one context window by design — and as of the 2026 spec, the attacks are named, numbered, and in several cases already disclosed with CVEs, not hypothetical. Guardrails & prompt injection orphan 1 rig OWASP has ranked prompt injection the #1 LLM application risk for three consecutive years, and in December 2025 OpenAI said publicly that it may never be fully solved — comparing it to phishing, a threat managed in layers rather than eliminated by one control. Evals as your test suite orphan 1 rig Calling evals "your test suite" is right about the role and misleading about the mechanics. A unit test is deterministic: it passes or it fails, and a passing test means something. An eval set is a finite sample, so whether it can detect a regression at all is a question of statistical power — and most eval sets are far too small to make the claim their CI gate implies. Training-serving skew orphan 1 rig Training-serving skew is two different failures wearing one metric decline. Staleness degrades smoothly and is fixed by freshness; a diverged transform costs a fixed penalty even at perfect freshness and is fixed only by collapsing two code paths into one. Buying a feature store helps exactly insofar as it forces the second thing. Distributed training orphan 2 rigs gpu-scheduling-gang-and-topology-aware priced getting a large gang of GPUs and placing it well; gpu-memory-arithmetic priced fitting the model on them. This lesson prices the thing that decides whether a months-long run finishes: interruptions. Meta measured 466 job interruptions across 16,384 H100s in 54 days — roughly one every 2.78 hours, sustained, with 419 of them unexpected and 78% of those hardware-attributable — and still reported over 90% effective training time. Those two numbers are not compatible with synchronous checkpointing at any interval. Reaching the second while living with the first needs two independent changes at once, and this lesson is about proving which two. Dataframes and arrays orphan 1 rig Relational, document and graph are three data models that answer the same shape of question: find the records matching a predicate. A dataframe, a matrix and an n-dimensional array answer a different one — apply one numeric operation to every element at once — and they pay for it in a different currency. The currency is which byte sits next to which byte. Get the layout wrong and an aggregate scan moves eight times the bytes it needs; get it right and the point lookup you also have to serve pays exactly the same eight times in the other direction. Neither is a tuning problem. Both are the same 64-byte cache line, read two ways.
