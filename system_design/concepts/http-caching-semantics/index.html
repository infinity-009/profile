Tier 1 · The network HTTP caching semantics

Seventy-six percent of the responses were 304s, and the page had not got one millisecond faster

A 304 Not Modified is a request. It costs a whole round trip, plus the entire connection handshake if the connection is cold, and it removes only the body — so on a small resource revalidation saves half the bytes and 0.2% of the time. The strategy that wins does not answer the freshness question faster; it renames the file so there is nothing to ask.

Read first — connection-setup-costbyte-budgets

01

The dashboard got 175 kB lighter per session and stayed exactly as slow

An internal dashboard loads 40 files — scripts, stylesheets, fonts, a handful of JSON endpoints. The team is in Amsterdam; the origin is in California, a 150 ms round trip away. People open the dashboard about five times in a working session, roughly five minutes apart, and between two of those opens somebody usually deploys, so about 2 of the 40 files are genuinely different by the time the page reloads.

It felt slow, so somebody added ETags and Cache-Control: max-age=0, must-revalidate to everything. The results looked excellent. Origin egress fell. The access log filled up with 304s — 152 of the 200 requests in a session, or 76%. Somebody put a graph of the 304 rate on the wall.

Load time did not move. Not by a little: by nothing measurable at all.

Here is the session, priced. Same 40 files, same five visits, HTTP/1.1 with the browser’s usual six connections per host, and bodies averaging 1,024 bytes.

no caching at all200355.25 s433.6 kB

max-age=600120213.15 s172.7 kB

Read the second row against the first. Revalidation removed 175.0 kB and zero round trips. It could not remove a round trip, because a 304 is a round trip. And read the second row against the last: renaming the files removes 154.6 kB more and 24 round trips, which at 150 ms each is 3.60 seconds of a five-visit session.

The 304 rate on the wall was a real measurement of the wrong quantity. A cache that answers questions cheaply and a cache that does not need to ask are not two settings of the same dial. One of them is on the critical path 100% of the time.

02

Add an ETag, watch the bytes fall and the clock stay put

The mental model almost everyone starts with is that a cache has one setting — how aggressive it is — and that an ETag makes the cache “smarter”: it still checks, but the check is cheap because no body comes back. Being told “nothing changed” sounds like a cache hit with a small confirmation cost.

It is not a cache hit. It is a miss with a small response. The client has to open its mouth and wait for an answer, and that wait is the whole cost of an HTTP request over any real distance.

Predict An API returns a 1,024-byte JSON body with an ETag and `max-age=0, must-revalidate`. The client is 150 ms away on a cold connection — TCP plus TLS 1.3 — on a 10 Mbps link. The ETag matches, so the origin answers 304 Not Modified. What fraction of the response time did the cache just save?
A conditional request is a request. The round trips are unchanged: TCP's handshake, TLS 1.3's handshake, and the request going out and the first byte coming back — three round trips, 450 ms, paid identically whether the answer is 304 or 200. The only thing the 304 removes is bytes on the wire, and at 10 Mbps 1,024 bytes of body plus 307 bytes of response headers, less the 180 bytes a 304 still carries, is 0.9208 ms out of 451.06 ms — 0.204%. The bytes saving is real and large (53.09%); the latency saving rounds to nothing. Computed in src/sim/httpcache.mjs from the round-trip counts in src/sim/handshake.mjs, which are read off the RFC message-flow figures rather than typed.
Show it without answering

Four things to do with it, in this order:

  1. Read the top band as it opens. 450 ms for the 304, 451 ms for the full fetch, and 0 ms for the fresh hit. The instrument the reader expected — a cheap check — is drawn against the right baseline for the first time.
  2. Press warm connection, then drag distance down to 0.5 ms. Now the 304 saves 58.84% of the response time, and the pitch was correct all along. (Both moves are needed: at 0.5 ms on the cold TLS 1.3 stack the handshake is still three round trips and the saving is only 35.90%.) Conditional requests are a datacentre-scale optimisation being applied to an internet-scale problem. This is where the intuition came from and where it is true.
  3. Drag body size right. At 562.6 kB the 304 finally saves half the response time. Below about 5.6 kB it saves under 1%. There is a body size at which revalidation is the right answer, and it is not the size of a JSON response.
  4. Now look at the lower band and drag files changed. Set it to 0 — nothing was deployed — and must-revalidate still spends 200 requests and 5.25 s asking about 40 files that are all identical, while the immutable build spends 48 requests once and never asks again.
03

The intuition: the phone call and the label on the tin

You keep a tin of paint in the shed and you want to know whether it is the colour currently on the wall.

Option one: phone the decorator. They answer in a second, and the answer is “yes, same colour.” Cheap answer, and you still spent the phone call. If the decorator is on the other side of the world, the phone call is most of your afternoon and the brevity of the answer is irrelevant. This is a conditional request. Notice what makes it expensive: not the length of the reply, but the fact that you had to wait for one.

Option two: trust the tin for a month. No phone call at all. Occasionally you paint a wall the wrong colour, and you have decided in advance how wrong you are willing to be. This is max-age, and it is the only option here that removes the call.

Option three: write the colour on the tin. Now the tin’s name is “Hague Blue 30”. If the decorator switches to a different colour, that is a different tin, with a different name, and you would not have gone looking in the shed for it. There is no question to ask, because a tin cannot be out of date about what it is.

Option three is what a content-hashed filename does — app.4f21c8e9.js instead of app.js — and it is the only one of the three that is never both wrong and silent.

“How do I make asking cheaper?” and “how do I not have to ask?” are different questions, and the second one is usually available. The header that configures all of this makes them look like alternatives on one dial, which is most of why this goes wrong.

04

The mechanism: one header, four questions, and a round trip that never gets cheaper

Cache-Control is four settings wearing one name

Every directive below is in the same comma-separated list, which is what makes them look comparable. They are not: they answer different questions, they fail differently, and two of them are not about freshness at all.

QuestionDirectivesWhat it decidesCost of getting it wrong
Freshness — may I use this without asking?max-age, s-maxage, immutablewhether a request happensstale answers, or requests you did not need
Validation — is what I hold still current?no-cache, must-revalidatehow big the response isa round trip you pay for and cannot feel
Scope — who may hold it?private, publicwhich caches may store itone user’s data served to another
Blocking — who waits for the answer?stale-while-revalidate, stale-if-errorwhether the reader is on the critical patha tail latency you thought was a cache hit
(none of the above)no-storewhether anything is written downnothing cached, and nothing revalidatable

Two name traps worth memorising, because both have caused real incidents in both directions. no-cache does not mean “do not cache”: RFC 9111 §5.2.2.4 makes it “store this, and never reuse it without revalidating first”, which requires a stored copy — it is a validation directive. no-store is the one that means what no-cache looks like it means, and it is a confidentiality directive: it also destroys your ability to send a 304 at all, because there is nothing left to compare against.

What a 304 is actually made of

RFC 9110 §15.4.5 requires a 304 to carry the fields it would have sent in a 200 for Cache-Control, Content-Location, Date, ETag, Expires and Vary. So it is not a bare status line. Counting one ordinary browser request to a JSON API on HTTP/1.1 — a session cookie, a bearer token, the usual client hints — with lines.join('\r\n') framing per RFC 9112 §2.1:

PartBytes
the request’s header block, including If-None-Match837
a 200’s response headers307
a 304’s response headers180
the whole conditional exchange1,017
the body it declined to send1,024

The exchange that avoids sending 1,024 bytes costs 1,017 bytes. Asking weighs 99.3% of what answering would have — and the reason is not the 304. It is the request: at 837 bytes the client’s own headers are 2.7× the size of the 200’s response headers, because cookies and a bearer token go out on every single ask. HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 fix exactly this and only this: under HPACK (RFC 7541 §2.3) an unchanged field is one indexed byte, so the same request block’s best case is 45 bytes. Which changes the byte arithmetic completely and the round-trip arithmetic not at all.

Where the time goes

revalidation latency = (setup round trips + 1) × RTT + (headers ÷ link rate)

The round-trip counts are not this lesson’s invention: they are summed from the RFC message-flow figures in src/sim/handshake.mjs. Warm keep-alive is 1. Cold TCP + TLS 1.3 is 3. Cold TCP + TLS 1.2 is 4. A 304 and a 200 have identical counts, because the status code is decided after the request arrives.

Freshness works on a completely different quantity

A max-age does not make a request cheaper. It deletes requests, and the arithmetic is a renewal cycle: a copy is fetched, served for T seconds, expires, and the next arrival misses. With Poisson reads at rate r, the expected requests per cycle are 1 + rT, so:

hit ratio = rT ÷ (1 + rT) · origin fetches per second = 1 ÷ (T + 1/r)

Derived once in src/sim/cache.mjs for the CDN lesson and imported here rather than rewritten, so the two cannot drift. Note where the read rate appears: only as 1/r, an additive term next to T.

Multiply the second expression by the number of private caches and you have the origin’s whole load, because private caches do not share with each other. And because r only appears as 1/r, the whole thing has a ceiling that traffic cannot move:

origin request rate ≤ caches ÷ max-age

A million browsers holding a 60-second copy cannot generate more than 16,667 origin requests per second, however hard anyone reads. This is the number that belongs in a capacity plan, because it does not move when traffic does.

A million caches at max-age=60, sweeping the per-client read rate over four decades:

Ten thousand times the traffic, from 10 k reads a second to 100 M, moves origin load by a factor of 2.67 — and the last decade of that, a 10× traffic increase, moves it by 0.15%. Origin load is a function of how many caches exist and how long each holds, not of how hard anybody reads.

The heuristic nobody configured

If you send no freshness information at all, RFC 9111 §4.2.2 permits a cache to invent some, and the widely implemented rule is a fraction of the time since Last-Modified — conventionally 10%, capped by nothing. Two opposite surprises fall out, and both are things people discover in production:

  • A document last edited a year ago is heuristically fresh for 36.5 days. You cannot fix it by pushing a change, because pushing a change is what makes it stop being cached.
  • A document edited a minute ago is fresh for 6 seconds. So a hot object arrives at the origin at almost its full read rate for exactly as long as it is interesting.

The Cache-Control header does not know how far away the client is, and the decision depends entirely on that.

Bandwidth makes the body cheap to send and leaves the round trips untouched, so on a fast link revalidation is worth less, not more. The same conclusion the connection-setup lesson reaches about handshakes, in a different costume.

And the fifth column nobody prints: who else is holding this

private versus public is the one directive on the page that is not a performance setting. Put a personalised response into a shared cache and the arithmetic is brutal, because the two factors that decide how much leaks are exactly the two you were trying to maximise. The fraction of responses served from someone else’s copy is hit ratio × (1 − 1/users).

One accidentally-public account summary, 5,000 users, one request each per 50 seconds, max-age=60:

QuantityValue
aggregate read rate100 requests/s
cache hit ratio99.983%
responses served from the wrong user’s copy99.963%
in the first hour359,868 of 360,000
mean time from deploy to the first leak10.0 ms
distinct users served one single cached fill3,493

Ten milliseconds. There is no monitoring window short enough to catch this before it has happened, and no rollback fast enough to make it not have happened. A private cache with the same hit ratio — above 99% — leaks exactly zero, at any traffic level, forever. That is what makes this a boundary rather than a knob: every other directive on this page trades latency against staleness, and this one trades nothing at all.

Vary, which divides the cache key rather than the cache

RFC 9111 §4.1: a stored response only matches if the request’s values for every header named in Vary match. So the key is the URL crossed with those header values, and each dimension divides every key’s arrival rate. The number it divides by is not the count of distinct values — it is the inverse Simpson index, 1/Σpᵢ², the number of equally likely values that would collide as often:

And note the last row in both directions: Vary: User-Agent nominally has thousands of values and behaves like fifty, which sounds like a reprieve and is not, because fifty is still enough to multiply origin load by fifty.

06

The trade-off: everything you can do about this costs you either freshness or a URL scheme

The lesson so far reads as “stop revalidating, use long max-age and content-hashed URLs.” Here is the bill for each of those, honestly.

A long max-age buys request removal with staleness you cannot revoke. There is no supported way to reach into a browser cache and invalidate an entry. Once you have sent max-age=86400, a million clients are entitled to ignore you for a day, and the only remedy is to change the URL — which is exactly the immutable strategy, arrived at the hard way, mid-incident. This is what the max-age=600 row in slot 1 is actually paying for: it is the only row that serves a superseded file, on 2 of the 5 visits, and it is still slower and bigger than the content-hashed build — 120 requests and 3.15 s against 48 and 1.65 s. The freshness lifetime that bought the request saving is the same lifetime you cannot take back.

Content-hashed URLs cost you a build step and one un-hashed document. Somebody must generate the hashes, rewrite every reference, and keep old versions on the origin long enough for in-flight pages to finish loading. And the HTML that references the hashed assets cannot itself be hashed — some document has to be the mutable entry point. So immutable caching does not remove revalidation; it concentrates all of it into one small resource, which is the correct place for it and is a design decision rather than a header.

stale-while-revalidate buys latency with staleness, and — this is the part worth being exact about — it buys no work at all. RFC 5861 §3 permits a cache to serve a stale copy immediately and refresh in the background for S seconds past expiry. Measured in the simulation, one client reading a max-age=30 resource every 5 seconds, 150 ms from the origin:

The first two columns are identical to every digit printed, and that is not a rounding artefact — the test asserts it at exactly zero tolerance. The request that triggers the refetch is the same request at the same instant whether it waits for the answer or not, so the origin does precisely the same work. What stale-while-revalidate changes is which of the two things happens to the reader who arrived at the wrong moment.

And its price has a shape worth knowing. In the limit of an unbounded window, the extra mean staleness is exactly 1/r — one inter-request interval — with max-age absent from the expression entirely. So it costs the most staleness on the quietest clients, which are exactly the clients whose p99 it was added to fix. At one read every 5 seconds that is 5 seconds of staleness; at one read an hour it is an hour.

And revalidation is not always the wrong answer. Three cases where it is the right one, and they share a shape: the resource is large, or the connection is warm and near, or correctness forbids serving a stale copy at all.

  • A large body over a warm connection. Above 562.6 kB at 10 Mbps, a 304 saves over half the response time. This is rsync, package mirrors, and a browser reloading a 4 MB source map.
  • Inside a datacentre. 58.84% at a 0.5 ms round trip. Service-to-service ETags on a keep-alive pool are a genuine optimisation.
  • When stale is unacceptable and the URL cannot change. A permissions document, a feature flag that gates a legal obligation. Here must-revalidate is doing real work — and note what it also buys you, which is the opposite of availability: RFC 9111 §5.2.2.2 requires a cache to return 504 rather than serve a stale copy when the origin is unreachable. You have chosen correctness over being up, on purpose, and you should know you have.
07

In the wild

The IETF standardised a directive whose only job is to stop clients asking. RFC 8246 (Bishop, HTTP Immutable Responses, September 2017) exists because a browser reload revalidates resources that the origin knows can never change — content-addressed static assets — and the protocol had no way to say so. The document’s whole mechanism is one token, immutable, and its whole argument is this lesson’s: those conditional requests were never going to return anything but 304, and the round trips they cost were pure loss. It is worth noticing what shape the fix took. Not a cheaper validator, not a compression scheme for conditional requests — a way to not send them.

Every modern build tool ships the same answer, and calls it something else. Rails’ asset pipeline fingerprints filenames with a digest of their contents; webpack, Vite and esbuild all emit [contenthash] in output names by default; and the reason given in each project’s documentation is always cache correctness rather than cache performance. The header that goes with them — Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable — is a year, which is only safe because the filename is a function of the bytes. Two mechanisms doing one job: the URL makes staleness impossible, and the max-age makes asking unnecessary.

stale-while-revalidate was proposed by a CDN operator, for the reason in slot 6. RFC 5861 (Nottingham, HTTP Cache-Control Extensions for Stale Content, May 2010) is four pages, and its motivation is explicitly about the tail: some requests must wait for the origin, and a cache that may serve stale can move that wait off the reader. Every major CDN implements it, and the honest way to describe what you have bought is a latency distribution change with an origin-load change of zero.

The shared-versus-private boundary has a canonical incident, and its shape is exactly the arithmetic in slot 5. On 25 December 2015, Steam served pages containing other users’ account details — email addresses, partial payment information — to logged-in visitors. Valve’s public explanation was a caching configuration change made while under a denial-of-service attack, and the number of users whose information was shown to someone else was reported at the time as around 34,000, over a window of roughly 90 minutes. Two features of that are worth carrying: the cause was a configuration change rather than a code defect, and the window was short. Both are what the model predicts — the first leak arrives milliseconds after the change, and the count is driven by the traffic rate rather than by how long anyone took to notice. (Figures here are as publicly reported at the time; this lesson’s model reproduces the shape, not that specific incident.)

Why Vary: User-Agent keeps appearing in CDN documentation as a thing not to do. The arithmetic in slot 5 is the reason: 5,000 distinct strings behave like 50 equally likely ones, which sounds survivable until you notice that it multiplies your origin’s request rate by 50 and divides each key’s arrival rate by the same factor. A header added to serve one slightly different response to old browsers ends up deciding your origin’s capacity.

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A 304 Not Modified is a request: it costs the same round trips as a 200 — one on a warm connection, three on a cold TCP + TLS 1.3 one — and removes only the body's transfer time
  • `max-age` and revalidation answer different questions, "may I use this without asking" and "is what I hold still current", so they are not substitutes: freshness removes a request and validation only makes one cheaper
  • `Cache-Control` is four settings in one header — freshness, validation, scope and blocking — and `no-cache` is a validation directive that stores the response, while `no-store` is a confidentiality directive that stores nothing
  • A content-hashed URL beats every revalidation strategy because a changed file is a different URL, so an unchanged file is never asked about again — and it is the only strategy that can never serve a superseded copy
  • `stale-while-revalidate` leaves origin fetches and the fresh-hit ratio bit-identical and only moves who waits, so it changes the latency distribution without changing the amount of work
  • In front of C private caches, `max-age=T` puts a hard ceiling of C/T on origin request rate, because read rate enters the renewal cycle only as the additive term 1/r
  • `private` versus `public` is a correctness boundary: on a shared cache the fraction of responses served from another user's copy is the hit ratio times (1 − 1/users), so the better the cache works the more it leaks

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    An API returns a 1,024-byte JSON body with max-age=0, must-revalidate and an ETag. The client is 150 ms away on a cold TCP + TLS 1.3 connection at 10 Mbps. The ETag matches and the origin answers 304. What did the cache save?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    53.09% of the bytes and 0.204% of the response time. The 304 is a request: three round trips at 150 ms is 450 ms, paid identically either way, and the only thing the 304 removes is bytes on the wire — 307 bytes of response headers and 1,024 of body, less the 180 bytes the 304 still carries, which is 0.9208 ms out of 451.06. The number to compare it against is not the full fetch but the fresh hit, which costs 0 ms and 0 bytes. Revalidation is not a cheap hit; it is a cheap miss.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A page loads 40 subresources. A reader visits it 5 times in a session, 5 minutes apart, and 2 files are genuinely different by the next visit. Compare max-age=0, must-revalidate with content-hashed filenames, over HTTP/1.1 with 6 connections at a 150 ms round trip.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    200 requests and 35 serial round trips (5.25 s, 258.6 kB) against 48 requests and 11 round trips (1.65 s, 104.1 kB). A 3.18× difference in round trips, 3.60 s of origin wait and 154.6 kB — and 152 of those 200 requests were conditional requests that the immutable build never sends at all. The reason is not that hashing answers the question faster. A file whose name contains its content's hash has no freshness question: if the content changed, the URL changed, so the cached copy is not stale, it is simply a copy of a different resource.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A shared CDN cache is accidentally serving a personalised account summary with Cache-Control: public, max-age=60. 5,000 users read it at one request every 50 seconds each. How bad is it, and how long before anyone could notice?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    99.963% of responses are the wrong user's — 359,868 of 360,000 in the first hour — and the first one arrives about 10 ms after the deploy. The leaked fraction is the hit ratio times (1 − 1/users), and at λ = 100 requests per second against a 60-second window the hit ratio is 99.983%. Both factors are quantities you were trying to maximise, so a cache working perfectly leaks perfectly. Each single fill is served to about 3,493 distinct users before it expires. This is why scope is not a tuning knob: every other directive trades latency against staleness, and this one trades nothing — it is either right or it is a data breach.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Adding stale-while-revalidate=120 to a resource with max-age=30 read once every 5 seconds. What moves, and what does not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Origin fetches: 0.028415 per second, before and after, identical to every printed digit — and the fresh-hit ratio is likewise unchanged at 0.853590. What moves is who waits. The share of requests that block on the origin falls from 14.6% to 0.002%, and p99 served latency falls from 150.0 ms to 0.0 ms: the tail stops being a network event. The bill is staleness — mean served age rises from 12.85 s to 17.97 s — and in the limit of an unbounded window the extra staleness is exactly 1/r, one inter-request interval, with no max-age in the expression at all. So it costs the most staleness on precisely the quiet clients whose p99 it was fixing.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A mobile app checks a feature-flag endpoint on every screen open with If-None-Match. The backend team is pleased: 99% of the responses are 304s and origin egress is down 60%. Users in Jakarta report that opening a screen "hangs for a second". What is happening, and what would you change?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The 99% figure measures the wrong thing. Every one of those 304s is a blocking round trip on a link with a 300 ms RTT and a frequently cold connection — so the app is paying about 900 ms of setup and exchange per screen open to be told nothing has changed. The 60% egress saving is real and the latency saving is zero. Two changes, in order: give the response a real max-age so most screen opens ask nothing at all, and add stale-while-revalidate so the refresh happens off the critical path. If the flags must be fresh within seconds, the answer is not a faster question — it is a different mechanism, a push or a long-lived stream, because a poll's floor is one round trip and no header changes that.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system can answer a question faster, or it can arrange for the question never to arise — and the second is usually available, usually cheaper, and usually not considered because the first one feels like the responsible engineering. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Library cataloguing by edition rather than by title. If two printings share a shelf label, every borrower must ask a librarian whether the copy in their hand is the current one; give each printing its own identifier and the question is structurally unaskable — which is exactly what a content-hashed filename does. Milk cartons are the same move: a use-by date printed on the carton means nobody phones the dairy to ask whether this bottle is still good. Aircraft part serial numbers with an airworthiness tag — you do not query a database about the bolt in your hand, because the bolt carries its own identity and provenance. A tamper-evident seal replaces "has this been opened?" with a fact you can see. In every case the cheap version answers the question and the good version deletes it, and the tell is the same: you find yourself optimising the cost of asking.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
