Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Blocking vs async explained well nowhere else

There are three ways to wait, not two, and the cheapest one costs 0.97 µs while the one you replaced cost 0.90

A blocked thread costs memory and zero CPU. A retry loop costs CPU and zero memory. An async completion costs a queue entry, a share of one wakeup, and a per-operation fee the other two never pay — so below a computable concurrency, and above a computable ready fraction, "make it async" is a rewrite that ends with more CPU per operation than it started with.

01

The async rewrite raised CPU per operation by 7.8% and capped throughput at a tenth of the machine

A service reads small records from a cache on the same host, over a unix socket. Two things are true of that read, and both were measured:

  • Nine times out of ten the bytes are already in the socket buffer. The call does not wait at all. It copies a few hundred bytes and returns — about 0.300 µs of CPU, which is what a cheap syscall costs.
  • The tenth time it waits about 100 µs, and a blocking call pays two context switches for that: one to give up the CPU, one to be woken. About 6.00 µs.

So the average cost of the wait, per operation, is 0.300 + 0.1 × 6.00 = 0.900 µs of CPU.

The service is rewritten to be asynchronous — io_uring submissions, completions reaped in a loop, continuations resumed on completion. The expected result was the ratio everyone quotes: an async completion costs 0.970 µs against a blocked thread’s 6.300 µs, so 6.49× less CPU per wait.

Measured, CPU per operation goes up, from 0.900 µs to 0.970 µs — 7.8% more CPU, for identical throughput. The 6.49× was real and it applied to one operation in ten. The other nine used to cost one syscall and now cost a submission, a completion and a continuation each.

Then a second number arrives, from the same rewrite. The service still does one DNS lookup on 5% of requests, and getaddrinfo has no asynchronous form, so the runtime hands it to a helper pool of four threads. At 2,600 requests per second that pool holds 2.6 threads permanently busy, the lookup’s wall clock has gone from 20 ms to 25.06 ms, and the whole service is capped at 4,000 requests per second — against the 40,000 its eight cores would allow. A 10× ceiling with no CPU term in it, on a service that has just been made “fully async”.

Both results come from the same fact, and it is not “async is bad”. It is that blocking, non-blocking and asynchronous are three different things, they charge for a wait in three different currencies, and the industry uses the words interchangeably.

02

The obvious fix is to stop waiting at all. That costs a whole core per waiting request.

If getting off the CPU costs two context switches, and being told about a completion costs a continuation, then the obvious move is to do neither: set the socket non-blocking, and just call read again until it returns something.

This is the mode almost everyone means when they say “non-blocking”, and it is also the mode most often mislabelled “async”. It is neither. A non-blocking call does not start anything and nothing will tell you when the data arrives — it returns EAGAIN, which means that call would have blocked, and the obligation to come back is now yours. Coming back is polling, whatever the code calls it, and its price is set by one parameter you may not have realised you were choosing: how eagerly you retry.

CPU per wait, retrying = 1.5 × syscall + duty × W

duty = syscall / interval — the share of a core the retry loop holds. Retrying as fast as the syscall allows makes duty 100%, and the CPU cost of the wait becomes the wait.

At a 1 ms wait, retried as hard as possible, that is 1000.45 µs of CPU against 6.30 µs to block — 158.8×, and a whole core held for a millisecond to avoid two switches that cost six microseconds. The half in that formula is not decoration: the completion lands at a uniformly random point between two retries, so the expected retry count carries a half, and at a wait of one interval that half is a quarter of the bill.

But the line crosses. Below some wait duration, retrying really is cheaper — and where it crosses is computable.

Predict A read finds its data already buffered 90% of the time; the other 10% it waits 100 µs. Blocking costs one 0.300 µs syscall when ready and two 3.00 µs switches when not. You convert it to async and run 40 operations in flight. What happens to CPU per operation?
Blocking averages 0.300 + 0.1 × 6.00 = 0.900 µs. Async costs its bookkeeping every time, ready or not: an SQE, a CQE and a continuation come to 0.670 µs, plus its own 0.300 µs submission syscall, because at 40 operations in flight the completion batch is 1.00 and there is nothing to divide that syscall across. Total 0.970 µs, which is 7.8% more. The first option is the belief this lesson exists to break: 6.49× is the ratio for an operation that actually waits, and nine in ten of these do not. The last option treats the mode as free — but a continuation is an object that has to be created, parked and resumed, and a blocking call has none, because its stack is its continuation.
Show it without answering

Three things to take from it, in this order:

  • The crossing is real, and it is about two context switches. At the default retry interval the break-even wait is 5.85 µs — almost exactly 2 × switchCost = 6.00 µs, because both modes make the call and the syscall very nearly cancels. Below it, spinning is cheaper in CPU and lower in latency, which is the entire content of an adaptive mutex.
  • Backing the retry off is a one-for-one trade, not a free win. A 100 µs interval divides the CPU by 333 and multiplies the break-even by 333 — and the noticed late column goes from 0.15 µs to 50.0 µs. You did not remove the cost; you moved it from CPU to latency.
  • The currencies are disjoint. In the lower band, the blocked row holds 0% of a core and 80.0 KiB; the polled row holds 100% of a core and 24.0 KiB. Neither number is a smaller version of the other, and that is why “what does a waiting request cost” has no single answer.
03

The intuition: three ways to wait for a parcel

You are working, and a parcel is coming.

You can stand by the front door until it arrives. You do nothing else — but you also consume nothing else. No electricity, no attention, no interruptions. What you consume is yourself: a whole person, reserved, for the whole wait. If four parcels are coming, four people stand by four doors. That is blocking, and its bill is a body per parcel and no work at all.

You can stay at your desk and walk out to check the porch every so often. Nobody is reserved. But every trip costs you a broken train of thought, the trips cost the same whether or not the parcel is there, and you learn about the delivery up to one interval late — on average, half. Check every thirty seconds and you barely work; check every twenty minutes and you work fine but the parcel sits outside for ten. That is non-blocking, and its bill is attention proportional to how often you check, plus staleness proportional to how rarely.

Or you can give the courier your mobile number and be texted when it is in the hallway. Now nobody stands anywhere and nobody checks. But notice what this cost that the other two did not: someone had to capture the number, attach it to this parcel, and store it — per parcel, every parcel, including the ones that turn out to be already at the door when you look. That is asynchronous, and its bill is a small fixed fee per request plus one message. The fee is real, it does not scale down, and if most of your parcels are already in the hallway it is pure loss.

The three are not degrees of the same thing. Blocking spends a server. Polling spends attention, at a rate you set. Notification spends a small per-request fee and buys back both. Which is cheapest depends on how long the wait is, how often there is no wait at all, and which of the three you are actually short of.

And now the failure that decides real systems. Suppose every courier texts — except one, who only knocks. Somebody has to stand by the door for that one. It does not matter that every other delivery is handled by text; the building’s throughput is now set by how many people are available to stand by doors, and there are four of them.

04

The mechanism: readiness is not completion, and the cost is a vector

The three modes, precisely

The distinction is who waits and who notices, and it produces three modes rather than two.

ModeThe callWho waitsHow you learn it finished
Blockingdoes not return until the data is therethe kernel, on your behalf, with your thread descheduledit returned
Non-blockingreturns now, EAGAIN if it would have waitednobody — nothing was startedyou call again and it succeeds
Asynchronousreturns now, having started the transfer into your bufferthe kernel, with no thread of yours helda completion is posted to you

The pair that get conflated are the last two, and the difference is a real API distinction:

  • epoll, kqueue, select and poll are readiness interfaces. They tell you a call would not block. You then make the call yourself, on your own thread, and if you were wrong about the descriptor you get EAGAIN again. An event loop built on epoll is a non-blocking program with a good scheduler for its retries, not an asynchronous one.
  • io_uring, Windows IOCP and POSIX AIO are completion interfaces. You hand over a buffer and a description of the work; the kernel performs the transfer and posts a completion. The read itself never runs on your thread.

The clearest evidence that these are genuinely different is what happened to the one that pretended: glibc implements POSIX AIO in user space with a pool of threads that make ordinary blocking calls. The interface is asynchronous and the implementation is blocking, and the performance is the performance of blocking — which is precisely the ambiguity this section exists to remove.

Read the third row against the second. The same mode, with one parameter moved, is 290× cheaper in CPU and 333× worse in detection lag. That is not a better implementation; it is the same implementation at a different point on a line, and choosing the point is the engineering.

Where spinning stops winning

Blocking’s CPU cost is flat in the wait duration. Retrying’s is linear in it. Two lines with different slopes cross exactly once:

W* = (2 × switchCost − 0.5 × syscall) / duty

duty = syscall / interval. Spin as hard as possible and duty is 1, giving 5.85 µs — very nearly the two context switches, because both modes make the call and the syscall almost cancels.

This is why adaptive mutexes exist. PTHREAD_MUTEX_ADAPTIVE_NP, the Linux futex fast path and InnoDB’s lock waits all spin briefly and then block: below a few microseconds of expected wait the spin is cheaper in CPU and lower in latency at the same time, and above it the spin is burning a core to save two switches. The break-even is not a tuning constant somebody guessed — it is 2 × switchCost, and it moves when your retry interval does.

Async’s per-operation fee, and why batching does not always remove it

An async operation costs things a blocking call never pays for. Here is the whole fee, drawn against what the blocking call cost on an operation whose data was already there:

Only the submission syscall amortises, and it amortises over the batch — how many completions one io_uring_enter returns. That batch is not a parameter. It is a fixed point:

cycle = ringEnter + k × perOp · k = C × cycle / (W + cycle)

C operations outstanding, each spending W in the device and one cycle in the loop that reaps it. k is floored at 1 — a loop that finds one completion still has to make the call.

And that floor is the surprise. k stays at exactly 1 until the completion rate approaches the loop’s own service rate, so batching is a property of load, not of the API:

06

The trade-off: you chose a colour for every function above the call

What async buys is real and this lesson is not an argument against it: for an operation that actually waits, 6.49× less CPU per wait, no kernel task per waiting request, and one wakeup divided by the batch. On a workload of genuine waits at genuine concurrency, nothing else competes.

What you gave up is that the mode is not a property of the call. It is a property of every function above the call, and there is no way to hold only part of it.

This is Bob Nystrom’s What Color is Your Function? (2015) in its operational rather than syntactic form. The syntactic version is that an async function can only be called from an async function, so the colour propagates up the whole call stack. The operational version is worse, because it is not checked by anything:

Four consequences worth carrying:

  • Nothing is uniformly async, and the list of exceptions is longer than the list of rules. getaddrinfo. A synchronous stat or open. A mutex. A page fault on mmap’ed data — which is a blocking disk read with no call site at all. getrandom draining its pool. A C library reached through FFI. Kernel AIO (io_submit) is genuinely asynchronous only for O_DIRECT; on a buffered file it blocks in the submission call, which is why io_uring had to exist. Every one of these reintroduces the thread cost without the thread being visible.
  • The helper pool is not a workaround, it is the architecture. libuv ships four threads and UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE to change it; Tokio has spawn_blocking; Go’s runtime hands an OS thread to a blocking syscall and brings up a replacement. Sizing that pool is the Little’s Law calculation λ · g · D, and it is a calculation almost nobody does, because the pool is not in the diagram.
  • The failure has no saturated resource, which is why it survives. At the 4,000 rps ceiling, CPU is at 10%, memory is fine, the event loop is idle, and every dashboard says there is headroom. The only signal is the occupancy of the blocking pool — how many of those four threads are inside a call right now — and that is a number you have to go and add.
  • Converting the hot path first is the wrong order. The calls that are ready 90%+ of the time are the ones that look most worth optimising and are the ones where async costs more. The operations worth converting are the ones that actually wait, and the way to find them is to measure the ready fraction before the rewrite, not the call rate.
07

In the wild

io_uring (Jens Axboe, Linux 5.1, 2019) exists because Linux had no working asynchronous file I/O, and the reasons are worth knowing because each one is an instance of this page. POSIX AIO in glibc is a user-space thread pool making blocking calls — asynchronous interface, blocking implementation, blocking performance. Kernel AIO (io_submit) is asynchronous only for O_DIRECT; on a buffered file the submission call itself blocks, so the “async” API had a blocking path through it. Axboe’s design documents state the goals directly: a completion interface with shared submission and completion rings so the syscall is amortised over a batch rather than paid per operation, and a polled mode that removes it entirely. Both of those are the ringEnter / batch term on this page, and the polled mode is a deliberate 100%-duty spin — the left-hand end of the crossover, chosen on purpose.

Windows had a completion interface first, and it changed how portable runtimes are shaped. I/O Completion Ports (Windows NT 3.5, 1994) are completion-based, while epoll and kqueue are readiness-based, and libuv has to present one API over both. The result is that libuv’s abstraction is completion-shaped — you hand over a buffer and get a callback — and on Unix it implements that shape on top of readiness by doing the non-blocking read itself after epoll says the descriptor is ready. Reading libuv’s documentation on this is the clearest available demonstration that readiness and completion are different interfaces rather than different words.

libuv’s four-thread default is where the ceiling on this page comes from. Filesystem calls, getaddrinfo, and some zlib and crypto work go to that pool because they have no portable non-blocking form. The number is UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE, its default is 4, and the arithmetic poolThreads / (g · D) turns that default into a throughput ceiling that no profile explains. The advice that circulates in that ecosystem — “raise UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE if you do a lot of filesystem or DNS work” — is a Little’s Law calculation being passed on as folklore.

Adaptive spinning is the crossover shipped in production, three times over. Linux’s futex fast path, PTHREAD_MUTEX_ADAPTIVE_NP, and InnoDB’s lock waits all spin for a bounded interval before blocking, for exactly the reason on this page: below a wait of about two context switches the spin is cheaper in CPU and lower in latency, and the bound exists because above it the spin is burning a core to save six microseconds.

And the deliberate extreme, which is the same trade taken to its end: DPDK and SPDK poll-mode drivers never block and never sleep. They spin on a dedicated core at 100% duty, permanently, because at packet-level waits the crossover is nowhere near — the alternative would be a context switch per packet. top shows a core pinned at 100% and that is the correct operating state, which makes the point that the currencies really are disjoint: a core burned deliberately is not a bug, it is the cheapest available way to wait for something that arrives every few hundred nanoseconds.

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Blocking, non-blocking and asynchronous are three modes, not two: blocking deschedules the caller, non-blocking returns EAGAIN and makes the caller come back, and asynchronous hands over a buffer and reports completion — readiness and completion are different interfaces
  • The cost per waiting request is a vector, not a number: a blocked thread costs resident memory and zero CPU, a retry loop costs CPU proportional to how eagerly it retries and no memory, and an async operation costs a queue entry plus a share of one wakeup
  • Spinning is cheaper than blocking below a wait of 2 × switchCost / duty — about 5.85 µs when retrying as hard as possible — and backing the retry interval off multiplies that break-even by exactly the factor it divides the CPU by, paid for in detection lag
  • An async operation carries a fixed per-operation cost (0.97 µs, most of it the continuation) that a blocking call does not, so blocking is cheaper for any operation whose data is already there more than 93.83% of the time, at any concurrency
  • The submission syscall only amortises when the completion rate approaches the loop's own service rate, so batching is a property of load rather than of the API — at low concurrency every async operation pays a full syscall of its own
  • One call with no async form puts λ·g·D OS threads back in the process by Little's Law, and caps the whole service at poolThreads/(g·D) requests per second — a ceiling with no CPU term in it

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A call returns immediately with EAGAIN instead of waiting. Someone calls this "async". What did it actually do, and what does it now cost you that a truly asynchronous call would not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It told you the operation would have blocked, and returned. Nothing has been started and nothing will tell you when the data arrives — you have to come back and ask again, which is polling however it is dressed up, and the cost is CPU proportional to how eagerly you retry: 1.5 + W/interval syscalls per wait, so 1000.45 µs for a 1 ms wait retried as fast as the syscall allows, against 6.30 µs to block — 158.8×. A completion interface (io_uring, IOCP) takes your buffer, performs the transfer, and reports that it is done; a readiness interface (epoll, poll) reports only that a call would not block, and you still make the call yourself.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Two identical waits, one blocked and one spun. Write down what each charges, in every currency you can name, and say which term is zero in which.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Blocked: 6.30 µs of CPU for the two switches around the wait, 80.0 KiB resident for the thread that holds the wait, one kernel task, and 0% of a core for the duration — a thread on a wait queue is not on the run queue and the scheduler never picks it. Spun: 100% of a core, 24.0 KiB (a socket and a parser, no stack and no task_struct), zero kernel tasks, and CPU that grows linearly with the wait. The currencies are disjoint, which is why there is no single number: blocking pays in memory, polling pays in CPU, and the winner depends entirely on which one you are short of.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Below what wait duration is a busy retry loop cheaper than blocking, and what happens to that number if you retry every 100 µs instead of as fast as you can?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Blocking costs a flat syscall + 2 × switchCost; retrying costs 1.5 × syscall + duty × W, where duty is the share of a core the loop holds. Setting them equal gives W* = (2 × switchCost − 0.5 × syscall) / duty, and at duty = 100% that is 5.85 µs — very nearly two context switches, because both modes make the call and the syscall almost cancels. Retrying every 100 µs drops duty to 0.30% and moves the break-even to 1.95 ms, a factor of 333 in both directions at once: 333× less CPU, and a completion noticed 50 µs late instead of 0.15 µs late.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A read finds its data already buffered 90% of the time; when it does not, the wait is 100 µs. You convert it from a blocking call to an async one and run 40 operations in flight. Predict the change in CPU per operation, and name the term that decided it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It goes up 7.8% — from 0.900 µs to 0.970 µs. The blocking call costs one syscall (0.300 µs) nine times out of ten and two switches the tenth time: 0.300 + 0.1 × 6.00 = 0.900 µs. The async call costs 0.670 µs of bookkeeping every single time — an SQE, a CQE, and a continuation to create and resume — plus its own 0.300 µs submission syscall, because at 40 in flight the batch is 1.00 and there is nothing to amortise it over. The deciding term is the continuation, at 0.450 µs the largest single part: a blocking call does not have one, because the stack is the continuation.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Above what share of already-ready operations does blocking beat async on CPU no matter how much concurrency you give it, and why does more concurrency help async at all?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    93.83%. Concurrency helps only through the batch: one io_uring_enter covers however many completions it returns, so the 0.300 µs syscall is divided by the batch. But the batch is a fixed point — k = C·cycle/(W+cycle) — and it stays pinned at 1 until the completion rate approaches the loop's own service rate. So batching is a property of load, not of the API: at 100 µs waits it takes 113 operations in flight before async draws level at 90% ready, and by then the loop is at 100% of its core. With the syscall amortised away entirely the floor is still 0.670 µs, which is above one syscall — hence a break-even below 100%.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    A service is "async all the way down" except for one DNS lookup: 5% of requests, 20 ms each, at 2,600 requests per second on eight cores. How many OS threads is it really running, and what caps it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    2.6 threads, resident, all the time — Little's Law on the helper pool: λ·g·D = 2,600 × 0.05 × 0.02. The threads did not go away; they moved into UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE and stopped being visible. The cap is poolThreads/(g·D) = 4 / 0.001 = 4,000 rps against the 40,000 the cores would allow — a 10× ceiling with no CPU term in it, so no amount of profiling finds it. And it is the queueing hockey stick on the way there: the lookup takes 25.06 ms at 2,600 rps and 109.14 ms at 3,800, for a call that takes 20 ms.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A Rust service using Tokio calls a synchronous C library through FFI on 2% of requests; the call takes 8 ms. A colleague says this is fine because the function is marked async and awaited, so the runtime will handle it. Grade that, and say what measurement would settle it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Marking a function async is a statement about its return type, not about whether it yields. An .await point only yields where the future returns Pending, and an FFI call that blocks never returns Pending — it occupies the worker thread for its whole duration, which is f·B arriving in a runtime that has one worker per core rather than one thread per request. The fix has a name in that ecosystem, spawn_blocking, and it is the helper pool: it moves the call to a thread whose count is then a Little's Law calculation, λ × 0.02 × 0.008 threads resident, capping the service at poolThreads / 0.00016 requests per second. The measurement that settles it is not CPU and not latency — both look fine at low load. It is the occupancy of the blocking pool, or equivalently Tokio's own worker-starvation counters: the question "how many of my threads are parked inside a call right now" has an answer, and it is the only one that distinguishes a syntactically async path from an operationally async one.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: three ways to wait for something you asked another party for — stand there until it is handed to you, go back to your own work and keep returning to check, or leave instructions for how to be told — and the third has setup paperwork per request that the other two skip, so it only pays off in volume. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The abstraction is the choice between blocking, polling and notification for a deferred result, and the invariants are that blocking reserves a whole server, polling costs attention proportional to its frequency and adds up to half a period of staleness, and notification costs per-request setup that only amortises at volume. A pharmacy: wait at the counter (blocking), come back every ten minutes to ask (polling — and you find out up to ten minutes late), or leave your mobile number for a text (notification, and someone has to capture and store the number for every prescription). A restaurant: the maître d' who stands with you, the pager buzzer, or the "we'll call your name" board — and the buzzer is capital that only makes sense above some covers per night. A doctor's referral: sit in the waiting room, ring the surgery every week, or be put on a callback list. And the coloured-function failure has an exact instance: a hospital that texts every result except the one lab that only answers the phone — so somebody has to sit on hold for those, and how many requests per hour the whole hospital can handle is set by how many people are willing to sit on hold, not by anything else in the building. The structural answer is always the same three: shorten the wait, batch the notifications, or accept a dedicated waiter and count how many you need.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
