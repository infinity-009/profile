Tier 2 · Scaling the stateless tier Multi-tenancy & noisy neighbours

One tenant demanding 10x its fair share turns a 40ms wait into 800ms for everyone else — a hard quota fixes 95% of that, and fair-share scheduling fixes all of it while still handing that tenant 10x more throughput than the quota would

Every tenant sharing a pool — CPU, disk I/O, a database, a queue — is exposed to every other tenant's usage, whether or not either one did anything wrong. `cell-based-architecture` and `shuffle-sharding` answer this by dividing the pool up so tenants stop sharing it. This lesson states the problem precisely and computes the cheaper answers that don't require dividing anything: a hard per-tenant quota, and fair-share scheduling — which turns out to buy the same protection as the quota, plus flexibility the quota structurally cannot offer.

01

Two hundred tenants, one shared queue, and a batch job that changed nothing about anyone else

A hundred customers share one API gateway’s connection pool, each sending a modest, steady stream of requests. One customer kicks off a nightly batch export — nothing malicious, nothing against the terms of service, just far more traffic than usual, all at once. Within seconds, every OTHER customer’s p99 latency climbs, even though not one of them changed a single request they were sending. Support tickets come in from customers who have no way to know a stranger’s batch job is the reason their own dashboard is suddenly slow.

Nobody did anything wrong except run one job that happened to ask for more than its share, and the pool had no way to tell the difference between “more” and “too much.” This is the noisy- neighbour problem, stated precisely rather than as a slogan: a shared resource pool with capacity C, serving multiple tenants, where one tenant’s demand disproportionate to its “fair share” degrades every OTHER tenant sharing that pool — even though those tenants are blameless and have no visibility into why their own numbers just got worse. cell-based-architecture and shuffle-sharding, the two previous lessons, both answer this by making tenants stop sharing the resource — full duplication, or a randomized partial duplication. This lesson computes the answers that work while tenants keep sharing ONE pool.

02

Assume the only real fix is to stop sharing

Once you’ve seen a noisy neighbour wreck a shared pool, the instinct is that sharing itself is the defect — the fix must be to give every tenant, or every small group, its own dedicated resources. That instinct is exactly what motivates full physical isolation, and it is not wrong that it works. What it skips is that a shared pool can be defended from the inside, with admission control or scheduling, for a small fraction of the cost of dividing it — commit to a number for how much that actually buys before the rig shows you one.

Predict 20 tenants share a pool. 19 of them each use half of their own fair share; the 20th demands 10x its fair share. With no mitigation, every tenant's own wait rises from a 40ms baseline to 800ms — a 20x penalty for tenants who changed nothing. What happens to that 800ms under (a) a hard per-tenant rate limit capping the noisy tenant at its own fair share, and (b) fair-share scheduling that guarantees each tenant a minimum rate but sets no hard ceiling?
A hard limit caps the noisy tenant's ADMITTED rate at its quota before the shared queue ever sees the rest, so the queue's utilisation drops back near what it would have been without a noisy tenant — the wait for everyone in it drops correspondingly. Fair-share scheduling never touches admission at all: it guarantees each of the other 19 tenants a minimum service rate, and a tenant using at or under its own guarantee experiences a wait bounded by its OWN demand against its OWN guarantee, independent of the 20th tenant's behaviour — the textbook isolation property of weighted fair queueing. Both land the other tenants back near the 40ms baseline; the two differ in what happens to the noisy tenant, not to everyone else.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s defaults — one tenant of 20 demanding 10x its fair share, everyone else using 50% of theirs — the shared pool sits at 97.5% utilization and every tenant’s wait rises from a 40ms baseline to 800ms, a 20x latency penalty imposed on 19 tenants who changed nothing. A hard rate limit, capping the noisy tenant’s admitted rate at its own fair share, brings that back to 42.1ms — within 5% of baseline. Fair-share scheduling brings it back to exactly 40ms, matching the baseline precisely, because the other tenants’ own wait under fair-share never depended on the noisy tenant’s demand in the first place. The difference between the two mitigations shows up on the noisy tenant’s side instead: the hard limit caps it at 50 requests/s, full stop; fair-share admits its full 500 requests/s demand, because nobody else was using their share of the pool right now — a 10x throughput gap for identical protection to everyone else.

03

A shared pump, and the two ways a building keeps one long shower from draining everyone's pressure

Every apartment in a building draws water from one pump sized for ordinary, staggered use across all units. One tenant runs a long shower, fills a deep tub, and starts the washing machine, all at once — far more than an equal share of the building’s flow. Every other tenant’s water pressure drops, including tenants on a floor that has nothing physically connecting it to the noisy unit’s plumbing except the shared pump’s finite total output. No pipe carries the cause from one apartment to the other. The only connection is the pump’s own limited capacity, exactly like a shared queue’s finite aggregate throughput.

A building has two ways to fix this without re-plumbing separate risers per wing (this lesson’s cell-based-architecture option, always available and always the most expensive). It can install a flow restrictor on each unit’s line — a hard cap, identical every hour of the day whether the building is busy or half-empty. Or it can install a pressure-balancing system that guarantees every unit a minimum flow but lets a unit’s UNUSED flow go to whoever is drawing more right now — the same water pressure at 3am whether one neighbour is showering or ten, and still full pressure for the one long shower on a night when nobody else is home. Both protect the quiet units. Only the second one gives the noisy one room to be noisy when it costs nobody else anything.

04

Three regimes, one closed form each

shared-queue utilisation: ρ = ΣDᵢ / C

Reused unchanged from kernel.mjs’s mm1Multiplier(ρ) = 1/(1-ρ) — the same M/M/1 result every queueing lesson in this course already uses. A shared FIFO queue has exactly one utilisation number, and every tenant in it experiences the SAME wait multiplier, because the queue has no concept of whose request is whose.

hard limit: admittedᵢ = min(Dᵢ, quotaᵢ)

Cap each tenant’s admitted demand before it joins the shared queue, then rerun the identical ρ formula at the resulting, lower aggregate. Simple, and every request above the quota is dropped or rejected outright — including the ones a tenant could safely have sent while everyone else was idle.

fair-share isolation: a tenant with Dᵢ ≤ its own guarantee gᵢ waits as if alone at capacity gᵢ

The weighted-fair-queueing / generalized-processor-sharing isolation bound (Parekh & Gallager, 1993): a tenant that stays within its own guaranteed minimum rate is protected from every OTHER tenant’s behaviour, full stop — no admission control, no dropped requests, just a scheduling discipline that never lets one flow’s backlog delay another flow past its own guarantee.

Fair-share’s other half — what a tenant asking for MORE than its guarantee actually gets — is a throughput question, not a latency one, and it is max-min fair (“water-filling”) allocation: give everyone at least min(demand, the equal share), then hand any leftover capacity, in equal increments, to whoever still wants more. maxMinFairAllocation is the one genuinely new piece of machinery in this lesson — nothing in this codebase modeled bandwidth-sharing fairness before — and it is why a hard limit and fair-share, identical for the other tenants, diverge for the noisy one.

06

Neither mitigation is free, and reservations are a third, distinct lever

A hard rate limit wastes capacity nobody else wants. At 10% or 50% utilization elsewhere, the table above shows a hard quota admitting 50 requests/s from the noisy tenant while 450 requests/s of genuinely available, unused pool capacity sits idle — capacity a hard limit has no mechanism to offer, because it caps at a fixed number regardless of what else is happening in the pool at that instant. It is also the cheaper mechanism to build: a counter per tenant and a comparison, no scheduler to get right.

Fair-share scheduling is real engineering, not a free upgrade. Implementing weighted fair queueing (or an approximation of it) correctly — deciding weights, handling heterogeneous work that doesn’t reduce to one clean “rate,” keeping the per-flow accounting cheap enough to run on every request — is a substantially harder system to build and reason about than a per-tenant counter, which is exactly why hard quotas remain the default answer, not fair-share, in most production rate-limiting systems.

A reservation is a third, separate lever from either — a guaranteed FLOOR is not the same decision as a hard CEILING. kubernetes-reconciliation.mdx already names this split for a single node: a pod’s request is a reservation made now, its limit is a promise about a future that is actually checked. The same distinction applies at the pool level here — a quota is a ceiling (min(demand, quota), this lesson’s regime (b)), a reservation is a floor (max(admitted, guarantee), half of regime (c)) — and fair-share scheduling is really a floor guarantee for every tenant plus opportunistic use of whatever’s left, not a single new idea but a combination of a reservation and a redistribution rule for the leftovers.

None of this removes the case for full isolation. A tenant whose blast radius has to be zero, always, regardless of engineering effort spent on quotas or schedulers — a regulator-mandated boundary, the single highest-risk customer on the platform — is still cell-based-architecture’s or shuffle-sharding’s problem to solve, not this lesson’s. What this lesson adds is the much larger set of ordinary cases where “good enough, for a fraction of the infrastructure cost” is the actual requirement, not “perfect.”

07

In the wild

Fair queueing as a scheduling discipline was formalized, not folk wisdom — Alan Demers, Srinivasan Keshav, and Scott Shenker, “Analysis and Simulation of a Fair Queueing Algorithm,” ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 1989, is the paper that turned “give everyone a fair turn” into an actual scheduling algorithm with provable properties, the ancestor of every weighted-fair-queueing implementation since.

The per-flow isolation guarantee this lesson’s isolatedWait computes is a proven bound, not this lesson’s own claim — Abhay K. Parekh and Robert G. Gallager, “A Generalized Processor Sharing Approach to Flow Control in Integrated Services Networks: The Single-Node Case,” IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, 1993, is the source of the theorem: a flow that stays within its guaranteed rate is bounded independent of every other flow’s behaviour, under GPS and its practical approximations like weighted fair queueing.

Kubernetes’ requests-vs-limits split, covered in kubernetes-reconciliation.mdx, is a single-node instance of the same reservation-vs-ceiling distinction this lesson generalizes to a whole shared pool — a request reserves a floor, a limit caps a ceiling, and neither one is the other.

Cloud provider per-tenant quotas and API rate limits are the hard-limit regime this lesson models, deployed at massive scale — the general pattern of capping admitted demand per account, per API key, or per customer before it reaches shared infrastructure, independent of any specific vendor’s published numbers.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Noisy-neighbour degradation happens through the RESOURCE, not through any code path connecting the tenants — a shared FIFO queue has no notion of whose request is whose, so raising the aggregate utilisation with one tenant's excess demand raises the wait for every tenant in that queue identically, including tenants who changed nothing
  • A hard per-tenant rate limit works by capping ADMITTED demand before it reaches the shared queue — it turns the noisy tenant's excess into dropped or rejected requests, which lowers the aggregate utilisation the shared queue actually sees, restoring everyone else's wait close to what it would have been without the noisy tenant at all
  • Fair-share (weighted-fair-queueing-style) scheduling gives every tenant a guaranteed MINIMUM service rate; a tenant whose own demand stays under its own guarantee experiences a wait bounded by its own demand against its own guarantee alone — the WFQ/GPS isolation property — which is why it protects other tenants exactly as well as a hard limit does, without ever capping anyone
  • Fair-share's one genuine advantage over a hard limit: unused capacity from an idle tenant is not wasted, it is redistributed (via max-min fair / water-filling allocation) to whoever is still asking for more — so a noisy tenant CAN legitimately burst past its own guarantee when nobody else is using theirs, which a hard ceiling, by construction, never allows
  • That advantage disappears exactly when everyone else is fully using their own guarantee — with no slack anywhere to redistribute, max-min fair allocation gives the noisy tenant nothing beyond its own equal share, identical to what a hard quota would have given it
  • Full physical isolation (cell-based-architecture) and combinatorial partial isolation (shuffle-sharding) solve noisy neighbours by making tenants stop sharing the resource at all; rate limits and fair-share scheduling solve it while tenants keep sharing ONE pool — cheaper in infrastructure, and the two families combine rather than compete: shrinking a cell just shrinks the pool a hard limit or a fair-share scheduler now has to defend

  1. Cued recall

    A team puts every tenant into one shared connection pool with no per-tenant accounting at all. One tenant's batch job asks for 10x its "fair" share; everyone else's request rate does not change at all. Why does everyone else's latency get worse anyway?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the pool is a single shared FIFO queue, and a FIFO queue's wait time is a function of AGGREGATE utilisation (total demand over capacity), not of any individual tenant's own demand. The noisy tenant's extra load raises that aggregate number for the whole queue, and every request already in that same queue — including requests from tenants who asked for exactly what they always ask for — waits behind more competition than before. Nobody else's code, traffic, or behaviour changed; the shared resource's utilisation did.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A hard per-tenant rate limit and fair-share scheduling both protect the other tenants to roughly the same degree in this lesson's numbers. If they buy the same protection, why would anyone choose the harder-to-implement fair-share scheduler over a simple quota?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because "the same protection for everyone else" is not the only outcome that differs. A hard limit caps the noisy tenant at its quota flatly, in every second, including the ones where the rest of the pool is sitting idle. Fair-share scheduling never lowers anyone's guarantee, but it DOES let a tenant use capacity nobody else currently wants — so the noisy tenant gets its full demand met whenever there's slack, and only gets throttled back to its guarantee once other tenants actually start using their own share. A hard quota can't tell the difference between "everyone's using their share" and "everyone's asleep"; fair-share scheduling can.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `cell-based-architecture.mdx` computes a noisy tenant claiming a growing share of ITS OWN cell's capacity as N grows and cells get smaller (9.2% of a big cell at N=4, 76.9% of a tiny one at N=100). A team reads that table and concludes "we should just make N very large so no cell is ever small enough for this to matter." Does that fix the problem this lesson describes?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it relocates the problem, it does not remove it. Shrinking cells to avoid one tenant dominating a cell just means each cell now holds fewer tenants sharing a smaller pool, which is EXACTLY the shared-pool-plus-one-noisy-tenant setup this lesson computes, just re-run at a smaller C. At the limit — one tenant per cell — there is no noisy-neighbour problem left inside a cell, but that is `cell-based-architecture`'s full-isolation answer at its most expensive extreme (N cells for N tenants), not a free side effect of picking a bigger N. Any cell that still holds more than one tenant needs its OWN answer to noisy neighbours — a quota or a fair-share scheduler sized to that cell's own C — which is what this lesson supplies and cell count alone does not.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: members of a population share one pool of a resource; a member taking far more than an equal share degrades the resource for every OTHER member, purely through the pool's own finite capacity, with no direct interaction between members required. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Water pressure in a shared apartment building's plumbing. Every unit draws from one shared pipe and pump system sized for typical, staggered usage across all units. If one unit runs an unusually long shower, fills a large tub, and runs the washing machine all at once — far more than its "equal share" of the building's flow — every OTHER unit's water pressure drops, even units on a completely different floor whose own usage has not changed at all. No pipe connects apartment 4B's faucet to apartment 2A's decision to run a bath; the only connection is the shared pump's finite total flow, exactly like a shared queue's finite aggregate capacity. A building fixes this the same three ways this lesson computes: flow restrictors per unit (a hard quota), a pressure-balancing valve system that guarantees every unit a minimum flow while letting idle units' unused flow go to whoever is drawing more (fair-share), or separate plumbing risers per wing (cell-based-architecture's full isolation) — cheaper coverage first, full separation only where it's worth paying for.
    Did you have that?
