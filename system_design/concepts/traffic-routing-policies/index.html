Tier 1 · The network Routing policies explained well nowhere else

A 50/50 weight in DNS sends between 19% and 81% of your traffic to one side

Weighted, latency-based, geo and health-checked DNS are sold as global load balancing. They divide resolutions, not requests, and nothing in the loop ever observes a queue. At 8,000 requests per second behind a 300-second TTL, one routing decision commits 80,001 requests — and the realised split's standard deviation depends on the resolver population, not on your weight. You would need 2,401 equally-sized resolvers to land within two points.

Read first — dns-resolutiondns-record-types

01

The weight says 50/50. The graphs say 81/19, and nobody changed anything.

Two regions, both live, both sized for half of peak. The DNS provider’s weighted policy is set to 50/50 and has been for months. Requests arrive at about 8,000 per second and the record’s TTL is 300 seconds.

At 14:00 the region-A dashboard reads 81% of traffic. At 14:20 it reads 34%. Nobody deployed, nobody touched the weights, and the provider’s own console cheerfully reports that it answered 49.97% of resolutions with region A over the last day — which is true.

Then a real incident. Region A’s addresses stop answering at 09:00:00. The health check is every 30 seconds and requires three consecutive failures. The record is withdrawn. And:

  • Until 09:01:20 every request that was going to region A is still going to region A. Nothing has been withdrawn yet, because nothing knows.
  • At 09:02:50 the last honouring client has moved.
  • At 11:00 — two hours later — a small, steady 0.5% of requests is still arriving at the dead addresses, and it does not decay.

The postmortem asks for a shorter TTL. That request is the reason this lesson exists.

02

Lower the TTL, raise the resolver count, average the graph — three fixes, and what each one is worth

The mental model almost everyone starts with is that DNS is a load balancer with a slow control plane: it does the same job as a balancer, just with a lag equal to the TTL. Under that model every problem above is a tuning problem, and the TTL is the dial.

It is not one balancer with lag. It is one decision per resolution instead of one per request, made by something that never sees a request. Those are different machines.

The previous two lessons established the caching half of this — TTLs in series add, negative answers have their own clock, a resolver may serve expired data for days (dns-resolution), and a provider that polls on your behalf inserts its own TTL and its own geography (dns-record-types). None of that is re-derived here. What is new is what happens when you try to use the same mechanism as a controller.

Start with the arithmetic of granularity. At 8,000 requests per second, a 300-second TTL and 30 resolvers, src/sim/routing-policy.mjs gives:

one cached answer serves 80,001 requests · the whole system makes 0.1 routing decisions per second against 8,000 requests per second

That is one routing decision per 80,000 requests. A load balancer makes 8,000 per second. The gap is not a lag, it is six orders of magnitude of granularity, and it is why the three fixes below do what they do.

Lowering the TTL buys the decay and not the blind period. In the failover above, 80 of the 170 seconds are spent before anything is published; the TTL only governs the 90 after. Cut the TTL stack from 90 seconds to 2 and the worst removal goes from 170 s to 18 s — real, and it does nothing to the 0.5% that never leaves.

Raising the resolver count works, and the number is much larger than anyone guesses. It is in the rig, and the prediction below is exactly that question.

Averaging the graph is not a fix at all, and it is the one that gets shipped, because it makes the graph look right. Drag the averaging window in the rig.

Predict A 50/50 weight, and a resolver population of exactly 12 resolvers each carrying one twelfth of your requests. Each resolver caches one answer per TTL and serves it to everyone behind it. In 90% of instants, where does the share of requests reaching pool A land?
The realised share is S = Σ sᵢXᵢ with Xᵢ ~ Bernoulli(0.5), so Var[S] = w(1−w)/Neff = 0.25/12 and the standard deviation is 14.43 points. At ±1.645 standard deviations that is roughly ±24 points, and enumerating all 4,096 resolver states gives a 90% range of exactly 25% to 75%. The first option is the belief this lesson breaks — it is right about the mean, which is exactly 50%, and that is what makes it survive. The last option is what happens as the population concentrates: with one resolver carrying most of the traffic the effective count falls to 1.3 and the range becomes 3% to 98%.

03

A thermostat that cannot feel the room

A thermostat works because it measures the thing it controls. It turns the heating on, the room warms, the sensor notices, it turns the heating off. Remove the sensor and you have a timer: it can still be set to “heat for twelve minutes an hour”, and on average the room will be about right, and on any given afternoon it will be too hot or too cold with no idea which.

DNS-based routing is the timer. The authoritative nameserver is told the ratio and it applies the ratio faithfully to every question it is asked. What it never learns is whether the room got warm — whether the requests actually arrived where it sent them, and whether the pool it sent them to is coping.

And there is a second thing wrong with the timer, which is subtler and matters more. The heating does not come on for a moment; it comes on for twelve minutes at a time, in a handful of large blocks per day. A ratio applied in a few coarse blocks is right on average and wrong at every instant, and the fewer and larger the blocks, the wider the swing. That is the resolver population: each resolver is one block, and its size is how many clients sit behind it.

04

The mechanism: a weight divides resolutions, and Σ shareᵢ² decides the rest

Write it down. There are n resolvers; resolver i carries share sᵢ of your request stream. Every TTL it re-queries, and a weighted authoritative answer hands it pool A with probability w. Between refreshes it hands its answer to everyone behind it. So the share of requests reaching pool A at any instant is

S = Σ sᵢ Xᵢ,   Xᵢ ~ Bernoulli(w)  ⟹  E[S] = w  and  Var[S] = w(1−w) · Σ sᵢ²

Two things follow, and they are the whole lesson.

The mean is exactly the weight. Not approximately — exactly, for any population, any TTL, any number of resolvers. This is why weighted DNS is not obviously broken and why the provider’s console is telling the truth when it says 49.97%. Every long-run average confirms the configuration.

The variance depends on the population through one number. Define the effective number of resolvers as the inverse Herfindahl index:

Neff = 1 ÷ Σ shareᵢ²  ⟹  sd(S) = √(w(1−w) / Neff)

With n equal shares Neff is exactly n. Concentration destroys it: in the model’s three populations, thirty resolvers with two large ones have Neff 6.7; eight resolvers with one carrying 86% of traffic have Neff 1.3; sixty equal ones have Neff 60. The test grades splitStats against exhaustive enumeration over all 2ⁿ resolver states to 1e−12, and against a seeded simulation that walks real cache phases, so three independent computations meet at these numbers.

Invert it and you get the population a working weight would need:

Neff ≥ z² w(1−w) / tol²

Why there is nothing to correct it

The five DNS policies differ in what they decide and are identical in what they observe:

RFC 1794 (1993) is the document that proposed rotating an address set as load balancing, and it is honest about the limits in a way the feature names since have not been. Note that latency-based routing measures the wrong endpoint and the wrong quantity: a nearby region that is queueing badly still wins on round-trip time, because RTT to a resolver says nothing about the depth of a queue behind it. load-balancing covers which algorithm to choose once you have per-request decisions and a load signal; the claim here is prior to that one, and it is that DNS has neither.

Health-checked failover: four terms, and only three of them are durations

The four terms, and who owns each:

Detection is interval × (failures − ½) on average, because the failure lands uniformly inside a check interval — so the wait to the first failing check is half an interval and each confirmation after it costs a whole one. At 30 seconds and three failures that is 75 s mean, 90 s worst.

The fourth term is not a duration. dns-resolution established that some clients cache with no expiry at all — a JVM whose networkaddress.cache.ttl was left at −1, and more commonly a connection pool that resolved a name once and now holds a live socket, which is not a DNS cache at all and has no TTL to honour. Those clients put a floor under the curve.

Health check every 30 s, three failures, 5 s to publish, a 30 s stub TTL over a 60 s resolver TTL, measured over a one-hour horizon. Two things to take from it.

The floor is the availability ceiling, exactly. One tenth of one percent of your traffic behind something that never re-resolves, and 99.9% is unreachable — not slow, unreachable, because the curve never goes below the floor.

And it is unreachable at every TTL. Swap the 30/60 s stack for a 1/1 s stack and an aggressive 10-second check: the worst removal falls from 170 s to 18 s, error-seconds in the hour fall from 142 to 33, and the ceiling stays at 99.50%. The model is asserted to behave this way across a thirty-fold TTL sweep in routing-policy.test.mjs, so if it ever stopped being true the test would say so.

Exact, by enumerating all 256 resolver states. The worst state is every resolver holding pool A at once, which at 50% capacity is 2.00× — and unlike a bound, that state has a real probability attached. Lower the weight to the headroom line, which is the obvious fix, and 47.5% of instants are still above it: the weight moved the mean and left the spread alone.

One result here is worth flagging because it goes the other way from the intuition the rest of the lesson builds. More resolvers is not always safer. If the weight already puts the mean above the headroom line, a tighter distribution puts more mass above it, not less — the concentrated population’s P(over) at a 50% pool is 69.8% while the sixty-equal-resolver population’s is 92.1%. Variance is only your friend when it is variance around a mean that is inside the line. Getting the mean right and the spread wrong is a different failure from getting the mean wrong.

Geolocation, and the mean that hides it

RFC 7871 specifies EDNS Client Subnet: the resolver passes a truncated prefix of the client’s address to the authoritative server so it can answer for the client’s location. It is optional at both ends, it is a privacy trade a resolver may decline, and any forwarder in the middle can drop it. Without it the answer is chosen for the resolver’s position.

Using the same RTT matrix as dns-record-types rather than a second one, with 15% of clients on an off-region resolver:

The middle column is what a dashboard shows and the fourth is what happens to a user. The ratio between them is exactly 1 ÷ affected share, so improving ECS coverage makes the remaining error more invisible without making it smaller for anyone it lands on — the conditional penalty is 123 ms in every row. At roughly three round trips before the first byte of application data on a new HTTPS connection, that is about 370 ms of added connection setup. Asia-Pacific clients served from a US-East PoP pay +198 ms per round trip. dns-record-types prices pinning one PoP for everybody; this is a fraction of clients pinned to the wrong one, which is the same distance with a far more misleading mean.

When does none of this matter? When the split is not load-bearing. Weighted DNS is genuinely good at coarse, slow, unidirectional shifts: draining a region over an hour, moving a percentage to a new provider over a week, splitting between two things that are both sized for all of it. The cases it is bad at are the ones where the ratio matters within a window shorter than a few hundred TTLs, or where one side cannot absorb the other’s share.

06

The trade-off: what you give up by not using DNS for this

One address with a real balancer behind it costs you a component you now operate. Publish one address, put an anycast front door or a load balancer behind it, and every decision after the first is made per request by something that can see a queue. What you have bought is a device in the request path — one that has capacity, a failure mode, a bill, and an upgrade schedule. DNS’s great virtue is that it is not in the path: after the answer is cached your nameserver can be down for the whole incident and nothing notices. Moving the decision into the path moves the risk into the path. anycast is the version of this where the failover is a BGP withdrawal rather than a record change, which removes the TTL term and not the client-honour term.

Keeping DNS and shrinking the TTL costs queries and buys only the decay. A 30-second TTL over a population that would otherwise cache for 300 seconds multiplies authoritative query volume roughly tenfold, and dns-record-types shows why the caching was load-bearing: the hit rate of a TTL cache is λT/(1+λT), so the term you are shrinking is the one that was making resolution free. You get a faster decay onto the same floor, and you pay for it in query volume at the one component whose unavailability you were trying to route around.

Closing the loop with a controller — a GSLB, or a script that rewrites weights from metrics — costs you a control plane with its own failure modes. It genuinely helps: it shortens detection and it can correct a weight against observed load. What it cannot do is change the granularity or the floor, because those live in the resolver and the client. And a controller that rewrites DNS from metrics has the classic hazard: it observes a shared, stale signal and acts on it in lockstep, which is the oscillation load-balancing describes for synchronised balancers, now with a 300-second cycle time instead of a 250-millisecond one.

Accepting the spread costs you capacity. The honest version of weighted DNS is to size both pools for the p99 of the realised share rather than for the weight. On the eight-resolver population that is 1.92× capacity at a 50/50 weight — so “50/50 across two regions” means provisioning each region for roughly all of peak, and the weight was never buying you a smaller region. This is the trade most teams have made without noticing, and the ones who noticed usually discovered it during a failover of the other region.

And the honest ceiling: the floor is not yours to remove. Detection is yours, publishing is your provider’s, the TTLs are the previous two lessons’ subject. The fraction of clients that never re-resolve is a property of software you do not control, running versions you do not know, and the only lever on it is not needing them to re-resolve — which means not encoding the routing decision in a name at all.

07

In the wild

RFC 1794 (1993) named this as a technique and its limitations in the same document. “DNS Support for Load Balancing” describes shuffling an address set on each answer and is explicit that the ordering is advisory, that caching resolvers will hold one ordering for the TTL, and that clients may reorder or ignore the set. Everything in this lesson is a quantification of a caveat that was written down the year the technique was standardised. Thirty years of product naming has not changed the mechanism; it has changed how confident the console looks.

RFC 7871 (2016) exists because operators had already deployed the workaround. EDNS Client Subnet was published as Informational, documenting what large CDNs and public resolvers were already doing, and its Security Considerations section is unusually direct that the feature trades client privacy for routing accuracy. That is why coverage is a policy question rather than a protocol question, and why “geoproximity is configured correctly and some users are on the wrong continent” is the expected outcome rather than a misconfiguration.

The pinned-client floor is the reason large properties moved the decision out of DNS. The architectural pattern that replaced DNS-based global balancing is a small number of stable anycast addresses published with long TTLs, with all of the routing decided inside the network by BGP and by per-request balancers at the edge. Read against this lesson, that design is not an optimisation — it is the only arrangement in which the routing decision does not have to travel through a cache you do not own to a client that may ignore it. The TTL goes up, not down, because the address is no longer the thing that changes.

And the failure mode with the best paper trail is the one where DNS was the control plane during a DNS incident. dns-resolution covers the mechanism — RFC 8767 permits serving expired records precisely when the authoritative servers are unreachable. Combine that with this lesson: if your failover plan is a record change, then during any incident that touches your nameservers your control plane and your fault are the same system, and the standard behaviour of every compliant resolver is to keep serving the answer you are trying to replace.

  • RFC 1794, DNS Support for Load Balancing, 1993 — the original description of address-set rotation as load balancing, and its own account of why resolver caching and client behaviour limit it.
  • RFC 7871, Client Subnet in DNS Queries, 2016 — EDNS Client Subnet, its optionality at both ends, and its explicit privacy-versus-accuracy trade.
  • RFC 2308, Negative Caching of DNS Queries, 1998 — the negative-cache rule, reused from dns-resolution rather than restated.
  • RFC 8767, Serving Stale Data to Improve DNS Resiliency, 2018 — why a DNS control plane degrades in exactly the incident it exists for. Modelled in dns-resolution.
  • Herfindahl–Hirschman index — the 1 ÷ Σ shareᵢ² used here as the effective number of resolvers is the concentration measure from industrial economics (Hirschman, 1945; Herfindahl, 1950), and the reason it appears is that it is exactly the coefficient that multiplies w(1−w) in the variance.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A weighted DNS policy divides resolutions, not requests, so the realised traffic split is the weight convolved with the population of clients behind each resolver
  • The realised split has mean exactly equal to the weight and standard deviation √(w(1−w)/Neff), where Neff = 1 ÷ Σ shareᵢ² is the effective number of resolvers
  • Because the tolerance enters as 1/tol², landing a 50/50 split within ±2 points needs 2,401 equally-sized resolvers
  • Averaging over one whole TTL removes only a third of the variance, so a dashboard that looks right is averaging the problem away rather than fixing it
  • Health-checked failover cannot be faster than detect + publish + the stacked TTLs, and a fraction of clients never re-resolve at all
  • Clients that never re-resolve put a floor under the error rate that no TTL reaches, so the floor — not the TTL — decides whether an availability target is achievable
  • Geolocation routes the resolver, not the client, unless EDNS Client Subnet is honoured end to end — so the error is bimodal rather than a small offset
  • DNS has no feedback loop: nothing in the resolution path observes queue depth, so an open-loop weight over unequal capacity is never corrected

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    You set a 50/50 weight across two regions. Traffic behind you arrives through a population of resolvers in which the two largest carry half your requests between them. What is the realised split, and which number in that sentence decided it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not 50/50. The realised share of requests reaching one side is S = Σ sᵢXᵢ where Xᵢ is 1 when resolver i currently holds that answer, so E[S] = w exactly and Var[S] = w(1−w)·Σ sᵢ². The only property of the population that enters is Neff = 1 ÷ Σ sᵢ², the effective number of resolvers — the inverse Herfindahl index. For the concentrated shape in the model, 30 resolvers have an effective count of 6.7, a standard deviation of 19.4 points, and a 90% range of 19% to 81%. Put one corporate resolver in front of everything and Neff falls to 1.3, the deviation rises to 43.1 points and the range is 3% to 98% — the split is essentially all-or-nothing, decided by one cache. The weight decided the mean and nothing else.

  2. Cued recall

    How many resolvers would you need before a 50/50 weight lands within ±2 points, and why does tightening the target hurt so much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    2,401 equally-sized independent resolvers. Setting z·√(w(1−w)/Neff) ≤ tol and solving gives Neff ≥ z²w(1−w)/tol², so the tolerance enters as 1/tol²: ±5 points needs 385, ±2 needs 2,401, ±1 needs 9,604. Halving what you will accept quadruples the population you need. And Neff is not the resolver count — it is the count after concentration, which is why a real population of thousands of resolvers can have an effective count in single figures.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your dashboard averages the split over five minutes and it reads close to 50/50. Has the problem gone away?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — the averaging has. Over a window of k whole TTLs the variance falls by exactly (k − ⅓)/k². At k = 1 that is 2/3: averaging over one entire TTL removes a third of the variance and the standard deviation is still 82% of the instantaneous one. Two hours of averaging at a five-minute TTL — k = 24 — leaves 20%. For the concentrated population the 90% range goes from 19–81% instantaneous to 24–76% over one TTL and only 43–56% over two hours. Your capacity has to survive the instantaneous number; the dashboard is showing you the averaged one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Pool A is sized for half your peak load and you weight it 50/50. How often is it over its 80% headroom, and does lowering the weight fix it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    On the eight-resolver population in the model, whose effective count is 5.1, pool A is above 80% of its own capacity in 65.6% of instants — the mean utilisation is exactly 1.00, so half the mass is above the mean and most of the rest is above the headroom line too. The 99th-percentile utilisation is 1.92× capacity and the worst enumerated state is 2.00×: every resolver holding pool A at once, which is a state with real probability rather than a bound. Lowering the weight to the headroom line — 40% — moves the mean onto the line and still leaves 47.5% of instants above it, because the weight sets the mean and the population sets the spread. Nothing in DNS observes the queue that is growing, so no correction happens at all.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A health check catches a dead address in three consecutive failures at 30-second intervals. Your TTLs are 30 s at the stub and 60 s at the resolver. How long is the dead address served, and what is the piece this arithmetic leaves out?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Detection averages 75 s — the failure lands uniformly inside a check interval, so the wait to the first failing check is half an interval and each confirmation costs a whole one, giving interval × (failures − ½). Add 5 s to publish and the stacked TTLs' worst case of 90 s, and the worst removal is 170 s, of which 80 s is completely blind. Over an hour that is 125 error-seconds, or 96.53% availability for that hour. What it leaves out is the clients that never re-resolve: a connection pool holding a live socket, or a runtime cache with no expiry. Those put a floor under the curve. At 0.1% pinned traffic the ceiling on availability is 99.90% and a 99.9% target is already unreachable; at 0.5% the ceiling is 99.50%, and dropping the TTL stack from 90 s to 2 s changes the worst removal from 170 s to 18 s and changes the ceiling not at all.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    Geoproximity routing is configured correctly and a measurable set of users are still served from the wrong continent. Why, and why does the mean latency look fine?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because an authoritative server sees the resolver's address, not the client's. RFC 7871's EDNS Client Subnet exists to pass a truncated client prefix along, and it is optional at both ends and droppable by any forwarder in between. So a client whose resolver appears to be elsewhere is geolocated to the resolver. The error is bimodal, not noisy: in the model, with 15% of clients on an off-region resolver and ECS honoured for half of them, 4.65% of traffic is misrouted, the fleet-mean penalty is 5.73 ms — a rounding error on any dashboard — and the clients it lands on pay 123 ms per round trip, about 370 ms before the first byte of a new HTTPS connection. The ratio between the two is exactly 1 ÷ affected share, so the rarer the misrouting the more invisible it is and the worse it is for whoever has it. Asia-Pacific clients served from a US-East PoP pay +198 ms.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team splits traffic between two versions of a service using a feature flag whose value is fetched once per process at start-up and held for the process's lifetime. They set the flag to 10% and the observed split is 4%. Someone proposes setting it to 25% to compensate. What is actually wrong, and what is wrong with the proposal?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The flag divides processes, not requests — exactly as a DNS weight divides resolutions rather than requests. The realised request share is Σ sᵢXᵢ over processes weighted by how much traffic each one serves, so its mean is the flag value and its variance is w(1−w)·Σ sᵢ², set by the effective number of processes: a fleet of forty processes behind an uneven balancer, or ten large ones, has an effective count in single figures and a deviation of many points. Raising the flag to 25% moves the mean to 25% and leaves the spread exactly where it was, so the next observation is as likely to read 40% as 12% — and if 4% was drawn from a 10% mean, compensating has just biased the experiment. Two structural fixes, and they are the same two DNS has: decide per request (evaluate the flag on each request rather than at start-up, which is the equivalent of putting a real balancer behind one address), or close the loop (measure the realised share and correct the flag, which is feedback DNS cannot have because it never sees the requests).
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a controller sets a ratio, but the decision is committed in coarse batches by intermediaries the controller cannot see and never hears from again — so the ratio holds on average and the realised outcome's spread is set by how few and how unequal the intermediaries are, not by the ratio. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A political party's national vote share against its seat count. The party campaigns for 40% of the vote and gets it, and the seats it wins are decided constituency by constituency, in lumps, by a few hundred districts of very unequal size. The mean is right and the variance is set by the number and unevenness of the districts — which is why a uniform national swing produces wildly different parliaments, and why the party with more votes can win fewer seats. The two fixes are the same two: make the decision finer (proportional representation counts votes rather than districts) or close the loop (nothing in a first-past-the-post election observes the seat total until it is over, which is exactly DNS's problem).

    Other instances: a factory's scrap rate set per batch rather than per unit, where a 2% target is met on average and any single shift is 0% or 8% because the decision that matters is made once per lot; a school's intended class-size ratio, which is committed once a year in whole classes and so realises as 24 or 31 rather than 28; and a fund's target asset allocation rebalanced quarterly, where the intended 60/40 is the mean and the realised exposure between rebalances is whatever the market did — which is why the interesting question about a rebalancing rule is never the target but the drift band, the exact quantity this lesson calls the spread.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
