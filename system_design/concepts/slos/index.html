Tier 6 · Running it in production SLOs and error budgets explained well nowhere else

The alert that wakes you for nothing and sleeps through the outage

Your alert fired at 3am for an event that cost you three tenths of a second of the month's reliability, and then it stayed quiet for ten days while a slow burn ate the entire budget. Both behaviours come from the same mistake: it is watching a rate and the thing that matters is a quantity.

Read first — percentiles

01

Two failures, one alert, and it is configured correctly

The service has a 99.9% availability objective over a rolling 30 days. The alert is the one everybody writes first: page if the error rate over the last five minutes exceeds 0.1%. It is derived directly from the SLO. Nobody made an arithmetic mistake.

March 3rd, 03:14. A dependency has a bad thirty seconds. 0.4% of requests fail for five minutes. The alert fires, somebody gets out of bed, and by the time they have a laptop open the graph is flat. They write “transient, no action” in the incident channel and go back to sleep.

March 8th through 18th. A slow memory leak in one replica of nine is failing 0.3% of requests, continuously, for ten days. The alert never fires — 0.3% is above 0.1%, but the five-minute window is noisy and the condition flaps, so it was given a “for 10m” clause months ago and now it never latches. On the 18th the monthly report shows the availability objective missed. Nobody was ever paged.

Look at what those two events actually cost, because it is not close:

EventError rateDurationBudget spent
The 3am page0.4%5 minutes0.046% of the month
The silent leak0.3%10 days100% of the month

One of these is 2,160 times more expensive than the other, and the alert reacted to the cheap one. Note also that the cheap one had the higher error rate. That is not a tuning error. The alert is watching the error rate, and the error rate is not the thing you are managing.

02

The number is a quantity you are allowed to spend

The belief this has to break
99.9% is a line the error rate has to stay under.

It is a budget. Over 30 days, 99.9% permits 0.1% of requests to fail — which, if you express it as time rather than requests, is 43.2 minutes of total outage. That budget can be spent as 43 minutes of everything broken, or 7.2 hours at a 10% error rate, or a fortnight at 0.2%. Those are the same amount of unreliability, and a threshold alert reacts to them completely differently.

ObjectiveBudget over 30 daysOver a year
99%7 hours 12 minutes3 days 15 hours
99.9%43.2 minutes8 hours 46 minutes
99.99%4.32 minutes52.6 minutes
99.999%25.9 seconds5.26 minutes

Once it is a quantity, the interesting signal is the rate you are spending it at — and the natural unit for that rate is multiples of the rate that would spend it exactly:

burn rate = observed error rate ÷ (1 − SLO)

Burn rate 1 means you finish the month having used the budget exactly, with nothing left and nothing overspent. Burn rate 2 means you run out on day 15. Burn rate 1000 means you run out in 43.2 minutes — the same 43.2, because spending the whole budget a thousand times faster takes a thousandth of the period.

That number is dimensionless, and that is what makes it worth having. A 99% service at burn rate 6 and a 99.999% service at burn rate 6 are in the same amount of trouble, and one alerting configuration describes both.

Which lets us price the alert from the top of the lesson exactly. It fires when the 5-minute error rate exceeds 0.1% — burn rate 1 — so consider the very smallest event that trips it: five minutes at exactly burn rate 1 consumes 5 ÷ 43,200 of the period, which is 0.0116% of the budget, or 0.30 seconds out of 43.2 minutes.

The threshold alert is calibrated to fire at precisely the moment an event becomes insignificant. That is why it fires constantly and why nobody trusts it. And raising the threshold does not repair it — that relocates the same defect and opens a blind spot for slow burns, which is the second failure from slot 1.

03

The intuition: nobody alerts on download speed

Your phone has a monthly data allowance. It does not warn you when a download is fast. It warns you when you are on track to run out before the month does — and that judgement needs three things: how fast you are consuming, how much is left, and how much month is left. Speed alone is meaningless. A 4K film at maximum throughput for four minutes is fine. A background sync at a hundredth of that speed, running all month, is what empties the allowance.

That is the entire idea, and the phone gets it right while the monitoring system gets it wrong.

An error rate is a speed. An error budget is a distance. You cannot tell whether a speed is a problem without knowing how far you still have to go and how much road is left — and burn rate is exactly the number that combines all three into one.

The second half of the analogy matters too. “You will exhaust the allowance tonight” and “you will exhaust it by the 28th” are both worth knowing and they are not the same alert. One wants somebody awake now; the other wants a ticket in the morning. A single threshold cannot express both, and that is why what follows has more than one tier rather than one well-tuned number.

04

The mechanism: one relationship, and where 14.4 comes from

Everything in burn-rate alerting is one line:

budget consumed = burn rate × (window ÷ period)

Read it forwards and it tells you what an incident cost. Read it backwards and it tells you what threshold to set. That backwards reading is the useful one, because it converts a judgement you can actually make — how much of the month am I willing to lose before somebody gets woken up? — into an error rate a monitoring system can compare against:

burn rate = budget fraction × period ÷ window

Google’s SRE Workbook recommends three tiers. Every number in them falls out of that line and a choice of how much budget each tier is allowed to cost:

Willing to loseOver a window of⇒ burn rateAt 99.9%, compare againstAction
2% of the month1 hour0.02 × 720 ÷ 1 = 14.41.44% errorspage
5% of the month6 hours0.05 × 720 ÷ 6 = 60.60% errorspage
10% of the month3 days0.10 × 720 ÷ 72 = 10.10% errorsticket

14.4 is not a tuned constant. Every blog post quotes it and none of them derive it. It is “2% of a 30-day budget, spent in one hour”, converted into an error rate. Change your mind about the 2% and you get a different number; change the period to a week and you get another. The three burn rates are the only portable part — thresholds scale with your SLO, burn rates do not.

A rolling window does not see an incident instantly, and does not stop seeing it

This is where the arithmetic gets genuinely interesting, and where most treatments stop.

During an incident at error rate r starting at t = 0, a window of length W contains bad events across r·t/W of its span. The observed rate ramps linearly, and crosses the threshold at

t_detect = W × threshold ÷ r

For the 1-hour, 14.4× tier facing a total outage: 3600 × 0.0144 ÷ 1 = 51.8 seconds.

Something quietly remarkable happens at that moment, and it is the actual theorem behind the whole scheme. 51.8 seconds of total outage is 51.8 ÷ 2592 = exactly 2% of the budget — which is the number the tier was designed around. Substitute the detection formula into the budget line and the incident’s error rate cancels out completely:

budget at firing = (r ÷ (1−SLO)) × (W × br × (1−SLO) ÷ r) ÷ period = br × W ÷ period

So a burn-rate alert fires when exactly its design fraction of the budget has been spent, no matter how fast the incident is burning. A total outage trips the fast tier in 52 seconds and a 2% error rate trips it in 43 minutes, and both have cost precisely 2% of the month when the page goes out. That is what a threshold alert cannot do at any setting: it fires at a rate, which corresponds to no fixed amount of damage.

Now the half nobody mentions. When the incident ends, those bad events are still inside the window. Nothing has removed them; the window has to slide past them. So before reading on, commit to a number:

Predict A 99.9% SLO alerting on burn rate 14.4 over a rolling 1-hour window. A total outage begins, pages you 52 seconds later, and the service fully recovers at the 2-minute mark. When does the alert stop firing?
The window is rolling, so those two bad minutes stay inside it until they age out. The observed rate decays as the window slides past the incident, and it only falls back under 1.44% when almost the entire hour has passed — precisely W × (1 − threshold ÷ r) = 3600 × (1 − 0.0144) = 59.1 minutes after recovery, clearing at 61.1 minutes. Two minutes of outage, an hour of paging, and nothing is misconfigured. The rig below has a switch that fixes it; the arithmetic is asserted in src/sim/slo.test.mjs against a stepped simulation that shares no code with the formula.
Show it without answering

The closed form for it is the detection formula’s mirror image:

t_reset = W × (1 − threshold ÷ r)

The threshold is small — 1.44% — so 1 − threshold/r sits very close to 1, and reset time is almost the entire window for any serious incident. That is a property of every rolling-window alert ever written, and it is invisible on a dashboard because dashboards draw the incident, not the alert.

The fix is a second, shorter window over the same threshold, with the alert requiring both. Now it clears as soon as either window drops below the threshold, and the short one drops twelve times sooner: 4.9 minutes instead of 59.1.

And here is the result that makes this the recommendation rather than a trade-off. Because the ramp is linear in t/W, a shorter window crosses the same threshold sooner, always — so requiring both leaves detection governed entirely by the long window. The short window improves reset time by 12× and costs exactly nothing in detection time. There is no dial being traded here, which is rare enough to be worth noticing.

The two rows in bold are the interesting ones, and they are interesting for opposite reasons.

Row one is silent and that is correct. Burn rate 5 sounds alarming — five times the rate that would spend the budget exactly — and the event costs 1.5 seconds out of 43.2 minutes. Both facts are true, and only one of them should wake anybody. This is the exact event the naive threshold alert pages for.

Row four is silent on the pager and that is also correct. Burn rate 3 is under the fastest tier’s 1.44%, so no page can see it, and a page would be the wrong response anyway: 10 days is not an emergency. The reader who wants to “fix” the page tier so it catches this has misdiagnosed — the gap is deliberate, and closing it is what the second tier is for.

Note also what the third row shows about the relationship between severity and speed: a 40 minute incident at 60% errors spends more than half the month’s budget, and the fastest tier still took 86 seconds to say so. There is no configuration in which you learn about a catastrophe instantly; the window has to fill.

The number can be meaningless before you write any alert at all

An availability objective is a claim about a proportion, and a proportion estimated from a small number of events has a confidence interval. At low traffic that interval is wider than the budget, and the monthly verdict is noise.

Read the first row carefully. The true availability is exactly on target, and the month could still record 43 failures — 43% over a budget of 30 — or 21, a third under. The measurement cannot resolve a 40% budget overrun. Any argument about whether that month met the objective is an argument about sampling noise.

The threshold is around 61,000 requests per 30-day period — roughly 2,000 a day — before a 99.9% objective can distinguish meeting from missing with a confidence interval narrower than a quarter of the budget. And the requirement scales as 1/(1 − SLO): four nines needs ten times the traffic of three. A service can be too small to have the SLO it has written down, and no amount of alerting configuration fixes that.

06

The trade-off: four properties, two knobs, and the one that is not a trade

An alerting rule has four properties worth naming, and they are not independent:

  • Precision — of the alerts that fire, how many were worth acting on.
  • Recall — of the events worth acting on, how many fired.
  • Detection time — how long from onset to firing.
  • Reset time — how long from recovery to clearing.

Lengthening the window improves precision and makes both time properties worse. Raising the burn-rate threshold improves precision and costs recall. There is no single window that is good at all four, which is why the recommended configuration has six expressions rather than one, and why every attempt to arrive at one perfect number fails.

The user’s availability is the product, and it is nobody’s dashboard

A request that touches five services sees all five of them. If each holds 99.9%, the journey holds the product:

0.999⁵ = 99.501%

Whose numberAvailabilityBudget over 30 days
Each of the five services99.9%43.2 minutes
What the user experiences99.501%3 hours 36 minutes

Every team met its objective. The user got five times the failure. This is the same multiplicative structure as retry amplification across hops, and it fails the same way: everybody measures their own component, and the product appears on no dashboard because it belongs to no team.

Inverted, it is worse than it looks. To give the user 99.9% end to end across five services, each one needs 99.98% — 0.999^(1/5) — which is an 8.6-minute monthly budget each. Adding a sixth service to the path silently tightens every existing service’s real requirement.

07

In the wild

The Workbook’s own caveat is the one people skip. The six-expression configuration exists because the five simpler ones each fail in a specific, nameable way. It is worth knowing the ladder rather than just the destination, because most production systems are standing on one of the middle rungs:

ApproachWhat it fixesWhat it still gets wrong
1. Error rate over 5 minutes—Fires for 0.012% of the budget
2. Same threshold, 36-hour windowPrecisionDetects in hours, then fires for most of 36 more
3. Short window plus “for 10 minutes”Reset timeA duration clause is not additive — misses a 9-minute total outage, and resets on one good minute
4. Burn rate over 1 hourDetection timeBlind to a slow burn: 3× forever never fires
5. Several burn ratesRecallSlow tiers still take most of their own window to reset
6. Multiwindow, multi-burn-rateReset timeNothing this arithmetic can see — but it is six expressions per SLO

Approach 3 deserves special attention because it is the most popular one in the wild and its defect is the least obvious: a “for N minutes” clause does not accumulate. A nine-minute total outage never satisfies a ten-minute condition, and a single healthy minute in the middle of a half-hour incident resets the timer to zero. It looks like it is measuring duration. It is measuring uninterrupted duration, which is a different and much weaker thing.

An SLO with no policy attached is decoration. The budget’s purpose is to be spent — a team that ends every month with 100% of its budget unused has an objective that is too loose, or is buying reliability nobody asked for. The number becomes load-bearing only when something happens at exhaustion: a feature freeze, a mandatory reliability sprint, releases requiring sign-off. Google’s framing is that the error budget is what converts an argument between product and infrastructure into an arithmetic question, and the conversion only works if both sides agreed in advance what running out means.

Availability is rarely the right SLI. For anything user-facing, a latency objective — “99% of requests under 300 ms” — usually correlates with experience far better than a success rate, because a service that answers every request in nine seconds is 100% available and completely broken. Burn-rate alerting works identically on a latency SLI: the “bad events” are just requests over the threshold instead of requests that errored, and everything in this lesson applies unchanged.

Windowing choice is a policy, not a detail. A rolling 30-day window means a bad Tuesday keeps hurting for 30 days and then stops abruptly; a calendar-month window means the budget resets on the 1st, which teams learn to exploit. Neither is wrong, but they produce different behaviour at the end of a bad month and the choice should be deliberate.

The reframing worth keeping: an error rate is a speed and an error budget is a distance. Every failure in this lesson — the 3am page for nothing, the ten-day leak nobody saw, the alert that outlived its outage by an hour, the SLO that could not be measured, the journey nobody owned — is what happens when a system reasons about one and reports the other.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • An error budget is a quantity, not a threshold — 99.9% over 30 days is 43.2 minutes of total outage
  • Burn rate is the error rate divided by (1 − SLO): dimensionless, so one tier configuration fits any SLO
  • Budget consumed = burn rate × window ÷ period — the single relationship 14.4, 6 and 1 are all derived from
  • A rolling window keeps firing for almost its entire length after the incident ends
  • The short window exists to turn the alert off, and it costs nothing in detection time
  • A journey's availability is the product of its components', so five services at 99.9% give the user 99.5%
  • Below roughly 2,000 requests a day a 99.9% SLO cannot distinguish meeting from missing

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A two-minute total outage pages you 52 seconds in. Why is the alert still firing an hour later, and what fixes it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the alert evaluates a rolling one-hour window, and the bad minutes stay inside that window until they age out of it. The observed rate decays as the window slides past the incident, and it only drops back under the 1.44% threshold when almost the whole hour has passed — precisely W × (1 − threshold/rate), which for a total outage is 59.1 of the 60 minutes. The fix is to require a second, shorter window over the same threshold: the alert clears as soon as either window falls below it, so a five-minute window brings the overhang down to 4.9 minutes. That is what the short window is for, and it is not "fewer false positives" — the long window's threshold already handles those.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Where does the burn rate 14.4 come from? Derive it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    From choosing how much budget you are willing to lose before waking somebody. Budget consumed = burn rate × window ÷ period. Fix the window at 1 hour, the period at 30 days = 720 hours, and the acceptable loss at 2%, and the burn rate is forced: 0.02 × 720 ÷ 1 = 14.4. The other two rows are the same line: 5% over 6 hours gives 6, and 10% over 3 days gives 1. Nothing was tuned, and nothing about 14.4 is special — change the 2% and you get a different number.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Adding a second window to the alert condition makes it stricter. Why does detection not get slower?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because during an incident at error rate r, a window of length W sees r·t/W — the observed rate ramps linearly in t/W. A shorter window therefore crosses the same threshold sooner, always. Requiring both windows means detection is governed by the slower one, which is always the long window, so the short window is free: it improves reset time and changes detection time by exactly nothing. That is why this configuration is the recommendation rather than a trade-off.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A service is failing 0.3% of requests, indefinitely. Nothing pages. Is the alerting broken?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — that is burn rate 3 against a 99.9% SLO, and 0.3% is well under the 1.44% the fastest tier compares against. It is also not an emergency: it exhausts a 30-day budget in 10 days, which is a problem for Tuesday morning. The slow tier catches it — 72-hour window at burn rate 1 — and files a ticket after about 24 hours. Paging for this would be the mistake; ignoring it entirely would be the other mistake, and having more than one tier is how you get neither.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A team tightens its SLO from 99.9% to 99.99% on the same service, keeping the same alerting configuration. Name two things that quietly break.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    First, every threshold falls by 10×: the fastest tier now compares the observed error rate against 0.144% instead of 1.44%. For many services that is inside the normal noise floor — a single retried request in a low-traffic minute — so the page tier becomes chatty for reasons that have nothing to do with reliability. Second, measurement gets 10× more expensive: the traffic needed to distinguish meeting from missing scales as 1/(1−SLO), so a service that could just about evidence three nines now cannot evidence four at all, and every month's verdict is inside the confidence interval. The burn rates themselves are the only things that survive unchanged, because they are dimensionless.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a rate and a remaining quantity are different signals, and alerting on the rate alone gives you both false alarms and blind spots. Name an instance outside reliability.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A startup's cash burn — literally the same word, and the same arithmetic: monthly spend matters only against runway, so a big one-off purchase is fine and a small permanent overspend is fatal. A phone battery: 8% per hour is alarming at 20% and irrelevant at 100%. A speeding fine measures rate; whether you arrive on time measures quantity, and they disagree constantly. Mobile data caps, calorie deficits, groundwater extraction, a rate limiter's own token bucket. In every case the fix has the same shape — express the rate as a multiple of the rate that would exactly consume the remaining quantity in the remaining time, and alert on that.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
