Tier 2 · Scaling the stateless tier Deployment Strategies

Rolling, blue-green, and canary all need the identical 1,791 exposed requests to catch the same bug — canary spends 179 seconds getting there at a 5% blast radius, blue-green spends 39 seconds at 100%

Rolling, blue-green, canary, and shadow traffic are not four unrelated techniques — they are four schedules for the same variable: what share of a fleet, and of real traffic, is running a new version that might be bad. This lesson shows that detecting a bad version needs a fixed number of real exposures no matter which strategy is running — a one-sample statistical test that doesn't care about your deployment tooling — and that the strategies differ only in how fast wall-clock time gathers that number and what fraction of live users is exposed while it does. It computes that number, the wall-clock cost of reaching it four different ways, and the capacity bill blue-green and shadow traffic pay to change the trade.

01

The canary looked clean for ten minutes. Then it got worse at 25%.

A team ships a subtly bad version — 1% errors against a 0.5% baseline — behind a canary carrying just 1% of the fleet’s 200 requests/second. Ten minutes pass. The dashboard shows nothing unusual. The canary is promoted to 25% of traffic, exactly as the runbook says a clean canary should be. Two minutes later, the pager goes off.

Nothing malfunctioned. Catching this bug requires 1,791 real requests served by the bad version before its error rate clears the baseline’s own statistical noise. At 1% of 200 rps, ten minutes of watching accumulates only 1,200 of those — the canary wasn’t lucky or broken, it was correctly clean, because the sample it was given was too thin to hold a verdict yet. This lesson computes that 1,791-request threshold, shows it doesn’t change no matter which of the four standard rollout strategies is running, and computes what each strategy pays — in time, in exposed users, or in duplicate capacity — to reach it.

02

Assume the strategy you pick is a safety dial, not a schedule

The instinct: blue-green, canary, rolling, and shadow traffic feel like a ladder from “risky” to “safe,” and picking further up the ladder should mean less damage if the new version turns out to be bad. The gap is that all but one of them (shadow) still has to expose some real traffic to find out — and how much evidence that traffic can produce is arithmetic, not a property of the tool.

Predict A bad version (1% error rate vs. a 0.5% baseline) is rolled out at 200 requests/second, once via a 5% canary and once via an instant blue-green cutover. Both need the same 1,791 exposed requests to statistically confirm the bug. Which detects it in less wall-clock time, and which exposes fewer live requests to the bug by the moment of detection?
The 1,791-request threshold is fixed by the bug and the confidence bar, not the strategy — but WHO pays it differs completely. Blue-green is already sending 100% of traffic to the bad version, so it accumulates 1,791 exposures fastest in wall-clock time (about 39 seconds) — meaning every user hitting the service during that window gets the bad version. Canary caps the share at 5%, so it takes longer to accumulate the same count (about 179 seconds) but never exposes more than 5% of live traffic while doing it. Same numerator, very different denominator.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — a 1% error rate against a 0.5% baseline, 200 rps of total traffic — every strategy that exposes real traffic needs the identical 1,791 requests before the bug clears the noise floor. Blue-green reaches it in 39 seconds, already at 100% of the fleet. Rolling reaches it in 75 seconds, with 20% of the fleet flipped. A 5% canary reaches it in 179 seconds (about 3 minutes), never exceeding 5% exposure the whole time. Drop the canary to 1% and it escapes its own first stage — only 1,200 of the needed 1,791 exposures accumulate in a 10-minute window — and is only caught 636 seconds (10.6 minutes) in, after being promoted, unwarned, to 25%. Shadow traffic never appears on this chart at all: its blast radius is 0% throughout, at a cost of 10 instance-hours of duplicate compute — the same order as blue-green’s own 10 instance-hours of running two full fleets side by side.

03

A kitchen testing a new recipe four different ways

A restaurant wants to try a new dish that might not work. Canary puts it on one table’s order tonight, watches how it comes back, and only adds it to more tables once several have gone well. Blue-green swaps the entire printed menu overnight — every table orders off the new menu starting tomorrow, and if the dish flops, the old menu is back by dinner. Rolling phases it in section by section of the dining room, one waiter’s tables at a time, so by the third section a third of the restaurant has already eaten it whether or not anyone’s noticed a problem yet. Shadow traffic is the chef cooking the new dish in the back for every ticket that comes in, tasting it themselves, and serving the old dish to every actual customer — nobody outside the kitchen ever eats it, but the chef also never learns whether a customer would have sent it back for a reason that only shows up once someone’s actually paying for it.

All four are the identical restaurant learning whether a dish works. They differ only in how many real diners have already been served it by the time anyone can tell, and in whether “telling” ever touches a real diner at all.

04

One shared cause: telling signal from noise needs a fixed amount of evidence

THE SCHEDULE          Every strategy is a schedule for fractionOnNew(t) — rolling ramps it in
                       cumulative batches, blue-green jumps it in one switch, canary ramps it in
                       observed stages, shadow holds it at zero for real traffic forever.

THE DETECTION FLOOR    Telling a bug's error rate apart from ordinary noise needs a FIXED number
                       of real exposures — n = z²p0(1-p0)/(p1-p0)² — set by the bug's severity and
                       the confidence bar. Nothing about the deployment strategy changes this
                       number; it only changes how fast traffic accumulates it.

THE RECOVERY FLOOR     Once caught, rollback for every strategy that exposed real traffic is the
                       same mechanism — repoint the load balancer, redeploy the known-good version
                       in parallel — so recovery time converges to roughly the same small constant
                       regardless of which strategy found the bug.

Each row traces back to the same fact: a rollout strategy controls WHO is exposed and HOW FAST, but it does not control how much evidence a fixed bug, at a fixed traffic volume, needs to produce before anyone — human or automated gate — can honestly call it bad.

A barely-worse bug can outrun an entire rolling rollout: at 0.55% vs. a 0.5% baseline, the detection threshold is 179,100 requests. A full 10-batch rolling rollout at 200 rps only ever exposes 66,000 requests to the new version, total, by the time every instance has been flipped — less than half of what’s needed to tell. The rollout completes, every instance is on the new version, and the model never crosses the line that would have called it bad. This isn’t a bug in the detection formula; it’s the formula being honest that some regressions are simply too subtle for any single rollout’s traffic volume to catch, no matter how carefully it’s staged.

06

Every strategy is buying something specific, not being safer in general

Rolling buys low capacity cost at the price of a growing, cumulative exposure. Nothing extra runs — the fleet replaces itself in place — but every batch that completes adds to the population already served by the new version, and (as the barely-worse-bug case shows) a subtle enough regression can survive the entire rollout undetected. Shrinking the batch size doesn’t fix that; it only changes how many steps the same total exposure is spread across.

Blue-green buys instant, symmetric rollback at the price of running two full fleets and exposing 100% of traffic from the first instant. It is the fastest strategy to reach a confident verdict in wall-clock time, precisely because it isn’t holding anything back — which is also exactly why, if the version is bad, every single user during that window gets it.

Canary buys a capped blast radius at the price of detection latency, and that latency has a floor set by traffic volume, not by policy. A tighter canary percentage doesn’t just take longer; per-stage sample resets mean it can let a bug ride past an entire stage’s worth of exposure completely unflagged before a larger stage’s traffic finally accumulates enough evidence. The observation window has to be sized against this lesson’s own detection-threshold arithmetic, or a canary is providing the appearance of a safety gate without its substance.

Shadow traffic buys a genuine 0% blast radius, but only for what it can observe from a discarded response. Nothing here is free: shadowing 100% of traffic costs the same duplicate compute as blue-green’s second fleet, and — this model can’t put a number on it, so it stays in prose — a shadowed request that writes to a database, charges a card, or sends an email either has to be sandboxed away from those real side effects, or it produces them for real regardless of whether anyone ever looks at the response. Shadow traffic validates computation; it does not by itself validate a version that has any side effect a diff can’t see.

Where this model is honest about its own limits: it assumes traffic is spread evenly across instances (no sticky sessions skewing which users hit new-version instances first), it models one bug of one fixed severity rather than several failure modes at once, and its canary treats each stage’s observation window as a hard, independent reset rather than a real-world rolling analysis window that might carry some signal forward. None of that changes the central result — that detecting a fixed bug needs a fixed number of real exposures — but it does mean the exact seconds this model prints are a clean illustration of the shape, not a number to paste into a real runbook’s timeout.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • All four strategies are schedules for one variable, fractionOnNew(t) — the share of the fleet, and (under even load balancing) the share of REAL traffic, running the new version at time t. Rolling ramps it in cumulative batches, blue-green jumps it from 0 to 1 in one switch, canary ramps it in stages with an observation window per stage, and shadow holds it at 0 forever while still running the new code against duplicated traffic.
  • The number of real requests a bad version must serve before its elevated error rate is statistically distinguishable from ordinary background noise — a one-sample proportion test, n = z²·p0(1-p0)/(p1-p0)² — depends only on the bug's severity and the confidence bar, never on which rollout strategy is running. Strategy only changes how fast wall-clock time accumulates that many exposures, and what share of the live fleet eats them meanwhile.
  • Rolling's blast radius is cumulative, not just the size of one batch: by the k-th batch, k·batchSize/N of the fleet has already been touched — a share that keeps climbing with every batch even though "the batch fraction" sounds like a small, fixed, instantaneous number.
  • Canary resets its sample count at every stage promotion. A bug too subtle to clear the detection bar within one stage's observation window is judged clean and promoted to the next, larger stage — so a smaller canary percentage doesn't just detect more slowly, it can let a bug ride past an entire stage of exposure completely undetected before a bigger stage's higher traffic share finally catches it.
  • Once a bug is caught, rollback for rolling, blue-green, and canary all use the identical mechanism — repoint the load balancer / redeploy the known-good version in parallel — so recovery time converges to roughly the same small constant regardless of which strategy exposed the bug. The strategies differ in how much exposure accumulates BEFORE detection, never in how fast they recover after it.
  • Blue-green and shadow traffic both pay a real, quantifiable capacity cost — running two full fleets, or a full fleet plus a shadow-sized slice of a second one, for as long as the coexistence window lasts — in exchange for two different things: blue-green buys an instant, all-or-nothing rollback; shadow buys a permanent 0% blast radius, at the cost of validating nothing about the new version's side effects, because its responses are discarded rather than compared.

  1. Cued recall

    A team ships a new version behind a 1% canary against 200 requests/second of total traffic. The new version's error rate is actually 1% against a 0.5% baseline — subtle, but real. After a 10-minute (600-second) observation window shows nothing unusual, the canary is promoted to 25% of traffic. Two minutes later, an alert fires. What happened, and did anything malfunction?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Nothing malfunctioned. Detecting this bug needs 1,791 real exposures to the bad version before its 1% error rate clears the baseline's own sampling noise (z=3). At 1% of 200 rps, the canary only accumulates 200 × 0.01 × 600 = 1,200 exposures in the full 10-minute window — short of the 1,791 needed — so the stage genuinely looks clean and gets promoted exactly as designed. Once promoted to 25%, the canary rate jumps to 50 rps, and the remaining exposures needed accumulate in under 36 seconds — landing the alert about 636 seconds (10.6 minutes) after the rollout began. The canary didn't fail; a 1% sample was simply too thin a slice of noise to separate this particular bug from it in time.

  2. Cued recall

    A team believes blue-green is strictly safer than rolling because its rollback is instant. Given the identical bug and the identical total traffic as above (1% vs. 0.5% baseline, 200 rps), is blue-green's total damage by the time it's caught actually smaller than rolling's?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — and the reason is subtle. Both strategies need the exact same 1,791 exposed requests to cross the detection threshold, because that number depends only on the bug and the confidence bar, not the strategy. But blue-green reaches that count fastest in wall-clock time (about 39 seconds) precisely because it is already sending 100% of live traffic to the bad version — meaning every single user hitting the service during that 39 seconds gets it. Rolling reaches the identical 1,791-request threshold more slowly (about 75 seconds) but with only 20% of the fleet touched at the moment of detection, so roughly 80% of the fleet's requests during that same window never reach the bad version at all. Same detection cost, in requests; a very different population pays it.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `health-checks.mdx` already established that a shallow health check (does the process respond to /health) can pass while the application underneath is broken, because "answers" and "answers correctly" are different properties. A team adds this lesson's statistical error-rate gate on top of their existing shallow per-batch health check before each rolling batch proceeds. Does that combination close the shallow-check gap for good?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Only if the batch is actually held open long enough to matter. The statistical gate can catch what a shallow check can't — a process that's up but wrong — but only by accumulating real exposures against this lesson's own arithmetic (n = z²p0(1-p0)/(p1-p0)²). If `batchDurationSeconds` is sized only long enough for the shallow health check to return 200 (a few seconds), the statistical gate has nowhere near enough traffic to evaluate before the next batch starts, and the rollout proceeds exactly as blind as if the gate were never added. The fix isn't "add a check" — health-checks.mdx already made that point about depth; this lesson makes the same point about TIME. A detection gate is only as good as the exposure count behind it, whatever mechanism is doing the detecting.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a decision-maker choosing to expose a larger share of a population to a change gets trustworthy evidence about that change sooner, in calendar time — but only because a larger share of the population has already been exposed to whatever the change turns out to be by the time that evidence arrives. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Phased clinical drug trials. A Phase I trial gives a new drug to a small cohort (dozens of people) specifically because a rare or subtle adverse effect can't yet be told apart from ordinary background health variation in so small a sample — exactly this lesson's noise floor. Phase II and Phase III progressively widen the cohort, the same way a canary ramps its traffic share, precisely because a bigger population accumulates enough adverse- event exposures to make a rare side effect statistically visible. Skipping straight to approving the drug for the entire population at once — this lesson's blue-green — would surface a real problem in less calendar time (more patients means faster accumulation of evidence), but only because the entire population was already exposed by the time anyone could tell. Post-market surveillance (Phase IV) is the pharmaceutical industry's canary that never fully turns off: a permanent, low-rate, real-population sample watching for exactly the failure mode too rare for even a large trial to have caught.
    Did you have that?
