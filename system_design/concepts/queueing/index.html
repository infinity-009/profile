Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Queueing & utilisation start here explained well nowhere else

Why a 20% traffic increase can make you 7× slower

Nothing broke, no bug shipped, the CPU is not at 100% — and your service is five times slower than it was yesterday. This is the first thing to understand about systems at scale, and it happens on a single machine.

01

One server, one campaign, and a number that makes no sense

You run a single API server. It handles 100 requests per second comfortably. Each request takes about 40 milliseconds. Nobody complains.

Marketing runs a campaign. Traffic rises 20%, to 120 requests per second.

Before reading on, guess what happens to response time.

The intuitive answer is a bit slower — maybe 45 or 50ms. Traffic went up by a fifth, so latency goes up by a fifth. That feels like how machines ought to work.

Here is what actually happens. If that server’s absolute ceiling is 140 requests per second, response time goes to roughly 280 milliseconds. Seven times worse, from a 20% increase.

Then it gets worse on its own. Clients that were configured to time out at 200ms start timing out. Timed-out clients retry. Retries are new requests, so the arrival rate goes up, so response times go up further, so more clients time out. The system can stay stuck in that state even after the campaign traffic stops.

Nothing broke. No bad deploy. No server crashed. Disk and network are fine. And the CPU is not even at 100%.

This is the most important idea in system design, and you do not need a single distributed component to meet it. One server, one queue, and arithmetic.

02

The mental model almost everyone starts with, and why it is wrong

Ask someone why the server is fine and you will get some version of the bucket model:

The server can do 140 requests per second. I am sending 120. 120 is less than 140, so I have spare capacity. Therefore nothing is waiting.

That sentence sounds like arithmetic, which is what makes it so convincing. It is wrong, and the quickest way to see why is to drive a server yourself rather than read about one.

Predict Set the load to 85%. The worker will be visibly idle in the gaps. How long does a typical request wait, as a multiple of the time it takes to serve one?
ρ/(1−ρ) = 0.85/0.15 = 5.67. Both facts are true simultaneously: the worker sits idle roughly 15% of the time and the average request waits almost six service times. The bucket model says that is impossible, which is exactly why it has to go.
Show it without answering

Put it at 85% and read the two panels together, because the contradiction is the point:

  • The worker is idle a noticeable fraction of the time. You can see the pale gaps in its timeline. It genuinely has nothing to do in those moments.
  • The queue is deep, and a typical request waits several times longer than it takes to actually serve it.

Both. Simultaneously. The bucket model says this is impossible: if the worker is ever idle, there is spare capacity, so nothing should be waiting for it.

Now push to 95%. Watch the queue-depth trace. It still comes back down sometimes — but the excursions get taller and last longer, and the gaps that used to drain them are gone.

03

The intuition: idle time is a resource

Forget servers. One cashier, one queue, 30 seconds per customer. That is a capacity of 120 customers per hour.

Now send exactly 120 customers per hour. Is the queue empty?

Only if they arrive exactly 30 seconds apart, forever. Real customers do not do that. Some minutes three walk in together; some minutes nobody comes.

When three arrive at once, two of them wait. The cashier can only work that backlog off during a later gap — a stretch when fewer than expected turn up. The queue drains using idle time.

Idle time is what a system uses to absorb unevenness. Run at 100% utilisation and there is no idle time left, so there is nothing left to absorb bursts with — and a queue that forms has no way to get back down.

That is the whole lesson. Everything below puts numbers on it.

And notice what it implies, because this is the part that overturns the bucket model: the queue is not caused by too much work. The average amount of work is comfortably within capacity. The queue is caused by work arriving unevenly. Averages hide arrival patterns, and the arrival pattern is what actually hurts you.

04

The mechanism, and the one formula worth memorising

Utilisation is the fraction of time your workers are busy. Three quantities set it:

ρ = λ / (c × μ)

λ (lambda) — arrivals per second. μ (mu) — how many requests one worker can finish per second. c — number of workers. ρ (rho) — utilisation, from 0 to 1.

For the simplest realistic case — random arrivals, random service times, one worker, which queueing theory calls M/M/1 — the mean time a request spends in the system is:

response time = service time ÷ (1 − ρ)

So response time as a multiple of service time is just 1/(1−ρ). As ρ approaches 1, the denominator approaches zero, and response time grows without bound.

That single fraction is the shape of nearly every capacity problem you will ever debug.

The shape matters more than any single value: nearly flat, then a wall. The curve gives you no warning. By the time latency is bad enough that somebody files a ticket, you are already on the vertical part, where a small traffic increase has an enormous effect. That asymmetry is why capacity planning cannot be done by extrapolating from a graph of last month’s latency.

Two further things this plot is telling you.

More workers let you run hotter

One worker at 90% is a 10× penalty. Sixteen workers at the same 90% utilisation is under 2×. Identical utilisation, radically different experience.

The reason is that a burst arriving at a pool of 16 can spread across whichever workers happen to be free, while a burst arriving at 1 worker can only queue. This is Erlang C, and it is the real argument for one shared pool over several small dedicated ones — and the reason per-tenant or per-endpoint worker partitioning quietly costs you latency.

Little’s Law tells you how much is in flight

L = λ × W

Requests in the system = arrival rate × time each one spends there.

If you serve 500 requests per second and each spends 200ms in the system, then on average 100 requests are in flight at once. That is your required connection count, thread count, and memory footprint — derived, not guessed. It is also the fastest way to check whether a capacity claim someone just made you is arithmetically possible.

Read the last column, not the second. The absolute latency at a given utilisation is interesting; the marginal cost of a little more traffic is what determines whether you get paged. At 50% load, another 5% of traffic is free. At 90%, another 5% doubles your latency.

Practical targets, which now follow rather than being arbitrary folklore:

  • Interactive services: 50–70%. You are buying idle time to absorb bursts with.
  • Batch and async work: 90%+ is fine. Nobody is waiting on a response, so queueing delay is not felt. This is why the same utilisation number is excellent for a video-encoding fleet and alarming for a checkout API.
  • Autoscalers target 60–70% CPU, not 90%, for exactly this reason — plus the scaling itself takes minutes, and the curve does not wait for you.

And the part nobody likes: running at 50% means paying for roughly twice the hardware you strictly need. That is not waste. That is the price of predictable latency, and it should appear in a design discussion as a deliberate purchase rather than an oversight.

06

The trade-off: variance is the variable you were not watching

Everything so far treated utilisation as the thing that matters. It is not the only thing. Two systems at identical utilisation with identical average service times can have completely different latency.

The top lane never queues at all. Perfectly spaced arrivals and perfectly constant service times mean each request finishes before the next arrives — at any utilisation below 100%. It is also completely unattainable, which is exactly why it is worth seeing: it shows that the queue is a product of unevenness, not of load.

The bottom lane is what real traffic looks like, and it is catastrophically worse at the same load.

Kingman’s formula names the missing term:

mean wait ÷ service time ≈ ρ/(1−ρ) × (ca² + cs²) / 2

ca² — how variable arrivals are. cs² — how variable service times are. Both are 1 for the “random” case, 0 for perfectly regular, and larger than 1 for bursty.

Variability is a multiplier on the wait you already had. Halve the variability and you halve the queueing delay at exactly the same utilisation — without buying a single server.

So the real trade-off is three-way, and you may pick two:

You wantYou pay withBecause
High utilisation and low latencyPerfectly smooth trafficOnly achievable if you control the arrival pattern, which you usually do not
Low latency and real bursty trafficIdle capacity — moneyIdle time is the buffer that absorbs bursts
High utilisation and bursty trafficQueueing delayAnd then bounded queues plus load shedding, or the delay becomes an outage

Martin Kleppmann puts the same trade-off plainly, in the context of networks rather than servers — the mathematics does not care which:

Latency guarantees are achievable in certain environments, if resources are statically partitioned… However, these guarantees come at the cost of reduced utilization — in other words, it is more expensive. Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., p. 357

What actually buys you headroom

Because variability is a multiplier, attacking it is often cheaper than buying servers:

  • Smooth the arrivals. Batching, request coalescing, jittering scheduled jobs so ten thousand cron tasks do not fire on the same second, admission control at the edge.
  • Cap the variance in service time. Paginate unbounded queries, put a ceiling on work per request, and move genuinely slow operations into a separate pool so they cannot occupy the workers that fast requests need. That last one is a bulkhead.
  • Grow the pool, not the number of pools. Erlang C rewards sharing.
  • Bound the queue and shed load. An unbounded queue does not prevent failure, it converts a latency problem into an outage — because requests keep being accepted long after anyone is still waiting for the answer. Try the first rig at 95% and watch how long the oldest queued request has been waiting.
07

In the wild

Retry storms and systems that will not recover. The failure at the top of this page — where timeouts cause retries which cause more timeouts — has a name. DDIA calls it a metastable failure: the system stays broken after the original trigger is gone, and needs a restart rather than patience. The recommended defences are all consequences of this lesson: randomised exponential backoff, circuit breakers, token buckets, and proactive load shedding. (DDIA 2nd ed., p. 38.)

Why your p99 is worse than you think, and gets worse as you grow. Dean and Barroso’s The Tail at Scale is the canonical treatment. The key result: if one user request fans out to 100 servers and each has a 1% chance of being slow, then roughly 63% of user requests hit at least one slow server. Your rare tail case becomes the common case purely from fan-out — so tail latency has to be engineered, not monitored. DDIA makes the same point at p. 42, Figure 2-6.

Which percentile to actually chase. DDIA notes that Amazon specifies internal response times at the 99.9th percentile, because the slowest requests often belong to the customers with the most data — the most valuable ones. It also records that Amazon judged the 99.99th percentile too expensive to be worth optimising. Both halves matter: high percentiles are where the users are, and there is a point past which chasing them stops paying. (DDIA 2nd ed., p. 41.)

Where response time actually goes. DDIA’s Figure 2-4 decomposes it into service time, queueing delay, and network latency — and observes that queueing delay often accounts for a large part of the variability, because a busy server can only handle so much in parallel. Head-of-line blocking is the same phenomenon one level down. (DDIA 2nd ed., pp. 38–39.)

Where this lesson came from. The interactive treatment of queueing is not original to this repo — Sam Rose’s visual guide to queueing got there first and remains the best single piece of writing on it. What is different here is that this is lesson one of a sequence rather than a standalone essay, so backpressure, load shedding, rate limiting and cascading failure can be built directly on top of it instead of re-deriving it.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Utilisation is arrivals divided by capacity, ρ = λ/(cμ)
  • Response time as a multiple of service time is 1/(1−ρ), so it grows without bound as ρ approaches 1
  • Idle time is the resource a system uses to absorb unevenness
  • Little's Law, L = λW, is distribution-free and works as a universal sanity check
  • Kingman — variability multiplies the wait by (ca²+cs²)/2, so burstiness costs as much as load
  • A pool of c servers beats c separate queues at the same utilisation

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    The worker is idle 15% of the time and the queue is deep. Explain how both can be true at once.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because arrivals are uneven. Idle time and queued work happen at different moments, not the same moment. The idle periods are when nothing has arrived; the queue forms during clusters of arrivals. Averaged over an hour there is spare capacity; at the instant a burst lands there is none, and the burst's queue has to be drained by the next idle gap. Utilisation is an average and tells you nothing about the moment.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Two services run at 85% utilisation with the same mean service time. One measures a 5.6× wait, the other 21×. What differs, and by roughly how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Variability. Kingman: wait ÷ service time ≈ ρ/(1−ρ) × (ca²+cs²)/2. At ρ = 0.85 the first factor is 5.67 for both. The second factor is 1 for Poisson arrivals and exponential service, and about 3.7 for the bursty one — so burstiness alone accounts for a ~4× difference in wait at identical load. This is measured in the variance rig, and Kingman's prediction is drawn next to it.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why does one pool of 8 workers beat 8 workers with their own queues, at the same total utilisation?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a request in the shared pool waits only if every worker is busy, while a request in its own queue waits if its worker is busy — even with seven others idle. That is Erlang C: the probability all c servers are busy falls off fast in c. Partitioning throws away exactly the statistical multiplexing that idle time provides.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    What does the marginal cost of the next 5% of traffic look like across the utilisation range?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Nearly free at low load and catastrophic at high load, because 1/(1−ρ) is convex. Going 50% → 55% moves the multiplier from 2.0 to 2.2. Going 90% → 95% moves it from 10 to 20. The same 5% of traffic costs ten times as much latency at the top of the range, which is why capacity plans denominated in "percent utilisation" mislead.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A queue-based worker pool runs at 70% and the team wants to save money by cutting the fleet 20%. What happens to latency, and what is the number you would bring to that meeting?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Cutting the fleet 20% raises ρ from 0.70 to 0.875. The multiplier goes from 1/(0.30) = 3.3 to 1/(0.125) = 8.0 — latency roughly 2.4× worse, for a 20% saving. Bring that ratio, not the utilisation figures: "20% cheaper, 2.4× slower" is a decision, "70% → 87.5%" sounds like headroom.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system with no slack cannot absorb variance, and the cost of removing the last slack is unbounded rather than proportional. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Hospital beds — occupancy above ~85% is associated with sharp rises in wait times and diversions, which is the same 1/(1−ρ) curve. Motorway traffic — flow collapses near capacity, and the jam propagates backwards because there is no gap to absorb a braking event. Airport runway scheduling and just-in-time supply chains behave identically. The signature is always the same: the metric looks like it has headroom right up to the point where the queue stops draining.
    Did you have that?
