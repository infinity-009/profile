Tier 4 · Distributed systems mechanics Service discovery

A registry that checks every 30 seconds routes one request in eleven to an instance that's already dead

Service A needs to call service B, and B runs as multiple, constantly-changing instances — scaling up, scaling down, crashing and getting replaced, each on an IP address that didn't exist an hour ago. A needs a CURRENT, WORKING instance of B, and there are exactly two places that "current" can be decided: in the calling client, or in a component the client always talks to instead. This lesson prices both, prices the registry both of them depend on, and computes the one number that makes "current" a lie: how often a client gets routed to an instance that has already crashed but the registry hasn't heard about it yet.

01

Ten instances of the payments service exist. Which one does 'call payments' actually mean?

Service A needs to call service B. Nothing unusual about that — except B is not one thing. B runs as ten instances this minute, eight last minute (two crashed), and it will run as fourteen an hour from now because an autoscaler just kicked in. Every one of those instances sits behind its own IP address, and none of those addresses is fixed: a crashed instance’s address is simply gone, and a newly scheduled one’s address didn’t exist a second ago.

So “call the payments service” cannot mean “call this address” — there is no single address that stays right for more than a few minutes. It has to mean something more like “call a current, working instance of the payments service, whichever one that happens to be right now.” Answering that question, cheaply and correctly, on every single request, is the entire subject of this lesson.

02

Assume 'ask something that tracks the instances' is basically one problem

The instinct: somewhere, something must know which instances are alive — call it a registry, ask it, done. That’s true as far as it goes, but it hides two separate design decisions (who does the asking, and how fresh is the answer) inside one word. Commit to a number on the freshness question before the rig shows it.

Predict A registry checks each instance's health every 30 seconds and requires 2 consecutive failed checks before removing it from rotation — a 60-second detection lag. Instances in this fleet crash on average every 10 minutes (600 seconds). Roughly what fraction of client requests land on an instance that has already crashed but the registry hasn't removed yet?
deadInstanceRoutingProbability treats this as a renewal-reward fraction: an instance alternates between an ALIVE period (mean 600s) and a DEAD-BUT-LISTED period (the fixed 60s detection lag), and the long-run fraction of time in the dead-but-listed state is that duration divided by the FULL cycle length — 60 / (600 + 60) ≈ 9.09%, not 60/600. The naive 10% guess divides by the alive time alone, which is close here but is the wrong denominator in general; at a short enough instance lifetime relative to the detection lag, the gap between the two would actually matter.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s defaults — a 5-second health check, 3 consecutive failures (a 15-second detection lag), and a 60-minute instance lifetime — about 0.41% of requests land on an already-dead instance. Slow the check to 30 seconds with a 2-failure threshold against a 10-minute instance lifetime (a fleet of short-lived, aggressively-autoscaled instances) and that climbs to 9.09% — about 1 request in 11. On the path side, at a 5ms hop and a 90% registry-cache hit rate, client-side discovery costs 5.85ms against server-side’s fixed 10ms — but drop the cache hit rate below about 37.7% and client-side becomes the more expensive option, because its lookup cost stops being cheap enough to beat one extra hop.

03

The switchboard and the direct line

A large company’s general phone line is server-side discovery made physical. You always dial the same number; an operator (or an auto-attendant) looks up who currently holds the role you need and transfers you. Every call pays the transfer step — a fixed cost, the same for everyone — and if the switchboard itself goes down, nobody in the entire company is reachable, no matter how many employees are sitting at their desks working normally. The switchboard’s own uptime is the whole company’s uptime, from a caller’s point of view.

Looking someone up in an internal directory and dialing their extension yourself is client-side discovery. It’s faster once you have the number — no transfer step — but the number in your hand is only as good as the last time you checked it. If that person left the company last month and the directory hasn’t caught up, you dial straight into a disconnected line, with nothing on the other end to notice the mistake and redirect you. The switchboard’s failure mode is “everyone is unreachable.” The direct-line failure mode is “you, personally, dialed a dead extension and don’t find out until you try.” Both failure modes exist because both approaches depend on the same underlying directory being both reachable and current — they just fail differently when it isn’t.

04

Two request shapes, one shared dependency

CLIENT-SIDE    The client queries the registry directly, gets back a list of healthy instances,
DISCOVERY      and picks one itself (often with a load-balancing policy — load-balancing.mdx
               already covers picking among known-healthy backends; not re-derived here). Puts
               more logic in every client. The registry lookup CAN be cached, so its cost is
               variable and shrinks with a warm cache.

SERVER-SIDE    The client always sends its request to one well-known, stable endpoint — a load
DISCOVERY      balancer or API gateway — which queries the registry itself and forwards. Client
               stays simple. Costs a fixed extra hop, always, with no caching lever to shrink it,
               and now the router's own availability matters as much as the registry's.

THE REGISTRY   Whichever pattern is chosen, both of them depend on the same thing: a service that
ITSELF         tracks which instances are currently alive. Instances register on startup and are
               supposed to deregister on shutdown — but the realistic failure is a crash with no
               graceful deregistration, which is exactly why the registry runs its own health
               checks and why THEIR lag is this lesson's second surface.

The first two rows are a genuine trade-off, priced in Slot 5. The third row is not optional for either of them — it’s the dependency both patterns share, which is why it gets its own number rather than a footnote.

Gossip solves an adjacent but different problem: gossip-protocols-and-membership is how nodes within one cluster learn about each other’s membership, peer to peer, with no registry at all. Service discovery is how a client of a service — often outside that cluster entirely — finds a current instance of it. The two can even compose (a cluster could use gossip internally while exposing a registry externally), but neither one substitutes for the other, and this lesson’s registry-availability numbers below have nothing to do with gossip’s convergence-round numbers.

06

The trade-off — and neither pattern escapes the registry

Client-side buys lower latency once the cache is warm, and sells complexity into every client. Every service that calls B now needs registry-query logic and a load-balancing policy baked in — load-balancing.mdx’s five policies are exactly the menu here — which means every client is a place that logic can be implemented inconsistently, or forgotten. Server-side buys uniform, centralized logic (one router, one place to fix a bug or add a policy) and sells a hop that no amount of client-side cleverness removes, because the hop lives in the network path, not in anyone’s cache.

Neither pattern makes the registry’s own reliability optional. A team that picks server-side discovery specifically to keep clients simple can end up trusting the router’s registry watch completely, with no fallback if the registry glitches; a team that picks client-side specifically to avoid a “single router as a bottleneck” can still route every client into the exact same single-point-of-failure the moment the registry itself is unavailable, because a client with no cached answer and no reachable registry has nothing to fall back to either. The honest fix in both cases is the same one directory-based-sharding.mdx already named: replicate the registry, put it behind consensus, and make its own outage degrade lookups (serve a stale-but-valid cached list) rather than fail them outright.

Faster health checks buy a lower dead-instance-routing probability and sell registry load and, past a point, false suspicions of instances that are only briefly slow — the exact accuracy/speed trade failure-detection.mdx already quantified for a single detector. This lesson’s detection lag is that same trade-off’s output, fed into a different question (how often does staleness actually get routed to) rather than a new one.

07

In the wild

DDIA’s own “Request Routing” section names all three of this lesson’s patterns explicitly, in the same figure. Kleppmann and Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., describe “three ways of routing a request to the right node” (printed p.266): letting clients hit any node and having that node forward internally, sending every request through “a routing tier first, which determines the node that should handle each request” (server-side discovery, in this lesson’s terms), or making “clients be aware of the sharding and the assignment… a client can connect directly to the appropriate node” (client-side discovery). The book then names the registry this lesson prices directly: “Many distributed data systems rely on a separate coordination service such as ZooKeeper or etcd to keep track of shard assignments… Each node registers itself… Kubernetes uses etcd to keep track of which service instance is running where” (printed pp.266–267) — the exact registration/health-check/lookup mechanism this lesson’s model computes, named for a sharding context but structurally identical to a service registry.

Consul, etcd, and ZooKeeper are the three registries this lesson is written to generalize over. Each supports the registration-plus-health-check pattern this lesson models — HashiCorp’s Consul ships client-side-oriented DNS/HTTP lookups alongside a server-side-oriented built-in proxy; Kubernetes uses etcd as its registry and its Service/kube-proxy machinery as a server-side discovery layer in front of it; Apache ZooKeeper is the coordination service DDIA cites directly for exactly this registration/lookup role.

Netflix’s Eureka is the client-side pattern’s best-known production instance, built explicitly so that clients keep a local, periodically-refreshed cache of the registry’s contents and keep routing to their last-known-good list if the registry itself becomes briefly unreachable — the same “degrade the lookup instead of failing it” defense this lesson’s trade-off section names as the honest fix for the registry-as-SPOF risk.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Service discovery answers a different question than gossip does: gossip is how nodes WITHIN one cluster learn about each other's membership; service discovery is how a CLIENT of a service — a caller, possibly outside that cluster entirely — finds A current, working instance of it. Same underlying problem shape (who's alive right now), different relationship (peer-to-peer inside a cluster vs. caller-to-callee across a service boundary)
  • Client-side discovery has the calling client query the registry directly and pick an instance itself (often load-balancing between the healthy ones — cross-reference `load-balancing`, not re-derived here) — that registry lookup CAN be cached, the same cache-hit-rate blend `directory-based-sharding`'s `directoryLookupCost` already prices, reused here unchanged as `registryLookupCost`
  • Server-side discovery has the client send every request to one well-known, stable endpoint (a load balancer or API gateway) that queries the registry itself and forwards — simpler clients, but a fixed extra network hop with no caching lever to shrink it, and that routing component's own availability now matters as much as the registry's does
  • A registry (Consul, etcd, ZooKeeper, a cloud provider's built-in service registry) is structurally the exact same thing as `directory-based-sharding`'s directory: a lookup table everything depends on to find anything, so its own availability puts the same hard ceiling on the whole system — `registryAvailabilityCeiling`/`systemAvailability` are that file's functions, reused unchanged
  • Instances register on startup and are supposed to deregister on shutdown, but the realistic case is a crash with no graceful deregistration — which is exactly `health-checks`' problem, reused here: the registry's own poll-based detection lag (`pollLagMs`, interval × consecutive-failure threshold) is the window during which a client can still be routed to an instance that's already gone
  • That lag turns into a genuine probability, not just a delay: at a 30-second check needing 2 consecutive failures (a 60-second detection lag) against an instance that lives a mean 10 minutes, about 1 request in 11 lands on an instance that's already dead — the number is `deadInstanceRoutingProbability`, a renewal-reward fraction (dead-but-listed time divided by total cycle time), not a guess

  1. Cued recall

    A team says "we use client-side discovery, so our clients need to be smart, but at least we save a hop." A second team says "we use server-side discovery, so our clients stay dumb, but we pay an extra hop on every request." Are both descriptions accurate, and is the "extra hop" always the more expensive option?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Both descriptions of the MECHANISM are accurate — client-side really does put more logic in every client (querying the registry, applying a load-balancing policy) in exchange for going straight to the instance, and server-side really does add a fixed second hop (client → router → instance) in exchange for a client that only ever needs to know one address. But "extra hop is always worse" is not automatically true: client-side's registry lookup is only cheap if it's well-cached — at a low cache hit rate, the lookup cost can exceed what the second hop would have cost. `clientSideVsServerSideHopCost` computes both totals from the same inputs precisely because which one wins depends on the cache hit rate and the hop latency, not on which pattern sounds simpler.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A registry checks instance health every 30 seconds and requires 2 consecutive failures before removing an instance from rotation — a 60-second detection lag. An instance in this fleet crashes on average every 10 minutes. Roughly what fraction of requests land on an instance that has already crashed but the registry hasn't removed yet, and why isn't the answer just "60 seconds out of every 10 minutes, so 10%"?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About 9.1% (`deadInstanceRoutingProbability` returns 0.0909 for these inputs) — very close to the naive "60s / 600s" guess, but not because that guess is the right method. The correct fraction is the dead-but-listed duration divided by the FULL cycle length (alive time plus dead-but-listed time): 60 / (600 + 60), not 60 / 600. At a much longer instance lifetime the two numbers converge, because the dead-but-listed window becomes a vanishingly small share of the denominator either way — but at short lifetimes (spot instances, aggressive autoscaling) the difference between dividing by the alive time and dividing by the full cycle actually matters.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `directory-based-sharding.mdx` computes `directoryAvailabilityCeiling` for a directory that maps KEYS to shards. This lesson reuses the identical function for a registry that maps SERVICE NAMES to instances. Is that the same risk, or does the different kind of thing being looked up change the analysis?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It's the same risk, because the risk was never about what's being looked up — it's about the fact that something everyone depends on has its own availability, and a request needs BOTH that lookup and the backend to be up. Whether the table maps keys to shards or service names to instances, the moment anything has to consult it before it can act, that table's own uptime becomes a hard ceiling on the whole system's, computed by the exact same `min(directory, backend)` argument either way. The specific numbers can differ (a shard directory might be consulted once per connection and cached for its lifetime; a service registry might be consulted per-request or watched continuously), but the STRUCTURE — one more thing in the critical path, therefore one more thing that must stay up — transfers unchanged, which is exactly why this lesson imports the function instead of writing a new one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a caller that needs to reach one of several interchangeable, churning providers of a service either asks a trusted directory itself and goes straight to the provider, or always goes through a fixed intermediary that asks the directory on its behalf — and either way, the directory's own reliability and the staleness of what it reports both become the caller's problem, invisibly. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Calling a large company's general phone line versus looking up an employee's direct extension in an internal directory. Dialing the switchboard (server-side): you always call the same number, an operator or auto-attendant looks up who's currently in that role and transfers you — simple for you, but every call pays the transfer step, and if the switchboard itself is down, nobody in the company is reachable no matter how many employees are actually at their desks. Looking up someone's direct line yourself (client-side): faster once you have the number, but the number you have might be for someone who left the company last month (the directory's own update lag), and if your copy of the directory is stale you dial straight into a disconnected line with nothing to catch the mistake and redirect you — exactly the "dead instance" this lesson quantifies, just with a person instead of a server.
    Did you have that?
