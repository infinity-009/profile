Tier 1 · The network TLS on the wire explained well nowhere else

The certificate is 88% of the handshake, and choosing RSA cost a round trip

The previous lesson counted the round trips. This one opens them. A server's first TLS flight is overwhelmingly one message — the certificate chain — and its size is set by your certificate authority's hierarchy and your key algorithm, not by your protocol version. On QUIC that size decides whether a cold handshake takes one round trip or two.

Read first — connection-setup-costbandwidth-delay-product

01

They removed a round trip on paper and got it back on the wire

A team runs a public API that mobile clients hit on cold connections all day — an app that wakes, asks one question and goes back to sleep, so connection reuse never gets a chance. They read the previous lesson’s ladder, saw that HTTP/3 over QUIC does a first-ever connection in one round trip against TCP-plus-TLS-1.3’s two, and shipped HTTP/3.

Round trip on their path is 40 ms. The predicted saving on a cold request was 40 ms out of 120.

Measured saving: zero. Cold HTTP/3 requests showed two round trips of setup, not one, on every client and in every region.

Everything they could think to check was clean. The handshake succeeded — no retries, no fallbacks, no alerts. There was no Retry packet, so it was not the documented address-validation exception from the last lesson. CPU on the terminators was flat. The cipher was AES-128-GCM with hardware acceleration. TLS version 1.3, group X25519, no HelloRetryRequest. Every TLS metric they had said the handshake was healthy, and it was: nothing failed.

Here is what was actually happening, and none of it is in a TLS dashboard.

Their server’s first flight — everything it says before the client is allowed to speak again — was 4,001 bytes. QUIC does not let a server send 4,001 bytes at that moment. RFC 9000 §8.1 caps a server at three times the bytes it has received from an address it has not yet validated, and a client’s first Initial datagram is at least 1,200 bytes, so the budget was 3,600 bytes. The server sent 3,600, stopped 401 bytes short of its own Finished message, and waited for any packet from the client to lift the limit.

That wait is the second round trip. And of the 4,001 bytes, 3,352 were one message: the certificate chain.

Their chain was RSA-2048: a leaf and two intermediates, 3,329 bytes of certificate. The same chain issued with ECDSA P-256 keys is 2,141 bytes, which makes the flight 2,575 — inside the 3,600-byte budget with 1,025 bytes to spare. Changing the certificate, and nothing else, removed the round trip. Certificate choice is a latency decision, and it is made by whoever runs your certificate pipeline, usually on grounds that have nothing to do with latency.

02

The obvious fix is a cipher, and the cipher is 0.1 nanoseconds

The mental model almost everyone starts with — the one this lesson has to break — is that TLS is one cost, and that cost is CPU. “Encryption is expensive” is the shape of it, and it makes three fixes look obvious. All three were tried here and all three did nothing:

  • Switch to ChaCha20-Poly1305. A different bulk cipher changes the per-byte rate from 0.106 to 0.440 nanoseconds per byte. The problem was 401 bytes of certificate, on a path where the flight was stalled by a permission to send, not by a rate.
  • Add terminator capacity. The flight is not CPU-bound. It is 4,001 bytes against a limit.
  • Turn on session resumption. Correct, and irrelevant to the measurement: resumption removes the certificate from subsequent handshakes, and every handshake in this workload is a first one.

The reason the model fails is that TLS is not one cost. It is at least four, with four different units, and only one of them is charged per byte of your response:

Before you touch the rig, commit to a number.

Predict A QUIC client's first Initial datagram is 1,200 bytes. The server's first flight — ServerHello, certificate chain, signature, Finished — is 4,001 bytes. How many of those bytes may the server send before the client's address is validated?
RFC 9000 §8.1 lets a server send at most three times what it has received from an unvalidated address: 3 × 1,200 = 3,600 bytes. The server transmits 3,600, stops 401 bytes short, and waits for any packet from the client — one extra round trip, with no error and no retry. Option 2 is the belief this rig breaks, and it is the belief of anyone who has read about certificate chains overflowing the initial congestion window: TCP's window is 14,600 bytes (RFC 6928), which is nearly four times larger, and on TCP this same flight fits comfortably in one round trip with 10,778 bytes spare. Show it without answering

Four things to do with it, in order:

  1. Drag intermediates across its range on RSA-2048. At 0 the flight is 1,764 bytes and the certificate is 73% of it. At 2 — an ordinary public chain — the flight is 3,822 bytes over TCP, 4,001 over QUIC, the certificate is 88% of it, and the QUIC row is over the wall. You did not choose that slider’s value. Your certificate authority’s hierarchy did.
  2. Click ECDSA P-256 and drag it again. Every position fits. Same protocol, same cipher, same number of certificates, same everything a protocol diagram shows.
  3. Read the note under the axis. TCP’s initial window is 14,600 bytes — nearly four times the QUIC budget — so on TCP this flight is one round trip at every setting, with over 10 kB spare. The warning you have heard about certificates overflowing the initcwnd is real arithmetic applied to the wrong budget.
  4. Now drag response size. At 100 kB the amber bar (one handshake) towers over the teal one (encrypting the body). The two meet at 3.43 MB on RSA-2048 and at 467 kB on ECDSA P-256 — the same control, a seven-fold difference, decided by the certificate again.
03

The intuition: a stack of laminated cards, and a switchboard that will not shout

You arrive at a building and the person inside wants proof of who you are. You cannot just say it. You hand over your own ID card, and because they have never heard of the office that issued it, you also hand over that office’s card, and the card of the office that issued that. Three laminated cards. The whole exchange is four sentences and three cards, and the cards are physically almost all of it.

Two things follow, and they are the whole lesson.

The cards are the bulk, and you did not choose their size. Whoever runs the card-issuing hierarchy decided how many cards there are and how big each one is. You can pick a card format that is smaller — that choice is yours — but you cannot decide to send fewer of them.

And there is a rule at the door. The person inside will not read out more than three times as much as you said first. Not to be difficult: because if they would, anyone could stand outside, say “send everything to that address over there”, and use the doorway as a megaphone aimed at a stranger. So the rule is denominated in your words, which makes the trick cost the attacker exactly as much as the victim. It is a good rule. It also means that if your cards are bulky, the doorman stops mid-sentence and waits for you to say something else — anything — before finishing. You get no error. You just wait.

Separately, there are two kinds of work here and they are priced differently. Unlocking the door is one heavy act, done once, no matter what you carry through it. Carrying boxes through the open door is light per box and never stops. Ask “is this door slow?” and the answer depends entirely on whether you are delivering an envelope or emptying a warehouse — and there is a specific number of boxes at which the two costs are equal.

04

The mechanism: every message, every field, and the two budgets

The sequence, with sizes

The messages are fixed by RFC 8446 §4 and the sizes below are arithmetic over the struct fields in those sections — no estimates. A modern lean client’s ClientHello is 275 bytes; a browser’s is larger because it adds GREASE values and pads to 512, which only makes the cleartext share bigger.

Three details in that figure are worth stopping on.

Only ServerHello is in cleartext. Everything from EncryptedExtensions onwards is already under handshake keys, which is why the braces are conventionally drawn around them. In TLS 1.2 the certificate is a plaintext record instead (RFC 5246 §7.4.2), and that is the largest privacy difference between the two versions — invisible in a round trip count.

Finished is 32 bytes of hash in 1.3 and 12 bytes in 1.2. Small, and a good check on whether a byte model was actually read off the RFC or remembered.

TLS 1.2 has a message TLS 1.3 does not: ServerKeyExchange, 333 bytes, carrying the ECDHE parameters and a second signature over them (RFC 4492 §5.4). TLS 1.3 folded that job into ServerHello’s key share and CertificateVerify. The 1.2 flight is 3,776 bytes to 1.3’s 3,822 — essentially the same size, one round trip apart, and with the certificate readable.

What is actually in a certificate

A certificate is DER: a tree of tag-length-value triples, where the length itself takes one byte below 128 and more above (X.690 §8.1.3), so a structure that crosses 256 bytes grows by two bytes of pure framing. Here is an RSA-2048 leaf, decomposed:

43% of that certificate is one public key and one signature, and both are set by an algorithm choice. The same leaf with ECDSA P-256 keys is 885 bytes, because a P-256 SubjectPublicKeyInfo is 91 bytes rather than 294 and its signature is about 71 rather than 256 — and the cryptographic share falls from 43% to 19%.

The third row is the one that surprises people: 264 bytes of every public leaf certificate is proof that it was logged. Certificate Transparency requires embedded signed timestamps, and at about 120 bytes each, two or three of them make the single largest extension in a modern leaf. A policy decision about auditability became a per-handshake byte cost on every connection on the internet.

The two budgets, and only one of them binds

QUIC: bytes the server may send = 3 × bytes received from an unvalidated address
TCP: bytes the server may send = initial congestion window = min(10·MSS, max(2·MSS, 14600))

RFC 9000 §8.1 for the first, and §14.1 for the 1,200-byte floor on the client’s Initial that makes it 3,600. RFC 6928 for the second, which at MSS 1460 is 14,600 bytes — this is the same initial window the bandwidth-delay lesson’s slow start starts from, imported from that model rather than re-derived. The two limits exist for completely different reasons: one is anti-reflection, one is congestion. They are not interchangeable and they differ by a factor of four.

They also fail differently, which is the part worth remembering. Overflowing the congestion window costs a round trip and everyone’s tooling can see it: the bytes go out in two flights, slow start doubles, life continues. Overflowing the amplification limit costs a round trip and produces no event at all — the server simply stops writing until the client says anything, so both endpoints believe the handshake went fine, because it did.

Record framing, which the application controls

Every TLS 1.3 record is 5 bytes of header, 1 byte of real content type hidden inside the encrypted part, and a 16-byte AEAD tag (RFC 8446 §5.1 and §5.2). It carries at most 16,384 bytes.

wire bytes = payload + 22 × ceil(payload ÷ bytes per write)

22 is the per-record overhead and it does not shrink with the record. bytes per write is capped at 16,384 by the record limit. Nothing in this expression is a protocol setting — the only variable is how much your application hands to the socket at a time.

A server that writes 100 bytes at a time pays that 22 bytes a thousand times for a 100 kB response. And it pays twice, because sealing a record has a fixed cost as well as a per-byte one — a key schedule lookup, a GHASH setup, a tag, and usually its own write syscall — so at a 100-byte record the fixed part is 99% of the CPU.

The crossover, which is a response size

crossover bytes = handshake CPU (µs) ÷ cipher cost (ns per byte) × 1000

Below it, the connection is handshake-bound: the cost is per connection, and the fixes are connection reuse, resumption and a cheaper signature. Above it, it is throughput-bound: the cost is per byte, and the fixes are a faster cipher or fewer bytes. The handshake term is dominated entirely by one signature, so this number is a property of your certificate.

Note the shape, because it is the same shape as the previous lesson’s byte crossover: a faster cipher pushes the crossover further out, so every improvement in symmetric performance widens the range of responses for which TLS cost is entirely per-connection.

Read the fourth column against the last. The certificate is 83–92% of the flight in every row, and the difference between the rows that fit and the rows that do not is entirely certificate bytes. Then read rows three and four: the same RSA-2048 key, one more intermediate, and the flight crosses the line. Nobody picks the number of intermediates. It is whatever the CA’s hierarchy is, plus whatever cross-signing it needs for old clients, and it can change under you when a CA rotates.

The last row is the sharpest version of the same point. One certificate covering a hundred hostnames means every client downloads all hundred names on every handshake — a 3,239-byte leaf, which is 1,958 bytes more than the two-name leaf above it, and 2,005 of those bytes are one extension holding other people’s domains.

One number I expected to be different

I built this model expecting the headline to be that a certificate chain overflows the initial congestion window, because that is the warning you see repeated. The model says it does not, and it is not close: at MSS 1460 the initial window is 14,600 bytes, an ordinary 3-certificate RSA-2048 flight is 3,822, and the headroom is 10,778 bytes. It takes 14 RSA-2048 certificates in one chain to need a second round trip on TCP, or nine RSA-4096 ones — configurations nobody has.

The binding budget turned out to be the one that is four times smaller and exists for an entirely different reason: QUIC’s anti-amplification limit, 3,600 bytes, which an ordinary chain crosses. So the widely repeated advice was arithmetic aimed at the wrong constant, and it becomes correct the moment you move to HTTP/3 — which is exactly when people stop thinking about certificate size, because the protocol is supposed to have solved round trips.

Second smaller surprise: I expected ECDSA to be a straight win on CPU. It is not. It is a win for the server by 7.4× and a loss for the client by 2.2×, and the reason it is still the right choice is a statement about who does how many handshakes, not about the algorithm.

06

The trade-off: every byte you remove is bought from somewhere

ECDSA is bought with compatibility and with client CPU. Very old clients do not support ECDSA at all, which is why CDNs run dual certificates — an ECDSA chain and an RSA chain on the same hostname, selected from the client’s signature_algorithms — and that means two pipelines, two expiries, two sets of OCSP staples and a selection bug surface that only ancient clients see. And the CPU moves to the client: 170 µs against 78 µs per handshake. Fine for a browser on a laptop, not obviously fine for a battery-powered device doing mutual TLS every fifteen minutes, which is the case the near recall question works through.

Certificate compression is bought with negotiation and attack surface. RFC 8879 compresses the Certificate message with zlib, brotli or zstd, and typically takes a large fraction off a chain — the standards-track admission that these bytes are a performance problem. But it is negotiated in the ClientHello, so it helps only clients that asked for it, and it puts a decompressor in the handshake path before authentication has completed. That is a deliberate, bounded risk, and it is not zero.

A shorter chain is mostly not yours to choose. You can drop a needlessly-sent root — a common misconfiguration that spends a whole certificate per handshake for nothing, because the client already has the root or does not trust it — and after that the hierarchy is your CA’s. Running a private CA gets you a two-certificate chain and loses public trust, which is the right trade inside a service mesh and the wrong one on the public internet.

Fewer names per certificate costs you certificate management. Splitting a 100-name certificate into 100 single-name ones removes about 1,950 bytes from every handshake and multiplies your issuance, renewal and monitoring by a hundred.

Filling records is bought with latency, and it is Nagle’s argument again. Buffering to 16,384 bytes before writing takes the wire expansion from 22% to 0.15% and the framing CPU from 1.35 ms to 20 µs — by holding bytes back. For a streaming response, a chat token or an interactive protocol that is exactly the wrong trade, and it is the same trade-off as TCP_NODELAY one layer up. The resolution is the same too: buffer where the data is bulk, flush where the data is a message, and never let a framework decide that for you silently.

Raising QUIC’s budget costs bytes on every connection. The limit is three times what the client sent, so a client that pads its Initial to 1,500 bytes raises the budget to 4,500 and the RSA flight fits. You have now spent 300 extra bytes on every connection, including the resumed ones that had no certificate to receive, to fix the cold ones — and you have moved closer to path MTU problems.

And the honest ceiling: none of this makes the last round trip smaller. Everything on this page removes bytes, and bytes only become latency when they cross a budget. Get under both budgets and the cold handshake is back to the round trip count the previous lesson gives you, which is one, plus the request. There is no fourth round trip to find.

One thing you cannot buy at all: the shape. Encryption hides the payload. It does not hide the hostname (24 bytes of cleartext ClientHello, and it cannot be encrypted under a key that depends on which certificate the hostname selects), the application protocol, the client library’s fingerprint, or the length and timing of every single record — the record length field is unencryptable by construction, because the receiver needs it to find the end of the record. TLS 1.3 permits record padding for precisely this and almost nobody enables it, because it costs bytes to buy a privacy property you can only measure statistically.

07

In the wild

The IETF standardised certificate compression, which is an admission. RFC 8879, TLS Certificate Compression (Ghedini & Vasiliev, December 2020), adds a compress_certificate extension and lets the Certificate message be sent compressed with zlib, brotli or zstd. A working group does not spend a standards-track RFC on compressing one message unless that message is the problem, and this lesson’s arithmetic says which message it is: 88% of the server’s flight. If you want one piece of external evidence that the certificate chain is the biggest thing on the wire, it is that TLS grew a compression negotiation for it and for nothing else.

QUIC’s amplification limit is normative, and it is the reason a big chain becomes a round trip. RFC 9000 §8.1 caps a server at three times the bytes received from an unvalidated address, and §14.1 puts a 1,200-byte floor under the client’s first Initial datagram. Both numbers are in the specification, both are load-bearing, and neither appears in a TLS metric. If you are measuring an HTTP/3 deployment and seeing two setup round trips with no Retry packet and no HelloRetryRequest, the arithmetic to do is the size of your certificate chain against 3,600.

Certificate Transparency put 264 bytes into every leaf on the internet. RFC 6962 (Laurie, Langley & Kasper, June 2013) defines the signed certificate timestamp, and browser policy made embedded SCTs effectively mandatory for public certificates. Each one is about 120 bytes and two or three are normal, which makes them the largest extension in a modern leaf — larger than the subject, the issuer and the validity period put together. It is a good trade: CT is how mis-issuance gets caught. It is also a clean example of a security policy that is paid for in bytes, per handshake, by everyone, forever — the sort of cost that never appears in the document that decided it.

The record framing story has a much older twin. Nagle’s algorithm and delayed ACKs exist because small writes were already expensive one layer down; TLS added a second, larger per-write tax on top of the same mistake, and unlike Nagle there is no TCP_NODELAY to argue about because the framing is mandatory. The fix in both cases is the same and lives in the application: know whether the thing you are writing is a message or a stream.

Where this goes next. connection-setup-cost has the round trip counts these bytes are charged against, and the ladder it builds is the thing this lesson’s budgets can add one to. bandwidth-delay-product owns the initial congestion window and slow start — this model imports its initialWindow and roundsNoLoss rather than re-deriving them, so the two lessons cannot disagree about what IW10 is. byte-budgets is the same counting discipline applied to payloads instead of handshakes, and cost-per-million-requests is where 268 Mbit/s of certificate turns into money.

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A server's first TLS 1.3 flight is about 88% Certificate message; with a 3-certificate RSA-2048 chain it is 3,822 bytes over TCP and 4,001 over QUIC, against QUIC's 3,600-byte anti-amplification budget — so the certificate, not the protocol version, decides whether a cold QUIC handshake is one round trip or two
  • The two budgets a first flight is charged against differ by four times: QUIC allows 3 × the client's Initial datagram, so 3,600 bytes (RFC 9000 §8.1), while TCP allows the initial congestion window of 14,600 bytes (RFC 6928) — which is why the chain is a round trip on QUIC and takes 14 RSA-2048 certificates to trouble TCP
  • Asymmetric crypto is charged per handshake and symmetric per byte, so there is a response size at which the two are equal: 3.43 MB for an RSA-2048 server, 467 kB for an ECDSA P-256 one
  • RSA-2048 signs in 326 µs and verifies in 10 µs; ECDSA P-256 signs in 13 µs and verifies in 33 µs — so RSA charges the party doing thousands of handshakes a second and ECDSA charges the party doing one
  • A cipher suite names three separate jobs — key exchange, authentication, bulk encryption — and TLS 1.3's suite name carries only the last one plus the hash, because the other two moved into the supported_groups, key_share and signature_algorithms extensions
  • Every TLS 1.3 record costs 22 bytes (5 header + 1 inner content type + 16 AEAD tag) and holds at most 16,384, so the application's write size sets the record count: 100 kB written 100 bytes at a time is 1,024 records and 22% expansion
  • Encryption hides the payload and not the shape: SNI and ALPN are cleartext in every version of TLS, the entire certificate chain is cleartext in TLS 1.2, and every record's length and timing is readable in both

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A team migrates an API to HTTP/3 expecting one round trip of setup and measures two on cold connections. The handshake succeeds, CPU is flat, the cipher is AES-128-GCM, and the TLS metrics show nothing wrong. Where is the second round trip, and what number would you check first?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The server's first flight does not fit the budget it is allowed to send before the client's address is validated. RFC 9000 §8.1 caps a server at three times the bytes it has received, and a client's first Initial datagram is at least 1,200 bytes (§14.1) — so the budget is 3,600 bytes. With a 3-certificate RSA-2048 chain the flight is 4,001 bytes: the server sends 3,600, stops 401 bytes short of its own Finished, and waits for any packet from the client to lift the limit. That wait is the extra round trip, and it is invisible to every TLS metric because nothing failed. The number to check first is the size of your certificate chain: 3,329 bytes here, wrapped in a Certificate message that is 88% of the whole flight. Switching the same chain to ECDSA P-256 makes it 2,141 bytes and the flight 2,575, which fits with 1,025 to spare.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Two engineers argue about a TLS certificate. One says RSA-2048 versus ECDSA P-256 is a security question with no performance consequence; the other says it is a performance question. Give the four numbers that settle it, and say who pays each one.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Bytes: a 3-certificate chain is 3,329 bytes in RSA-2048 and 2,141 in ECDSA P-256, because a 2048-bit public key is 294 bytes of SubjectPublicKeyInfo against 91, and its signature is 256 bytes against about 71. Round trips: that difference straddles QUIC's 3,600-byte limit, so RSA costs one extra round trip on a cold QUIC connection and ECDSA costs none. Server CPU: the signature is 326 µs for RSA-2048 and 13 µs for ECDSA P-256, so at 10,000 handshakes a second that is 3.26 cores against 0.13. Client CPU: the direction reverses — RSA verification is 10 µs and ECDSA verification 33 µs, so the client's handshake goes from 78 µs to 170 µs. The trade is deliberate and correct: the server does thousands of handshakes a second and each client does one, so moving work to the client is the right direction.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A gRPC service streams a 100 kB response and the platform team notices its TLS bytes on the wire are 22% higher than the payload while a neighbouring service is at 0.15%. Neither has touched the cipher. What is different, and what is the cost besides bytes?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The write size. A TLS 1.3 record costs 22 bytes whatever it carries — 5 bytes of header, 1 byte of inner content type, 16 bytes of AEAD tag (RFC 8446 §5.1, §5.2) — and holds at most 16,384. Writing 100 bytes at a time makes 1,024 records and 22,528 bytes of framing; buffering to the record limit makes 7 records and 154 bytes. The second cost is larger than the first: each record carries a fixed CPU charge for sealing and usually its own write syscall, so the chatty version burns about 1.35 ms against 20 µs — more CPU than four RSA-2048 signatures for one response. And it is a real trade-off, not a free win: buffering to fill a record means holding bytes back, which is the same argument as Nagle's algorithm against time to first byte.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    "It is all encrypted, so an observer learns nothing." Name four things an on-path observer reads off a TLS 1.3 connection anyway, and one more that TLS 1.2 gives away and 1.3 does not.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    On 1.3: the hostname, because the server_name extension is 24 bytes of cleartext ClientHello sent before any key exists (RFC 6066 §3) — it has to be, since the server needs it to pick which certificate to present; the application protocol, from the client's cleartext ALPN list (RFC 7301); the client's identity, because its exact extension set, cipher list and group list are a stable fingerprint of library and version, which is the basis of JA3/JA4; and the size and timing of every record, because the record length field cannot be encrypted — the receiver needs it to find the end of the record. That last one is enough to identify which page was fetched. The 1.2-only extra is the entire certificate chain, sent as a plaintext record (RFC 5246 §7.4.2) — issuer, validity, and the full SAN list, so on a shared certificate you learn every other hostname it covers. TLS 1.3 sends it under handshake keys.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A fleet of 40,000 IoT sensors does mutual TLS to a gateway, each sensor reconnecting every 15 minutes and sending 900 bytes. The gateway is at 70% CPU and the network graph is flat. An engineer proposes ECDSA P-256 everywhere "because it is smaller". What breaks, and what is the actual shape of the problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Two things the lesson's own numbers predict. First, mutual TLS doubles the certificate problem and moves half of it onto the sensor: the client now sends its own chain and its own CertificateVerify, so the sensor signs once per connection and the gateway verifies a chain per connection. Second, the direction of the ECDSA trade reverses on the client side — ECDSA verification is 33 µs against RSA's 10 µs, and a sensor is exactly the party that cannot afford verification. The right split is asymmetric: ECDSA for the gateway's certificate (it signs 44 times a second, and 13 µs beats 326 µs), and the sensor's own key chosen on what the sensor pays. Then the shape: 40,000 reconnects every 900 seconds is 44 handshakes a second, so at 362 µs each the gateway spends 1.6% of one core on handshakes — the CPU is not the handshake. And the 900-byte payload is far below every crossover in this lesson, so the connection is handshake-bound by a factor of thousands: the fix is not a cipher, it is not reconnecting every 15 minutes. Reuse, or session resumption, deletes the certificate entirely.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a responder that caps how much it will send to a requester who has not yet proved the return address is really theirs — because otherwise the channel is a weapon pointed at whoever that address names. Name instances outside computing, and say what the cap is denominated in.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The abstraction is amplification control, and the cap is always denominated in what the requester already spent rather than in an absolute limit — which is the clever part, because it makes the attack cost the attacker as much as the victim. Instances: reply-paid post, where a business caps the size and count of prepaid returns precisely because otherwise anyone could mass-submit a rival's address and have the bulk mailed there; a bank's new-payee hold, which limits the first transfer to a freshly added account until the account is confirmed; a locksmith or pharmacy that will hand over only a token quantity until identity and address are verified in person; freight collect shipping, refused above a value until the consignee confirms; and a callback protocol at a call centre, which will not read an account back to you on an inbound call but will ring the number on file. In every case the mechanism is the same and so is the failure it produces: a legitimate requester with an unusually large legitimate request gets stalled and told nothing, exactly as a QUIC server with a large certificate chain stalls with no error anywhere in its logs.
    Did you have that?
