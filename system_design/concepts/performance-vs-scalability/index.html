Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Performance vs scalability explained well nowhere else

The 8 ms design and the 12 ms design, and why the slower one does six times the work

Performance is how long one unit of work takes for one user. Scalability is whether that number survives more load or more data. They are separate axes, all four quadrants are populated, and the interesting designs are the ones where buying one sells the other. The number worth carrying: put 1.2 ms of an 8 ms request inside a lock and you have set a ceiling of 833 requests a second that no amount of hardware moves.

01

2.8 milliseconds in staging, 26 seconds in production

A report endpoint joins two tables in application memory. Against the test fixture — 1,000 rows on each side — it answers in 2.8 ms. It is the fastest endpoint in the service. It was written that way on purpose: no hash table to build, no allocation, no spill path, perfect cache locality.

Production has 100,000 rows on each side. The same endpoint takes 26.0 seconds.

Nothing changed. No deploy, no regression, no bad index. The code does exactly what it did in staging, which is compare every row against every row: a thousand rows is a million comparisons, a hundred thousand rows is ten billion. 100× the data is 10,000× the work.

The hash join it replaced looks worse on every benchmark anyone ran:

Read the crossing row, because it is where the whole lesson lives. 1,764 rows sits above every fixture anybody writes and below every table anybody ships. Both designs are correct, both were measured honestly, and the measurement was taken on the wrong side of the only number that mattered.

Predict Two implementations of one endpoint. A answers in 8 ms, of which 1.2 ms is inside a mutex only one request may hold. B answers in 12 ms, of which 0.06 ms is. On 64 workers, which delivers more requests a second, and roughly by how much?
Fleet throughput can never exceed 1/S, where S is the part of a request that only one request at a time may execute. A's ceiling is 1/1.2 ms = 833/s and it is already pinned at it by 12 workers. B's is 1/0.06 ms = 16,667/s, and at 64 workers it is doing 5,321/s — 6.4× A. The first option is the belief this lesson exists to break: a 1.5× difference in per-request time produced a 20× difference in ceiling, pointing the other way. And by 64 workers A is not even the faster one per request — 76.8 ms against 12.0 ms.

02

The obvious fix, and the ceiling it installs

The obvious fix for a slow endpoint is to stop asking the database. Keep the hot index in process, behind a mutex so concurrent requests cannot corrupt it. This is a good optimisation and it works: the request goes from 12.0 ms to 8.0 ms, a third faster, measured correctly on a real box.

The mental model almost everyone brings to that result is that the fleet just got a third faster too. Throughput is requests per second, latency is seconds per request, so make the request quicker and more of them fit in a second. Multiply by the worker count and you are done.

Here is what the two designs actually do as the fleet grows:

Three things in that table, and none of them is visible at one worker.

The fast design stops improving at 12 workers. 833 requests a second, forever. Workers 13 through 256 produce nothing at all; fleet efficiency at 64 is 10.4%, meaning 57 machines’ worth of capacity has been bought and is idle by construction.

They are not idle in a harmless way. The requests those workers admit do not vanish — they queue at the mutex. So the request that took 8.00 ms on an idle box takes 76.8 ms at 64 workers and 307 ms at 256. Throughput flattens; latency does not. Past the ceiling, buying a machine converts money directly into latency.

The crossover is at 10 workers. Not 10,000 — ten. The design that was 1.5× slower for one user is ahead of the “optimised” one on any fleet you would actually run, and by 256 workers it is doing 20× the work at a twentieth of the latency.

Put the rig’s serialised part slider at its floor and the crimson curve tracks the dashed diagonal all the way out, the amber marker disappears, and the readout says there is no crossover. That is the same optimisation with the sharing removed, and it is simply correct. The mutex was never the point of the change; it was the price.

03

The intuition

A kitchen with one pass window.

Every dish takes eight minutes to make. Seven of those minutes are a chef at a station — chopping, searing, plating — and any number of chefs can do that at once. The last minute and a bit is at the pass: the head chef checks the plate, wipes the rim, calls it away. One plate at a time, because there is one pass.

Hire a second chef and dishes per hour nearly doubles. A fourth, and it nearly quadruples. Somewhere around the twelfth chef something changes, and it does not announce itself: the pass is now busy all the time. Chefs are finishing plates faster than the head chef can call them away, so plates start stacking up at the window. Dishes served per hour stops rising — it is pinned at sixty divided by the pass time, and nothing in the rest of the kitchen affects that number.

Hire the thirteenth chef and the restaurant serves exactly as many dishes as before. What changes is that each dish now sits under a heat lamp for longer. The kitchen’s speed and the kitchen’s capacity turned out to be two different questions, and hiring answers only one of them.

Performance is how long one dish takes when the kitchen is empty. Scalability is what happens to the number of dishes an hour when you add chefs. A step that every dish must pass through alone improves the first — one pass is faster than a committee — and caps the second at 1 divided by the time spent there.

Two things carry over exactly. First, the pass does not have to be slow to be the ceiling. One minute out of eight is 15% of the work, and it is 100% of the limit. Second, you cannot find it by timing one dish. A stopwatch on an empty kitchen measures eight minutes and tells you nothing about the twelfth chef.

04

The mechanism

Two definitions, kept apart on purpose:

performance — how long ONE unit of work takes, at one size, for one user

A duration. Milliseconds. Measured at concurrency 1 and at whatever n your fixture has.

scalability — whether that duration, or the rate achieved, HOLDS as load or data grows

Not a value: a derivative. There is no such thing as “how scalable is it” in milliseconds, and any answer in milliseconds is an answer to the other question.

Because they are separate properties, all four combinations exist, and the same word — faster — is used for every one of them:

scalesdoes not scale
fast for one user0.02 ms serialised of 3.02 ms · 99.5% efficient at 641.2 ms of 8.00 ms · 10.4% efficient at 64
slow for one user0.06 ms of 40.06 ms · 100.0% efficient at 649 ms of 49.00 ms · 8.5% efficient at 64

The top-right cell is the one that gets shipped, because it wins every benchmark that is run at one concurrency — which is every benchmark that is easy to run.

The load axis: 1/S is the whole story

Split each request into the part that only one request at a time may execute and the part that does not care:

ceiling = 1 / S

S — seconds of each request spent inside the shared thing: a mutex, a single-writer row, a sequence generator, one partition’s leader, a global counter. Whatever else you buy, the fleet cannot complete more than 1/S requests a second.

1.2 ms gives 833 a second. 0.06 ms gives 16,667. The ratio of the ceilings is the inverse ratio of the serialised parts and has nothing to do with the request times, which is why a 1.5× performance win came with a 20× scalability loss and nobody in the review noticed: the two numbers do not appear in the same sentence anywhere in the diff.

What happens between one worker and the ceiling is queueing, and it is the queueing (queueing) that turns “flattens” into “gets worse”. The model here solves the exact case — N requests in flight, each spending its parallel time and then queueing at a single-server station — by Mean Value Analysis, and a seeded discrete-event simulation of the same system agrees to within 0.12% across 1 to 64 workers.

And it is not Amdahl’s Law. Amdahl describes one job carved into a serial and a parallel part, so it charges you the full serial time of every one of the other N−1 workers. N independent requests only pay for the ones actually queued when they arrive, which is fewer:

Both laws agree at one worker and both converge on the same ceiling of 1/S, which is the part a design decision rests on. But Amdahl is wrong in the middle, in the pessimistic direction, and the middle is where a fleet actually lives.

The data axis: an exponent beats a constant, eventually

The same independence shows up with n in place of N. Cost of one unit of work is a sum of terms with different exponents, and “faster” is only defined once you say at which n:

cost(n) = setup + a·n + b·n·log₂n + c·n²

The nested loop has a tiny setup and a real c. The hash join has a large setup, no c at all. Which one is faster is a question about n and nothing else.

At 1,000 rows the nested loop’s n² term is already 92% of its cost — and its cost is 2.8 ms, so nobody looks. That is the trap: the superlinear share is high long before the absolute number is alarming, so the signal exists in staging and is invisible.

05

The numbers

When does the load axis matter? As soon as the fleet is bigger than the ceiling divided by one worker’s throughput. For the 8 ms / 1.2 ms design that is 12 workers — one thread pool. This is not a 100k-rps problem; it is a one-pod problem.

Read the right-hand column against the left. A serialised share of 15% is not a 15% problem. The share and the loss are not on the same scale at all: dropping it from 15% to 1% is a 15-fold increase in ceiling, and it is the difference between a design that stops at one pod and one that runs to 160 workers before anybody has to think about it.

When does the data axis matter? At the crossover, which is the number nobody computes: 1,764 rows. Below it the nested loop is genuinely the right choice — at 100 rows it is 47× faster and the hash join’s 3 ms setup is pure waste. Above it the nested loop is a bug with a timestamp on it.

And the failure mode past a ceiling is not a plateau. At 256 workers the fast design’s latency is 307 ms against 8.00 ms at one — a 38× inflation, all of it queueing, none of it work. A system pinned at its ceiling with load still arriving is the metastable state from queueing, not a comfortable saturation.

The other shape: a shared copy rather than a shared lock. If the cache lives on every node instead of behind a mutex, nothing is serialised — and every write must be told to every node, which is a cost between pairs of nodes and therefore quadratic. Capacity does not flatten, it reverses. With a 2% serialised share and a coherency coefficient of 0.0005, relative capacity peaks at 44 nodes and a fleet of 88 delivers 14.6% less than 44 did. This is the κ term of the Universal Scalability Law, and it is the reason “we removed the locks” is not a claim about scalability.

06

The trade-off

Buying headroom costs single-request latency, nearly every time. Look at what the scalable side of every one of these pairs actually costs. Sharding a hot counter into 64 stripes means a read must now sum 64 stripes. Replicating for read capacity means a write is not visible immediately. Partitioning means a query that spanned partitions is now a scatter-gather with a tail instead of a lookup. Batching for throughput makes the first item in the batch wait for the last (throughput-vs-latency). The stateless design in this lesson is 12 ms instead of 8 — that 4 ms is the price of its ceiling, it is real, and every user pays it forever, including at 3 a.m. when there are nine of them.

So the fast-and-unscalable quadrant is not a mistake in the small. At 100 rows the nested loop is 47× faster and correct. At one pod the shared index is a third faster and correct. The defect is not the design; it is shipping it without knowing where its crossover is, because past the crossover the same design is a 620× regression that no profiler will attribute to the change that caused it — the change was correct when it was made, and the row count changed later, silently, without a deploy.

Measuring performance is easy and measuring scalability is expensive. A p50 comes from one request. A ceiling comes from a load test at several concurrencies with a fitted curve, and it needs production-shaped data, because the crossover is a property of n. This asymmetry is the actual reason the wrong number gets used: it is the one you already have. A canary of one pod at low load is structurally incapable of detecting a ceiling regression, and it will report the p50 win truthfully while it happens.

And the honest version of this lesson refuses a rule. There is no general answer to whether to pay 4 ms for a twenty-fold ceiling, because it depends on a number about the future — how big the fleet and the table get. What is not a judgement call is stating both numbers. A design review that records “8 ms, ceiling 833/s at 12 workers” against “12 ms, ceiling 16,667/s” has done the whole job, whichever one it then picks. One that records “8 ms, 33% faster” has measured one axis and asserted the other.

07

In the wild

The Big Kernel Lock. Early Linux SMP support serialised the kernel behind one lock. That is the fastest design on one CPU — a single uncontended lock is a few instructions and no cache-line ping-pong — and a hard ceiling on many. Removing it took roughly fifteen years of incremental work and finished in 2.6.39 (2011), and the replacement was not “no lock”: it was many narrower locks, which is exactly the 833 × k move above.

MongoDB’s write lock, three times. A single process-wide write lock became database-level in 2.2 (2012) and document-level with WiredTiger in 3.0 (2015). Each step lowered S and left per-request latency roughly where it was; the releases were sold on concurrency, which is the correct word for what changed.

Memcached’s global cache lock. Early versions guarded the hash table and LRU with one lock, and the fix was per-bucket and per-item locking. Facebook’s Scaling Memcache at Facebook (NSDI 2013) reports exactly this class of work — removing serialisation from the fast path — and is worth reading as a catalogue of a decade of finding S in a system nobody thought had one.

Query planners compute the crossover this lesson computes. PostgreSQL’s cost-based planner chooses nested-loop, hash or merge join by estimating each one’s cost at the estimated row count. That is the same arithmetic as the table at the top of this page, run per query, and it is why a plan flip after a statistics change can turn a 3 ms query into a 26-second one with no code change at all: the planner’s estimate of n moved across a crossover.

The single hot row. An AUTO_INCREMENT counter, an inventory quantity, a per-account balance — one row every write must serialise on. The standard fix is a sharded counter: k rows, writes hashed across them, reads summing k. Ceiling multiplied by k, read path k times more expensive. The trade is explicit and it is the same trade as everything else on this page.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Performance is a duration for one unit of work at one size; scalability is whether that duration or the achieved rate holds as load or data grows — a value versus a derivative
  • The two are independent, so all four quadrants exist: fast and unscalable, slow and scalable, and both of the obvious ones
  • If S is the part of each request that only one request at a time may execute, fleet throughput can never exceed 1/S, whatever the fleet size
  • Every worker you add above the ceiling produces nothing and still inflates latency, because the extra requests wait at the shared part instead of being served
  • A design with a small constant and a bad exponent beats one with a large constant and a good exponent up to a crossover row count, and the crossover is usually above fixture size and below production size
  • Amdahl's Law describes one job carved into a serial and a parallel part; for N independent requests contending on a shared resource it charges all N−1 others against you and under-predicts throughput by up to 53%
  • A shared thing that is copied rather than locked costs pairwise between nodes, so capacity peaks and then falls — more machines make it slower
  • A benchmark run at one concurrency measures performance only, and cannot detect a scaling ceiling at all

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  4. Cued recall

    Why is Amdahl's Law the wrong law for a fleet of independent requests sharing one lock, and which direction is it wrong in?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Amdahl describes one job split into a serial part and a parallel part, so it charges you the full serial time of every one of the other N−1 workers. N independent requests only cost you the ones actually queued at the instant you arrive, which is fewer. So Amdahl under-predicts: at 8 workers it says 488/s where the exact closed-network model says 747/s and a discrete-event simulation measures 746.6/s — Amdahl is 53% low. Both agree at one worker and both agree on the ceiling of 1/S, which is the part a design decision actually rests on.     Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A design keeps a cache on every node instead of behind a lock, so nothing is serialised. What is the new failure shape, and where is its peak?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Coherency. Every write has to be told to every other node, and there are N(N−1) ordered pairs, so the cost is quadratic in the fleet — which does not flatten capacity, it reverses it. With a 2% serialised share and κ = 0.0005 the peak is at 44 nodes, and doubling to 88 delivers 14.6% less than 44 did. A single shared lock can only cap you; a shared copy can take capacity back. This is the κ term of the Universal Scalability Law, and it is why "no locks" is not the same claim as "scalable".
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team replaces a per-request database call with an in-process LRU cache guarded by one mutex. p50 falls from 12 ms to 8 ms on the canary, which is one pod. They roll it out to 200 pods behind a load balancer and total requests per second falls. What happened, and what is the fix that keeps the cache?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The mutex is per pod, so nothing about the 200-pod fleet is serialised across pods — which means the fleet ceiling did not move and the p50 win is real. The thing to check first is therefore not the cache at all: it is what got shared inside each pod. Each pod has its own worker pool, and if the mutex is held for 1.2 ms of an 8 ms request then that pod cannot exceed 833 requests a second however many threads it runs — so a pod that used to serve 1,000/s on 16 threads now serves 833/s, and 200 pods lose 33,400 requests a second between them. The fix is to remove the serialisation, not the cache: shard the cache into k independent maps with their own locks and the ceiling becomes 833 × k, or use a lock-free read path so S is the write share only. The general statement is that a per-request improvement is measured at concurrency 1 and a ceiling only exists above it, so a canary of one pod at low load is structurally incapable of detecting this class of regression. It is also why the honest canary metric is requests a second at a fixed latency budget rather than p50.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system's speed at one unit of demand and its behaviour as demand grows are independent properties, and any step that routes all units through one shared thing improves the first while capping the second. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A single-till shop. One till is the fastest possible checkout when there is one customer — no queue discipline, no lane choice, no idle staff. It also caps the shop at 60/(minutes per transaction) customers an hour, and hiring more shelf-stackers moves that number not at all; what it moves is how long each customer stands in line. Other instances: a hospital with one CT scanner, where every pathway is fast until the scanner is the pathway; a single runway airport, where taxi and gate improvements are free and the runway is 1/S; a notary or a single approving signature in any bureaucracy, which is the purest form — the step takes two minutes and sets the throughput of the whole institution; a one-lane bridge, where widening the roads on both sides raises the queue and not the flow; and a kitchen with one pass window, where hiring chefs raises dishes started per hour and not dishes served. In every case the diagnostic question is the same and it is not "how fast is it": it is what must every unit pass through alone, and how long does it spend there.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
