Tier 1 · The network Port exhaustion explained well nowhere else

The service handled 460 requests a second cleanly and fell over at 480

A connection is identified by five things, four of which are fixed when you talk to one backend, so the only free variable is the source port — and there are 28,232 of them. A closed socket holds its port for another 60 seconds, which turns a count into a rate: about 470 new connections a second to any one destination, forever, no matter how fast the requests are. A port pool has no waiting room, so the failure is Erlang-B, and the first failures appear 0.8% below the ceiling. There is no degraded region to alert on.

01

Nothing is saturated and the connections are being refused

A service talks to one database. It opens a connection, runs a 20-millisecond query, closes it. The code is unremarkable and has been in production for two years.

Traffic grows. At 460 requests a second everything is clean. At 480 requests a second the service starts returning errors, and the errors are these:

dial tcp 10.4.2.11:5432: connect: cannot assign requested address

EADDRNOTAVAIL. Not a refusal from the database, not a timeout, not a reset — the client’s own kernel declining to create a socket. Meanwhile:

  • CPU is at 31%.
  • Memory is flat.
  • The database is bored: 6% CPU, no lock waits, query time unchanged at 20 ms.
  • The network interface is carrying 40 Mbit/s on a 10 Gbit/s link.
  • There are 47 connections open to the database, which is well under its limit of 500.

Every dashboard says the system is idle. And the thing that broke is not a resource anybody provisions, monitors or thinks about: it is the number 28,232, and it was reached at a rate of 470 connections per second that nothing in the request path can make faster.

Predict The queries take 20 ms. Suppose you optimise them to 2 ms — ten times faster. What happens to the rate at which this service starts failing?
A port is held for the connection's life plus TIME_WAIT, and TIME_WAIT is 60 seconds. Residence goes from 60.02 s to 60.002 s, so the ceiling moves from 470.4/s to 470.5/s — 0.03%. The tenfold answer is the belief this lesson breaks: it assumes the port is occupied by the work, when 99.97% of the occupancy is the quarantine after the work. This is also why the bug is so hard to find — every instinct says a faster backend gives more headroom, and here it gives none.
Show it without answering
02

Raise the connection limit. Widen the port range. Neither is the problem.

The first three things everyone tries, in order, and what each one does.

Raise the database’s max_connections. It was never the limit — there are 47 connections open against an allowance of 500. The error comes from the client’s kernel before a packet is sent. This one is easy to rule out and it still costs an hour, because the error message says “connect” and connect problems are usually the other end.

Widen net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range to 1024 65535. This is the correct diagnosis with the wrong response, and it does work — for a while. It takes the range from 28,232 ports to 64,512, so the ceiling goes from 470/s to 1,075/s. That is 2.29×, and it is the most this lever can ever give, from any starting point, because a port number is a 16-bit field and 65,535 is the whole of it. One year of growth eats it. And it has a cost nobody mentions, which the numbers section comes back to: it makes the failure sharper.

Set net.ipv4.tcp_tw_reuse=1. Bigger — a factor of about 60, because an outbound connect() may then reuse a TIME_WAIT tuple after roughly a second instead of a minute, when TCP timestamps confirm the ordering is safe. It is also the point at which you are deliberately giving up the protection TIME_WAIT exists to provide, on purpose, in production, to work around a design problem one layer up. (Its old sibling tcp_tw_recycle did something similar and was removed from Linux in 4.12 because it silently broke clients behind NAT: it kept per-host timestamp state, and many hosts behind one address do not present one coherent timestamp clock.)

Now the part that makes all three of these the wrong conversation.

A connection is identified by five things, not one: protocol, source address, source port, destination address, destination port. Four of those are fixed here — TCP, this host, that database, port 5432. So the only field that can distinguish two of this service’s connections is the source port, and the kernel is choosing it from a finite range.

And a closed port is not a free port. The side that closes first holds the tuple in TIME_WAIT for 2·MSL, so that a delayed duplicate segment from the old connection cannot be delivered into a new connection that reuses the same tuple. On Linux that is 60 seconds, and — the detail that stops most of the tuning conversations — it is TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN in include/net/tcp.h, a compile-time constant. There is no sysctl.

So the port’s residence time is the query plus a minute:

That is the whole bug. Little’s Law turns a residence time into an occupancy, and an occupancy limit into a rate limit:

ports held = connections per second × (hold + 60)
ceiling    = ports ÷ 60           ← the hold barely appears

At 400 connections a second the service is holding 24,008 source ports, and it is doing useful work in about 8 of them.

03

The intuition

A hospital has eight operating theatres. An operation takes 15 minutes. How many operations an hour?

The tempting answer is 32 — four per theatre. The real answer depends on something the question did not mention: after every case the theatre must be cleaned, restocked and changed over, and that takes 40 minutes. So a theatre is occupied for 55 minutes per 15-minute operation, and eight theatres do about 8.7 operations an hour, not 32.

Now notice what does and does not help.

Making the operation faster does almost nothing. Get the surgeon down to 5 minutes and the theatre is occupied 45 minutes instead of 55 — an 18% gain on a 67% speedup, because you optimised the small part. This is exactly the 20 ms query: the work was never the occupancy.

Adding theatres helps proportionally, and there is a building. You can go from eight to twelve. You cannot go to eight hundred. This is the port range: a real lever, with a hard bound.

Reducing the turnover helps a lot, and it is the thing everyone is scared to touch, because the clean-down is there for a reason and shortening it is a decision about infection, not about throughput. This is tcp_tw_reuse.

And doing several cases per setup changes the arithmetic entirely. One clean-down, then four operations back to back: 40 + 60 = 100 minutes for four cases instead of 220. This is connection pooling, and note what makes it different from the others — it is not a bigger number anywhere. It is amortising the fixed cost over more work, which is why it has no ceiling while everything else does.

04

The mechanism

Why the destination is the interesting field

Uniqueness is over the whole tuple, so port 41000 may be in use against the database and against a cache and against forty different pods, simultaneously. The ceiling is per (source address, destination endpoint) pair, and either side multiplies it:

configurationtuple space
one source address, one endpoint28,232
one source address, 40 endpoints1,129,280
four source addresses, 40 endpoints4,517,120
40 endpoints, but bind() before connect()28,232

That last row is a one-line code change with a fortyfold consequence. connect() on an unbound socket lets the kernel pick a port knowing the destination, so it only needs uniqueness against that destination. Binding a source address first forces the choice before the destination is known, so the port must be unique across the whole host. Linux 4.2 added IP_BIND_ADDRESS_NO_PORT to defer the port choice and get the multiplier back.

Why the failure has no warning region

A connect() that finds no free port does not wait. There is no queue for a source port — it returns EADDRNOTAVAIL immediately. That makes a port range a loss system: c servers, no waiting room, and blocking given by Erlang-B (Erlang, 1917):

B(0, a) = 1
B(k, a) = a·B(k−1, a) / (k + a·B(k−1, a))        a = offered load in erlangs = rate × residence

Two properties of that formula do the explaining, and both are checked against a simulation rather than asserted:

It does not care about the shape of the holding-time distribution. Erlang-B is exact for M/G/c/c, so only the mean residence time enters. The model’s test runs the same offered load with fixed, exponential and heavy-tailed holds and requires all three to land on the same closed-form answer — which is what licenses quoting one number for real traffic that is nothing like exponential.

Below the ceiling it is flat, and above it, it is a proportion. A loss system carries at most ports/residence connections a second whatever is offered, so

error rate  =  1 − carried / offered  ≥  1 − ceiling / offered

and it sits essentially on that bound. Half of connects fail at twice the ceiling; reaching 99% needs about a hundred times it. So the shape is a knee with nothing before it, not a cliff with something after it — and it is the “nothing before it” that makes this an outage rather than a capacity warning.

When headroom belongs to someone else. A sidecar making 4,000 connections a second across 40 upstream pods sits at 100/s per endpoint and 21% occupancy — comfortable. Lose 38 of those pods to a rolling deploy and each of the two survivors takes 2,000/s: 76.5% of connects fail. The sidecar’s configuration did not change. Worse is shared SNAT: 80 instances egressing through one NAT address at 8,000 connections a second need 480,400 tuples against a 28,232-port space — 1,702% — and no single instance can measure the limit it is hitting.

06

The trade-off

The fix that works has a price, and so does every fix that does not.

Pooling moves the queue into your process. A pool is a finite set of connections with waiting callers, which is a queue, which means Erlang-C behaviour, a wait time that explodes near saturation, and a new decision — the pool size — that is now yours to get wrong. You have traded a kernel limit you did not know about for an application limit you have to size. That is a good trade and it is still a trade; the sizing is its own lesson.

Pooling holds connections open, and the backend counts those. 200 app instances with a pool of 20 is 4,000 connections a database must hold whether or not they are busy — and each one costs server-side memory and a backend process or thread. This is the exact reason PgBouncer exists. Removing a client-side port limit can create a server-side connection limit.

tcp_tw_reuse gives up what TIME_WAIT is for. The state exists so a delayed duplicate from an old connection cannot be accepted into a new one on the same tuple. Reuse leans on TCP timestamps to make that safe, which is sound when timestamps are present and monotone — and a NAT that rewrites addresses without coherent timestamps is the case where it is not. Its sibling tcp_tw_recycle was removed from Linux entirely for that reason. You are trading a correctness margin for throughput, which is defensible if you say out loud that that is what you are doing.

Widening the port range takes ports from the listeners. 1024 65535 overlaps the registered range, so a service that later wants to bind 8080 or 9090 may find an outbound connection already using it, and the failure is intermittent and looks like nothing. Distributions leave the default at 32768 for this reason. And, as above, it makes the eventual failure sharper.

Spreading across endpoints buys headroom you do not control. The multiplier is the current size of the upstream, so your safety margin is a property of someone else’s deployment, and it shrinks exactly when the upstream is unhealthy. It is a real mechanism and a bad safety plan.

And the one that is not a trade-off at all: monitoring this is nearly free and almost never done. ss -s, nstat TcpExtTCPTimeWaitOverflow, or a gauge on ss -tan state time-wait | wc -l against the range width would have turned this incident into a ticket. The reason nobody has it is that the resource does not appear in any standard dashboard — it is not CPU, memory, disk, or network, so no exporter ships it by default.

07

In the wild

tcp_tw_recycle was removed from Linux in 4.12 (commit tcp: remove tcp_tw_recycle, 2017). The removal note and Vincent Bernat’s widely-cited Coping with the TCP TIME-WAIT state on busy Linux servers (2014, updated after the removal) are the canonical account of why the aggressive sysctl route is a trap: per-host timestamp caching cannot survive multiple hosts behind one address, which is most of the internet.

AWS documents NAT gateway limits in exactly these terms — a quota on simultaneous connections per unique destination through a single NAT gateway address, with the recommended remedies being to add addresses or to spread destinations. The ErrorPortAllocation metric exists precisely because this failure has no other symptom, and it is the shared-SNAT case in this lesson with a vendor’s numbers on it.

Envoy and Linkerd both pool upstream connections by default and expose the per-upstream limits as circuit-breaker settings. The design choice is the one this lesson argues for: hold connections and multiplex requests over them rather than open one per request, which is also why HTTP/2 to upstreams is preferred — one connection carrying many streams is the pooling fix and the head-of-line-blocking trade-off in the same decision.

PgBouncer’s existence is the second half of the trade-off. Postgres allocates a backend process per connection, so the pooling that fixes the client’s port problem creates a server-side memory problem, and the standard answer is a second pooler in the middle running in transaction-pooling mode. Two poolers in series is not an accident of history; it is what the two limits require.

Cloudflare’s engineering blog has written repeatedly on ephemeral-port pressure, including the technique of binding many source addresses and using IP_BIND_ADDRESS_NO_PORT so that the destination multiplier survives an explicit source-address choice — which is the bind() trap in this lesson, solved at scale.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A connection is identified by the 5-tuple of protocol, source address, source port, destination address and destination port, so a source port may be reused freely against a different destination and the limit is per source-address-and-destination-endpoint pair
  • TIME_WAIT holds the tuple for 2·MSL after close — 60 seconds on Linux and a compile-time constant, not a sysctl — so a port's residence time is the connection's life plus a minute
  • By Little's Law the ports in use are rate × (hold + 60), which converts a count of ports into a maximum rate of new connections of ports ÷ 60, independent of how fast the requests are
  • A port pool has no waiting room, so blocking is Erlang-B rather than a queue — and Erlang-B is exact for any holding-time distribution with that mean, not just exponential ones
  • Above the ceiling the error rate is 1 − ceiling/offered, so it grows in proportion to the overload; below the ceiling it is essentially zero, so the whole transition lives in the last 1% of load and there is no degraded region to alert on
  • Widening the port range is bounded by the 16-bit port field at about 2.3× from the Linux default, and it also shrinks the warning region — so it moves the wall further away and makes it sharper
  • Connection reuse is the only unbounded lever, because it divides the connection rate rather than multiplying the port space
  • Calling bind() to a source address before connect() forces the port to be chosen without knowing the destination, which destroys the destination multiplier entirely

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A service opens a fresh connection per request to one database endpoint. Requests take 20 ms. It is fine at 460 requests a second and returning connection errors at 480. Where does a limit at that value come from, when neither CPU, memory, database nor network is near saturation?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    28,232 ephemeral ports divided by a 60-second TIME_WAIT is 470 connections a second. The 5-tuple has four of its five fields fixed — TCP, this host, that database address, port 5432 — so the only thing that distinguishes two connections is the source port, and Linux's default ip_local_port_range is 32768–60999. Each closed socket holds its tuple for TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN = 60 s, so by Little's Law the ports in use are rate × (0.02 + 60): at 400/s that is 24,008 ports, of which 99.97% are doing nothing at all. The request being 20 ms rather than 2 seconds moves the ceiling by 0.03%, which is why making the query faster does not help and is the detail that makes this so hard to diagnose.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Same service. Explain why it went from zero errors to visible errors across a 4% traffic increase, rather than degrading as it approached the limit.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a port pool has no queue, so this is a loss system and the blocking curve is Erlang-B. Measured on 28,232 ports: 460/s → 0.000%, 470/s → 0.42%, 480/s → 2.15%, 500/s → 5.98%, 600/s → 21.6%. The first failures appear at 466.5/s, which is 0.82% below the ceiling — that is the entire warning region, and no alert threshold you would choose sits inside it. Above the ceiling the shape changes character: a loss system carries at most ports/60 connections a second whatever is offered, so the error rate becomes 1 − ceiling/offered and grows proportionally — half of connects fail at twice the ceiling, and reaching 99% failure needs about a hundred times it. So it is a knee with nothing before it, not a cliff with something after it.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A sidecar proxy makes 4,000 new connections a second, spread across 40 upstream pods, and has run happily for months on a default port range that affords 470 a second. How?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the destination is part of the tuple, so each pod is its own port space. 4,000 across 40 endpoints is 100/s each, holding 6,001 ports in total — 21% of the range, comfortable. The tuple space is 28,232 × 40 = 1,129,280, not 28,232. The danger is that this headroom is a property of the fleet, not of the proxy: a deploy or an outage that leaves 2 healthy pods puts 2,000/s on each, and blocking goes to 76.5%. So the failure is triggered by the upstream getting smaller, which is exactly when everything else is also going wrong, and the proxy's own configuration never changed. One more trap: this multiplier only exists for connect() on an unbound socket. Code that bind()s a source address first makes the kernel choose a port with no destination known, so the port must be unique per source address alone and the ×40 disappears — IP_BIND_ADDRESS_NO_PORT (Linux 4.2) exists to get it back.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You have five options: widen ip_local_port_range, set tcp_tw_reuse, add source addresses, spread across more destination endpoints, or pool connections. Rank them by what they actually buy, and say which one is different in kind.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    On 28,232 ports with 600 rps: pooling ×5,000 (ceiling 2.35 M/s), tcp_tw_reuse ×60, 40 endpoints ×40, 4 source addresses ×4, widening the range ×2.29. The reflex fix is last, and it is structurally last: it is bounded by the width of a 16-bit port field, so from any starting range it can never exceed 65535/ports. Pooling is different in kind — every other lever multiplies the port space and therefore has a ceiling, while pooling divides the connection rate and has none. 600 rps at 5,000 requests per connection is 0.12 new connections a second, holding 7 ports: 0.03% of the range. It does not give you headroom on this axis, it removes the axis.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Someone proposes widening the port range to 1024–65535 as a precaution, arguing that even if it is only 2.3× it buys margin and costs nothing. What does it cost?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It halves the warning you get. The warning region — how far below the ceiling the first failures appear — shrinks as the range widens: 1.21% at 16,384 ports, 0.82% at 28,232, 0.43% at 64,512. Measured across the model the exponent is about 0.60, and it is worth knowing why it is not the textbook 0.5: holding the failure probability fixed while the pool grows does not hold z fixed, and the effective z falls from 2.41 at 250 ports to 1.09 at 64,512, so a larger pool reaches any given blocking probability closer to its ceiling in units of √n. So the honest description of the reflex fix is: 2.29× the ceiling, 0.53× the warning. It is not free, it is not enough, and it makes the eventual incident more abrupt. Do it if you like, but do not do it instead of pooling.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A batch job in a serverless function opens a connection per row to an external HTTPS API, 300 rows a second, and starts failing with connection errors after about 90 seconds of every run. There is no database, no proxy, and no configuration anyone can change on the platform. What is the mechanism, and why 90 seconds?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The same ceiling, reached through shared SNAT. Functions egress through a managed NAT gateway, and every instance behind one external address shares that address's port space against a given destination endpoint — so the 5-tuple's source address is not yours and neither is the port range. 300/s of fresh connections holds 300 × 60 = 18,000 tuples, and the platform's per-destination allowance is finite and shared with everything else behind that address. The 90 seconds is the tell and it is the most useful part: the failure is not immediate because the pool starts empty and fills at the offered rate until the first TIME_WAIT expiries start returning ports. Occupancy rises linearly for a minute, then plateaus at rate × 60; if that plateau is above the allowance you fail shortly after the ramp, every run, at roughly the same elapsed time. A CPU or memory problem would not have a characteristic constant like that. Two fixes and they are the two from the lesson: reuse the connection (an HTTP agent with keep-alive across rows takes 300/s to nearly zero), or spread the destination if the API has several addresses. Nothing you can tune inside the function changes a port range you do not own.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a reusable identifier that must stay quarantined for a fixed period after release converts a count of identifiers into a maximum rate of use — and the quarantine, not the work, dominates the occupancy. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Hospital operating theatres and turnover time. A theatre is a reusable identifier; an operation is the work; the mandatory clean-down and changeover between cases is the quarantine. Eight theatres with a 40-minute turnover cap the throughput of short procedures at a number that has nothing to do with how short they are — a 15-minute cataract list is 73% turnover, so buying a faster surgeon changes almost nothing and the only real levers are reducing the turnover, adding theatres, or batching several cases into one setup. That last one is connection pooling exactly. Other instances: hotel rooms with a fixed housekeeping window, which is why check-out is hours before check-in and why the room count is not the guest-per-day count; airport gates with a mandatory towing and inspection gap, where the gap sets movements per hour regardless of turnaround ambition; rental cars needing valet and inspection between hires; quarantined livestock pens; and reused telephone numbers, which carriers hold for a fixed aging period so that calls for the previous subscriber do not reach the new one — the same reason as TIME_WAIT, for the same duration-based reason, in the same shape. In every case the diagnostic question is the one nobody asks first: how long is the thing unavailable after it stops being used?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
