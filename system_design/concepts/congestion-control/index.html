Tier 1 · The network Congestion control explained well nowhere else

Two senders on the same 100 Mbps path, at the same hour — one got 0.76 Mbps and the other got 92.9

A sender is not told its share of the network; it has to guess, and every algorithm guesses from a different measurement. Guess from dropped packets and you need a full buffer to learn anything, so you build the delay you then report. Guess from delivered rate and you keep 93% of a link that 1% loss reduces the other sender to under 1% of. And a short transfer never gets far enough into any of this to find out, because it spends its whole life doubling.

01

Three symptoms, one link, and nobody had changed anything

A research group shares a 100 Mbps path with an operations team. The two sites are far apart: the round trip is 100 ms. Overnight, an upstream device begins dropping about one packet in a hundred — a figure small enough that the interface graph rounds it to zero.

Three things are now reported, all on the same morning.

The nightly export is running at 0.76 Mbps. That is 0.76% of the link, and the transfer that used to finish before breakfast will not finish before lunch tomorrow.

A colleague, moving a similar file across the same path at the same hour on the same hardware, is getting 92.9 Mbps. He is asked what he did differently and does not know. His machine was reinstalled last month.

And the video calls over that link, which is the third report, have round trips of nine seconds whenever anyone is copying a large file — even though during those copies the link shows 100% utilisation and not a single dropped packet. The network team, following the obvious logic, had increased the buffer on that device the previous quarter precisely so that nothing would be dropped.

One path. One link rate. One distance. One loss rate. Three senders, and a spread of 122× in throughput and 88× in delay between them — produced entirely by what each one privately believes a congested network looks like.

02

The obvious fix is a faster link, and none of these three numbers contains the link rate

The mental model almost everyone starts with is that congestion control is a detail of TCP, the same in every stack, and that a slow transfer means a slow network. On that model the answers are: buy more link, and make the buffers deep enough that nothing is ever dropped. Both were done here. Both made it worse.

Here is what is actually going on. A sender is never told its share of the network. No packet comes back saying “you may have 40 Mbps”; there is no such packet and no such authority. So the sender has to estimate, from whatever it can observe, how many unacknowledged bytes it is entitled to keep in flight — and then keep re-estimating for the life of the connection, because the answer changes whenever anyone else starts or stops. That estimate is called the congestion window, and the algorithm that maintains it is the whole subject.

There are only ever two things a sender can observe: what came back, and how long it took. The three algorithms on this path differ in which of those they believe.

  • Reno (RFC 5681, and the shape of everything before 2006) grows the window by one segment per round trip and halves it whenever a packet is lost. A lost packet is the signal.
  • CUBIC (RFC 9438, Linux’s default since 2006) does the same thing on a different curve: it cuts the window to 70% on a loss, then follows a cubic in elapsed seconds back up to the level it reached before. Still a lost packet, still the signal.
  • BBR (Google, 2016) does not read the loss counter at all. It keeps a maximum filter over the delivery rate it has recently observed and a minimum filter over the round trips it has recently measured, and paces at the product of the two.

That last rule has an arithmetic consequence worth working out before you look at the picture. If a sender paces at its estimate of the bottleneck rate, then random loss does not reduce its rate — it only wastes the lost fraction of it. So the estimate says BBR should deliver about the link rate, less whatever its own probing costs it, less the loss.

Predict One 100 Mbps path, a 100 ms round trip, one bandwidth-delay product of buffer, and 1% of packets lost. Two senders run at the same time on identical hardware. The CUBIC one measures 0.76 Mbps. What does the BBR one get?
BBR's rate does not contain the loss rate. It paces at estimated bandwidth × minimum RTT, and 1% loss removes 1% of the bytes it sends and changes nothing else — so the prediction is 100 Mbps × 0.9498 × 0.99 = 94.0, where 0.9498 is the share of the link BBR's own gain cycle and ProbeRTT leave available. Measured: 92.9 Mbps against CUBIC's 0.76, a factor of 122 on one path at one instant. The first option is the model this lesson exists to break — 1% loss really is 1% of the packets, and it costs one sender 1% of its rate and the other 99.2% of its rate. Every figure here is printed by src/sim/cc.mjs and asserted in its test at the rig's own parameters.
Show it without answering

Four things to do with it, in this order:

  1. Leave it alone and read the top band. 0.76 Mbps and 92.9 Mbps on one path. Now drag packet loss to the far left, to none: all three converge on the link, and CUBIC is now the faster one at 100.0% against BBR’s 94.6%. Whatever BBR is buying, it is not free, and on a clean path it is not buying anything.
  2. Press 100× BDP of buffer and stop looking at the top band, which now says nothing. The middle band is the whole story: CUBIC settles at a mean round trip of 9.10 s and BBR at 103 ms on a path whose propagation delay is 100 ms. Both are at 100% utilisation. Drag the bottleneck buffer slider across its whole range and watch two bars sweep from 116 ms to nine seconds while the third does not move at all.
  3. Press a quarter BDP. Now the slate Reno bar’s throughput drops to 89.2% — this is the honest limit on “big buffers are bad”, and the reason a buffer below one bandwidth-delay product costs real bandwidth. CUBIC barely notices the same buffer, at 99.2%, and pays for that with occupancy.
  4. Drag transfer size to its far left. The bottom band is a different problem entirely. At 14.6 kB the transfer is one round trip long and gets 1.17% of the link, and no steady-state law on this page describes it, because it never reaches a steady state.

That is the finding, and it is not “BBR is better”. It is that the sender’s belief about what congestion looks like sets the operating point, and the operating point is a pair of numbers, not one. Throughput and delay are decided separately, by different mechanisms, and an algorithm can be first on one and last on the other.

03

The intuition: filling a sink you cannot see into

You have to fill a bucket from a tap, through a sink. The sink has a plughole that drains at some fixed rate you were never told, and a basin above it that holds some amount of water you were also never told. You cannot see the water level. You are blindfolded. Your job is to run the tap as fast as the plughole will take, and no faster.

Strategy one. Open the tap a little more each second until you hear water hit the floor, then halve it, then start creeping up again. This works. It is the only thing that works if the only information you have is the sound of overflow. But notice three things about it.

It cannot find the plughole’s rate; it can only find the rate at which the basin overflows, which is a different number, larger by however much the basin holds. Its steady state therefore sits at the brim — a nearly full basin, all the time, by construction. And it must overflow deliberately, over and over, forever, because overflow is its only measurement.

Now someone hands you a bigger basin. Your tap setting goes up, because it takes longer to overflow. Your throughput does not improve at all — the plughole was always the limit. What changed is that there is now a great deal of standing water above the plughole, and everything else pouring into that sink has to wait behind it. The basin is not a safety margin. It is your setpoint.

Strategy two is strategy one with better manners: after each overflow, rush the tap straight back to just under the setting that overflowed last time and then hover there, nudging up very slowly. You overflow far less often and you waste far less time at low settings. You also spend nearly all your time with the basin nearly full, which is the same trade written the other way round.

Strategy three. Put your hand under the plughole and feel the flow, and time how long a drop takes to appear. Now you know the two numbers you actually needed — the drain rate and the transit time — and you can set the tap to their product without ever spilling a drop. The cost is that you have to keep checking: every so often you must turn the tap almost off for a moment, to feel the transit time with an empty basin, because a basin with water in it makes every drop look slow.

An algorithm that learns only from failure is positioned by whatever absorbs its failures. Nothing in its rules mentions the buffer, and the buffer decides where it sits.

04

The mechanism: one search, two setpoints, and the exponent that changed everything

Phase one: slow start is a search, and it is logarithmic

A connection does not begin at its steady state. RFC 6928 sets the initial window to min(10·MSS, max(2·MSS, 14600)) bytes — 14,600 on an ordinary 1500-byte path — and slow start doubles it every round trip. So the number of round trips before the window first covers the pipe is logarithmic in the bandwidth-delay product:

rounds to cover the pipe = ceil(log2(BDP ÷ initial window))

On the path in slot 1 the pipe is 100 Mbps × 100 ms = 1.25 MB, so the count is ceil(log2(1,250,000 ÷ 14,600)) = ceil(6.42) = 7 round trips, which is 700 ms before the sender is first allowed to keep the path busy.

The logarithm is the good news and it is worth appreciating: a hundred times the pipe costs only seven more round trips. A 100 Gbps path at 100 ms is 1.25 GB in flight and slow start covers it in 17 rounds, 1.7 seconds. Exponential search is extremely well suited to a quantity you know nothing about.

Two consequences fall out of the doubling that are easy to miss, and both matter more than the logarithm.

The last step overshoots. The window that first covers the pipe covers between 1× and 2× of it, because the previous step was half of it. On this path it lands at 1.50× BDP, so 619 kB has nowhere to go but the bottleneck buffer. That is why a connection’s first loss usually happens on slow start’s last round, before congestion avoidance has run at all.

And a small transfer never gets there. After k rounds a lossless sender has delivered IW × (2^k − 1) bytes. So on this path:

A 200 kB response takes 400 ms on this 100 Mbps link and it would take 400 ms on a 10 Gbps one; the only thing that changes is the reported utilisation, from 4.00% to 0.04%. Slot 5 says what to do about that, and the answer is not a congestion-control algorithm.

Phase two: CUBIC, and what happens when you change the clock

Reno’s rule is arithmetic in round trips: +1 segment per RTT, ×0.5 on loss. That makes the window a linear ramp, and it makes throughput scale as 1/RTT — so on a long path Reno recovers so slowly after each loss that it cannot use the pipe. CUBIC’s fix, from Ha, Rhee and Xu in 2008, is a single substitution: make the window a function of elapsed seconds rather than of round trips.

W_cubic(t) = C·(t − K)³ + W_max, with K = cbrt(W_max · 0.3 ÷ C) and C = 0.4

t is seconds since the last reduction. W_max is the window the flow held when that reduction happened. K is therefore the time at which the curve arrives back at W_max — concave on the way up, inflecting there, convex beyond it. RFC 9438 §4.2.

Two counter-intuitive results fall out of that one substitution, and they are the same trade seen from two sides.

Distance almost stops mattering. Solve for the steady state under loss at probability p — set the packets delivered in one cycle, which is the area under the curve divided by the RTT, equal to the 1/p packets a cycle delivers by definition — and you get:

rate ≈ k · MSS · RTT^(−1/4) · p^(−3/4), with k = (C·(4 − 0.3) ÷ (4 × 0.3))^(1/4) = 1.0538

Compare Reno’s MSS·C/(RTT·√p), which is RTT^(−1)·p^(−1/2). Quadrupling the distance costs Reno 75% of its rate and CUBIC 29.3%.

Both exponents are measurable from a simulation that contains neither of them. src/sim/cc.mjs implements the RFC 9438 update rule round by round with no closed-form constant anywhere in it, and its measured log-log slopes are −0.75 and −0.25 for CUBIC against Reno’s exactly −1.00 and −0.50. The constant is reproduced too, and doing so surfaced something the published law does not mention:

Loss processCUBIC’s constantReno’s constantPublished
Periodic — a loss every 1/p packets exactly1.05361.22241.0538 and 1.2247

Every published throughput law assumes the first row, and every real path is the second. Mathis et al. handled that in 1997 by printing separate constants for the two loss processes; the same effect applies to CUBIC and is larger, at 1.21× against Reno’s 1.06×. Under i.i.d. loss a cycle usually ends before the window reaches W_max at all, so the steady state settles higher than the periodic idealisation predicts. This is why simulate has a lossProcess option: grading a periodic-loss law with a random-loss run reports a 21% error that is not an error in either.

And the window spends most of its life at the ceiling. This is the result that matters for slot 6, and it is scale-free — no RTT, no loss rate, no W_max, no C:

share of a cycle above φ·W_max = cbrt((1 − φ) ÷ 0.3) for CUBIC, (1 − φ) ÷ 0.5 for Reno

At φ = 0.9 that is 69.3% for CUBIC and 20.0% for Reno — a factor of 3.47. Verified against a two-million-point numerical integration of the window curve, and measured at 0.689–0.696 in the simulation once you weight by time rather than by rounds.

Now put those two together with the definition of W_max, which is where this becomes a statement about the network rather than about a curve. W_max is the window at which the bottleneck buffer overflowed. So the top decile of the window is the top decile of the queue, and CUBIC is designed to sit in it 69% of the time. Its mean window over a cycle is 92.5% of W_max, where Reno’s is 75% — and where a linear increase using CUBIC’s own reduction factor would give 85%. The cubic shape recovers half of what the reduction gave up. It recovers it in the buffer.

Phase three: BBR, and estimating the two things separately

BBR’s rule is the one from the sink: measure the drain rate and the transit time, and hold their product in flight. Concretely, from the 2016 ACM Queue paper:

  • BtlBw — a maximum filter over the delivery rate observed in the last ten round trips. A maximum, because the bottleneck rate is the best you have ever seen recently; anything lower was your own restraint or somebody else’s burst.
  • RTprop — a minimum filter over recent round trips. A minimum, because propagation delay is the smallest round trip possible and everything above it is queue.
  • Pace at gain × BtlBw, holding inflight near BtlBw × RTprop, cycling the gain through the published sequence [5/4, 3/4, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1], one round trip per phase.
  • Every 10 seconds, drop to four packets for 200 ms — ProbeRTT — because a min filter that never sees an empty queue cannot tell propagation from queueing.

Two numbers follow from the gain cycle alone, and neither contains the buffer’s depth:

mean RTT inflation = mean(max(1, gain)) = (1.25 + 1 + 6)/8 = 1.03125

One round at gain 1.25 puts a quarter of a BDP more on the wire than the link drains, so that round’s RTT is 1.25× propagation; the 0.75 round hands it back; six rounds at 1 add nothing. Measured across buffers spanning 200× and round trips spanning 60×: 1.030, and it does not move.

link share = mean(min(1, gain)) × (1 − ProbeRTT duty) = 0.96875 × (1 − 0.2/10.2) = 0.94975

The mirror image, and the half nobody quotes: a round at gain 1.25 cannot deliver 1.25 of the link, but a round at gain 0.75 does deliver only 0.75. Measured 94.5–95.2% across the same sweeps.

Read it three ways.

Down the CUBIC column, the exponent is visible. A hundred times the loss rate divides CUBIC’s throughput by 100^(3/4) = 31.6 and Reno’s by 100^(1/2) = 10.0. So CUBIC is much faster than Reno at low loss and falls away faster as loss rises — the two are the same fact, and the crossing is real: it happens at 0.055% on this path, and the crossover loss scales as RTT³, which is 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 1 ms and 1.5% at 300 ms. Below that RTT the cubic region does not engage at all, which is why it is a long-path feature and invisible inside a datacentre.

Across the last two rows, the reason bandwidth cannot fix loss. At 1% loss the loss-based senders are at 0.76 and 1.5 Mbps. There is no link speed at which those numbers improve, because the link rate is not in k·MSS·RTT^(−1/4)·p^(−3/4). Ten times the bandwidth on this path buys the CUBIC sender nothing at all; halving the loss rate buys it 2^0.75 = 1.68×, and halving the round trip buys it 2^0.25 = 1.19×. If a monitoring dashboard shows 1% loss, the procurement conversation is the wrong conversation.

And along the top row, the honest correction. On a clean path BBR is the slowest of the three. Whatever it is buying is not throughput.

The first half of that is a true property of the cubic function and it is exactly why RFC 9438 §4.3 requires a TCP-friendly region: whenever a Reno-equivalent window would be larger, CUBIC must use that instead. src/sim/cc.mjs deliberately does not implement §4.3, because the cubic region is what is under test and cubicRenoCrossoverLoss states where it stops applying — so read that 0.12× as “the cubic region alone would be eight times worse here, which is what the fallback exists to prevent”, not as a claim about Linux. It is the clearest possible demonstration that CUBIC’s design is about long fat paths and nothing else.

Where each of these binds

  • Inside a datacentre, 0.1–1 ms, loss near zero. None of this matters much; every algorithm fills the link, and the interesting question is queueing at the servers rather than on the wire. The cubic region never engages.
  • Across a region or an ocean, clean path. All three fill the link, and the only difference is delay — which is slot 6, and which is decided by the buffer rather than by the sender.
  • Any path with loss above about 0.1%. The choice of algorithm is worth 20× to 122×. This is the case for cellular, satellite, Wi-Fi with interference, and any path with a device quietly dropping.
  • Below about 1.85 MB per transfer on a 100 Mbps, 100 ms path. None of the above binds. Slow start decides, the answer is a count of round trips, and the fixes are the ones from the earlier lessons: fewer round trips, a shorter round trip, or a copy of the data closer to the reader.

The trade-off: BBR's queue-free operating point costs 5% of the link and all of the fairness

Everything above says loss is the wrong signal. So stop using it. Here is the bill, and it has three lines.

Line one: the drain phase and ProbeRTT are not free. BBR’s advertised 100% of the bottleneck is actually 94.98% of it, and the missing part decomposes exactly:

Line two: what it buys, which is the number the whole lesson turns on. Same 100 Mbps path, same 100 ms of propagation, no random loss at all, sweeping only the bottleneck buffer:

From one BDP to a hundred, CUBIC’s throughput moves by 0.00% and its round trip moves by a factor of 50, to 9.10 seconds. BBR’s does not move at all, at any depth, because the buffer’s size is not a term in BtlBw × RTprop. At 100 × BDP the two are 88× apart in delay while both report 100% utilisation — and that is the trade in one line: 5.0% of the link for 88× the latency.

Two things worth being precise about, because the usual telling of bufferbloat overshoots in both directions. First, a buffer below one BDP genuinely does cost throughput: Reno measures 89.2% at a quarter BDP, because after halving, its window drops under the pipe and the link idles for 60% of the cycle. The honest rule is “buffers stop helping at one bandwidth-delay product”, not “buffers never help”. Second, CUBIC handles that same shallow buffer far better — 99.2%, starving for only 12.6% of the cycle instead of 60% — and it pays for the difference in exactly the currency slot 4 named: at a quarter BDP its mean round trip is 116 ms against Reno’s 105 ms. CUBIC’s whole design is this trade, made deliberately.

And the harm is not in the mean. It is in the tail, and it is paid by other flows: nine seconds of standing queue is nine seconds added to every DNS lookup, keystroke and video frame sharing that bottleneck, which lands on their p99 rather than on the bulk transfer’s throughput graph. The sender causing it sees a perfect dashboard.

Line three: BBR is unfair, and its estimate is inside its own loop. Two flows sharing a bottleneck hold whatever inflight their own rules say, so the split follows the ratio of their targets. CUBIC’s target grows with the buffer; BBRv1’s is capped at about two BDPs and does not.

Bottleneck bufferBBR’s shareCUBIC’s share

Which is the shape most people have backwards: BBR is the aggressive one in a shallow buffer and the loser in a deep one. There is exactly one buffer depth at which this pairing is fair — 1.16 BDP — and an operator picks it, not either algorithm. This is a deliberately simple model of a genuinely messy interaction, and it does not capture BBRv1 driving loss into the tens of percent in a shallow buffer, RTT-based unfairness between the two, or ProbeRTT synchronisation between BBR flows; the research below measured all three.

The last part is subtler and it is the reason ProbeRTT exists. BBR’s inflight target is cwnd_gain × BtlBw × min RTT. If something else is holding a standing queue up, the minimum round trip BBR can observe is propagation plus that queue — so it targets more inflight than the pipe holds by exactly the queue it just measured, which makes more queue, which raises the next minimum. With a cwnd gain of two the queue doubles each filter window, so a 100-BDP buffer fills in 7 windows and an 8-BDP one in 4. The estimate is inside its own feedback loop, and draining to four packets is what breaks it — which is why the 1.9% in the budget above is hatched.

So the three ways out of this lesson, and what each costs:

  • Keep the loss-based sender and fix the buffer. Active queue management — CoDel, fq_codel, RED — drops or marks before the queue is full, which restores a signal that arrives at one BDP instead of at the buffer’s depth. What you gave up is a small amount of throughput and a metric your operations team is measured on: “I have deliberately introduced loss to reduce latency” is a defensible sentence that reads, on a dashboard, as a fault.
  • Change the signal. BBR, or delay-based control generally. What you gave up is the 5.0% above, interoperability with everything else on the path, and a fairness argument that is still running.
  • Do not have a steady state. Most requests are too short to reach one, so the leverage is in round trips and not in the algorithm: fewer of them, shorter ones, or a copy of the data nearer the reader. Slot 4’s table is the whole argument, and it is why a CDN beats a congestion-control choice for anything under a megabyte.
07

In the wild

Google measured the 122× on production paths, and their number for it was 133×. Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Hassas Yeganeh and Jacobson’s BBR: Congestion-Based Congestion Control (ACM Queue 14(5), September–October 2016) reports raising a receive buffer on one US–Europe path: “Manually raising the receive buffer on one US-Europe path caused BBR immediately to reach 2 Gbps, while CUBIC remained at 15 Mbps — the 133× relative improvement predicted by Mathis et al.” Same mechanism, same order of magnitude, on a real backbone.

The same paper is also where the model here disagrees with a measurement, which is worth stating plainly. On a 100 Mbps, 100 ms link — the path in this lesson — the paper reports that “CUBIC’s throughput decreases by 10 times at 0.1 percent loss and totally stalls above 1 percent.” The model here has CUBIC at 4.6 Mbps at 0.1% loss, a factor of 22 down, so it is about twice as pessimistic as Google’s measurement of a real stack. Two things account for the gap and both are named above: simulate omits RFC 9438 §4.3’s TCP-friendly floor, and a real Linux stack has HyStart, SACK-based recovery and ACK clocking that this round-based model does not. The direction, the exponent and the order of magnitude reproduce; the constant does not, and a model that claimed it did would be lying.

And the deployment result, at a scale that settles the argument about delay. The same authors report BBR “reduces median RTT by 53 percent on average globally and by more than 80 percent in the developing world”, measured across “more than 200 million YouTube playback connections … on five continents over a week”, with throughput barely changed. Read that against the buffer table in slot 6: half the round-trip time and no more bandwidth is exactly what you get when you move the operating point from pipe + buffer to pipe. It is the largest congestion-control experiment ever run and its headline result is a latency number.

CUBIC’s own paper is honest about which trade it made. Ha, Rhee and Xu’s CUBIC: A New TCP-Friendly High-Speed TCP Variant (ACM SIGOPS Operating Systems Review 42(5), July 2008) sets out to solve exactly the 1/RTT problem in slot 5’s distance sweep, and the mechanism it chose — a window that is a function of real time and that plateaus near its previous maximum — is the mechanism that produces slot 6’s nine seconds. It became Linux’s default in 2006 and is therefore, by volume, the control loop that most of the internet’s bytes have passed through. RFC 9438 (Xu, Ha, Rhee, Goel and Eggert, August 2023) is the standards-track version, and §4.3’s TCP-friendly region is where the committee wrote down the correction that the 5 ms row in slot 5 demonstrates the need for.

The fairness argument was settled by measurement, not by argument. Hock, Bless and Zitterbart’s Experimental Evaluation of BBR Congestion Control (IEEE ICNP 2017) put BBRv1 against CUBIC across buffer depths and found precisely the shape in slot 6’s share table, including BBR driving high loss rather than yielding in a shallow buffer. Ware, Mukerjee, Seshan and Sherry’s Modeling BBR’s Interactions with Loss-Based Congestion Control (ACM IMC 2019) went further and explained it: BBRv1’s throughput in a shallow buffer is largely independent of how many loss-based flows it competes with, because its inflight cap is a constant and theirs is not. Both papers are the reason BBRv2 and BBRv3 exist and both added a loss and ECN response to the original design — which is to say the “ignore loss entirely” position did not survive contact with the internet.

Bufferbloat was named before either of these and is the reason the numbers are so large. Gettys and Nichols’ Bufferbloat: Dark Buffers in the Internet (Communications of the ACM 55(1), January 2012) is the paper that pointed out that memory got cheap faster than anyone updated their buffer sizing, and the BBR paper puts the same history in one sentence: “Subsequent memory price decreases resulted in buffers orders of magnitude larger than ISP link BDPs, and the resulting bufferbloat yielded RTTs of seconds instead of milliseconds.” Seconds instead of milliseconds is the 9.10 against 0.103 in slot 6, measured rather than recalled.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A loss-based sender's only congestion signal is a full buffer, so its operating point is defined by the buffer's depth rather than by anything in its own rules — W_max is pipe plus buffer, which makes bufferbloat structural and not a tuning mistake
  • CUBIC's window is a function of elapsed seconds since the last reduction, not of round trips, so its rate law is RTT^(−1/4)·p^(−3/4) where Reno's is RTT^(−1)·p^(−1/2)
  • Because the cubic curve is flat near its ceiling, CUBIC spends 69.3% of every cycle in the top decile of its window against Reno's 20% — and that window's ceiling is the level at which the buffer last overflowed
  • BBR estimates bottleneck bandwidth and propagation delay with separate filters and paces at their product, so a dropped packet is not a signal at all — which is why it holds throughput on a lossy path and why it queues nothing when the buffer is deep
  • What BBR pays for a queue-free operating point is 5.0% of the link: 3.125% to the gain cycle's drain phase and 1.9% to ProbeRTT, so on a clean shallow-buffered path it is the slower sender
  • BBR and a loss-based flow hold a fixed ratio of bytes in flight, so which one wins is a property of the buffer and not of either algorithm — BBR takes 63% of a quarter-BDP bottleneck and 9% of a 20-BDP one
  • Slow start doubles, so the round trips needed to cover a pipe are logarithmic in the bandwidth-delay product — but a transfer smaller than IW·(2^k − 1) never once sends at line rate, and its duration does not contain the link rate at all

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Two senders, one 100 Mbps path at a 100 ms round trip, 1% of packets lost, running at the same time on identical hardware. One gets 0.76 Mbps. The other gets 92.9 Mbps. Neither is misconfigured and nothing is broken. What differs, and why is the gap a factor of 122 rather than a few percent?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    What differs is what each one treats as evidence of congestion. The slow one is loss-based: it reduces its window on every drop and rebuilds it slowly, so its rate is pinned by the loss rate — k·MSS·RTT^(−1/4)·p^(−3/4), which at 1% loss and 100 ms is under a megabit whatever the link is. The fast one is BBR: it max-filters the delivery rate it observes, min-filters the round trip, paces at their product, and never reads the loss counter, so 1% loss costs it 1% of its bytes and nothing else. It measures 92.9 Mbps against CUBIC's 0.76. The gap is a factor of 122 because the two quantities are not the same kind of thing: one is a fraction of the link and the other is a function of p with the link absent from it. Note what is not the difference: not the link, not the distance, not the loss rate — all three are shared.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    An operator replaces a bottleneck's buffer with one a hundred times deeper, so that nothing is ever dropped while a window grows. Link utilisation stays at 100.0%. What moves, by how much, and why can the algorithm not stop it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The round trip, from 183 ms to 9.10 seconds — measured on a 100 Mbps path whose propagation delay is 100 ms, a factor of 88 against BBR's 103 ms on the identical path. It cannot be stopped because a loss-based sender's only congestion signal is a drop, so it necessarily grows the window until something drops a packet, and the window at which that happens is pipe + buffer by definition. So the buffer is not a safety margin whose size is a tuning choice — it is the algorithm's setpoint, and CUBIC's mean window sits at 92.5% of it. Every one of those seconds is paid by whatever else shares the path, and it is paid in the tail: a video call's p99 is the queue's occupancy divided by the drain rate.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    CUBIC's window is a cubic function of elapsed seconds since the last reduction rather than a linear function of round trips. Name the two consequences that fall out of that single substitution, and give the exponents.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    First, throughput stops depending much on distance. Reno's law is RTT^(−1)·p^(−1/2); CUBIC's is RTT^(−1/4)·p^(−3/4), measured from the simulation at −0.25 and −0.75 against Reno's exactly −1.00 and −0.50. Quadrupling the distance costs Reno 75% of its rate and CUBIC 29%. Second, the window spends most of its life at the ceiling. The cubic is flat near W_max, so time above 0.9·W_max is ((1 − 0.9)/0.3)^(1/3) = 69.3% of every cycle against Reno's linear 20% — a scale-free result with no RTT, no loss rate and no W_max in it. Those two are the same trade seen twice: CUBIC bought distance-independence with occupancy, and since W_max is the level at which the buffer overflowed, the top decile of the window is the top decile of the queue.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You are asked to pick a congestion control for a service and told BBR is strictly better because it holds throughput under loss. Where is that wrong, and what is the number?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It is wrong in three places. On a clean path BBR is the slower sender: it delivers 94.6% of the link where CUBIC delivers 100.0%, because the gain cycle's 0.75 drain phase costs 3.125% and ProbeRTT costs another 1.9% — 5.0% spent buying a queue-free operating point, which is worth nothing when there is no queue. It is unfair, in a direction most people have backwards: BBRv1's inflight target is about two BDPs whatever the buffer does, so it is the aggressive one in a shallow buffer — 63% of a quarter-BDP bottleneck — and the loser in a deep one, 9% at 20 BDP. There is exactly one buffer depth at which this pairing is fair, 1.16 BDP, and the operator picks it rather than either algorithm. And its min-RTT estimate sits inside its own feedback loop: if something else holds a standing queue up, BBR measures propagation-plus-queue, targets more inflight, creates more queue, and with a cwnd gain of two fills a 100-BDP buffer in 7 filter windows. ProbeRTT is the fix and that is why it is a correctness requirement rather than an optimisation.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    A 200 kB API response crosses a 100 Mbps path at a 100 ms round trip. The capacity plan divides and gets 16 ms. Estimate the real number, and say which of the algorithms above decided it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    400 ms, 25× the plan — and none of them decided it. Slow start doubles from RFC 6928's 14,600-byte initial window, so k round trips carry 14,600·(2^k − 1) bytes: 14.6 kB, 43.8, 102.2, 219.0. 200 kB needs 4 round trips, and 4 × 100 ms is 400 ms. The achieved rate is 4.00 Mbps, which is 4% of the link — and the same object on a 10 Gbps link would take the same 400 ms and read 0.04%, because the link rate is not in the expression. On this path the window does not cover the pipe until round 7, so anything under 1.85 MB never sends at line rate even once. Which is the practical correction to the whole lesson: CUBIC and BBR differ enormously in steady state, and a great deal of real traffic never reaches one.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team runs a batch job that writes to an object store through a fixed connection pool. They double the pool size and throughput does not improve, but the p99 write latency goes from 40 ms to 900 ms, and the store's own dashboards show it serving at exactly the same rate as before. Their concurrency controller only backs off when a request fails. What is the mechanism, and what would fix it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The same control loop with different units. The controller's only congestion signal is a failure, so it raises concurrency until something is rejected — and the queue in front of the store is what absorbs everything between "saturated" and "rejecting". So its operating point is not a property of the controller; it is service capacity + queue depth, exactly as a loss-based sender's window is pipe + buffer. Throughput did not move because the store was already saturated; latency moved because a queue's occupancy divided by its drain rate is a duration, and doubling the pool doubled the occupancy. The fix is the BBR move, not a smaller pool: measure the two quantities separately — the completion rate you are actually getting and the minimum latency you have recently seen — and hold in-flight requests at their product. That is Little's Law used as a setpoint rather than as an explanation, and it is what an AIMD-on-error controller structurally cannot do. The bill is the same one BBR pays: a few percent of throughput to keep probing what the minimum latency is, and no interoperability with the other clients still pushing until they fail.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a controller whose only feedback is the signal that it has already gone too far must overshoot in order to learn anything, so its operating point is set by whatever absorbs the overshoot rather than by the controller's own rules — and the cure is to measure the two underlying quantities separately instead of waiting for the composite failure. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A thermostat with no thermometer, only a window that opens when the room is too hot. It cannot find the right temperature; it can only find the temperature at which the window opens, and how hot the room gets depends on how much thermal mass the walls have — the mass is the buffer, and a heavier building is a hotter one, not a steadier one. Other instances, and the giveaway is the same in each: a driver who brakes only on the rumble strip ends up positioned by the width of the shoulder rather than by the lane, which is why a wider shoulder makes the drifting worse and not safer; a hospital that adds beds because the corridor is full gets a longer wait rather than a shorter one, since the corridor's occupancy was the only admission signal in the loop; a company that hires only after a deadline slips is staffed by the size of its slack rather than by its workload; the natural rate of unemployment as a monetary target, where policy tightens only once inflation prints and the delay in the signal is what sets the overshoot. In every case the reform is BBR's: stop waiting for the composite failure and instrument the two things that compose it — here, put a thermometer in the room.
    Did you have that?
