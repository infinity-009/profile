Tier 4 · Distributed systems mechanics Fencing tokens explained well nowhere else

The lock worked perfectly and two clients wrote anyway, ten times a minute

A distributed lock cannot stop a stale writer, because the thing enforcing the lock and the thing receiving the write are different systems and only one of them knows a lock exists. The corruption rate is one line of arithmetic — mean pause over lease TTL — and at ordinary settings it is one bad write every ten seconds. The fix is not a better lock. It is moving the check to where the write lands.

01

Nothing failed, nothing logged an error, and the data was wrong

A job takes a distributed lock before writing to a shared record. The lock is real — ZooKeeper, etcd, a Redis lease, it does not matter which — and it is correct: it never hands the same lock to two holders at once. The lease is 30 seconds, which is generous for work that takes two. The service runs at 100 lock acquisitions a second.

Then a holder stopped. Not crashed — stopped. A garbage collection pause, and the process was absent for 1.4 seconds while doing nothing it could observe. The lease expired. The lock service did the right thing and granted the lock to somebody else, who did their work and wrote. The first holder resumed, still holding a variable that said it owned the lock, and wrote too.

Here is the part worth sitting with: you can compute how often this happens from a single line of arithmetic, and it needs nothing you do not already have.

P(a write lands after its lease expired) ≈ mean pause ÷ lease TTL

The GC log already tells you the mean pause. Nothing about the tail is required — see slot 4 for why. At a 30 ms mean pause and a 30 s lease that is 1 in 1,000.

One acquisition in a thousand, at a hundred a second, is one corrupted write every ten seconds. For as long as the service has been running.

And every component reported success. The lock service granted and released correctly. The database accepted a well-formed write from an authenticated client. No exception was raised, no metric moved, no alert fired. The only evidence is a value that is older than the value it replaced, which is not a thing most systems check.

02

Check that you still hold the lock before writing. Watch it not be enough.

The belief worth naming, because it is where nearly everyone goes next and because it is almost right: that the holder should verify it still owns the lock immediately before writing. Read the clock, confirm there is time left, then write. It sounds airtight, it is a genuine improvement, and it does not close the hole — because the pause can land between the check and the write, and no ordering of two operations removes the gap between them.

A paused process cannot detect that it paused. That is the whole difficulty. Any check the client performs may have happened an arbitrarily long time ago, and the client has no way to know how long ago, because the clock it would ask was not being read during the interval it needs to ask about.

Predict A 30 s lease. The JVM's GC log shows a 30 ms mean pause. 100 lock acquisitions a second, and a lock service that is provably correct — it never grants the same lock twice. How often does a write reach the resource from a holder whose lease had already expired?
mean pause ÷ lease = 30 ms / 30 s = 1 in 1,000 acquisitions, and at 100 a second that is one every ten seconds. The first option is the belief this slot exists to break: the lock is correct, and its correctness is exactly why nobody looks for the bug. The lock service enforces mutual exclusion on the lock; the write goes to a resource that has never heard of it.
Show it without answering

Four things to take from it.

Checking the clock is a real fix and a partial one. 10⁻³ to 1.25×10⁻⁶ is worth having. It is also still one bad write every couple of hours at this rate, and “we made it 800 times rarer” is a different claim from “we fixed it”.

The safety margin is a knob with a bill attached. Every acquisition that finds less than the margin left does its work and then throws it away — exactly s/L of them, 16.7% at a five-second margin on a thirty-second lease. Raising the margin to make the fix safer raises the waste in direct proportion.

Switching on the fence changes nothing you can see. This is the most important thing in the rig and the reason it is drawn this way rather than as a curve. The pauses still happen at the same rate. The leases still expire underneath them. The stale writes are still sent. A reader who comes away thinking fencing prevents the race has learned the wrong lesson — it prevents the race from landing.

And then the lease is free. With the fence on, drag the lease down to a second. Corruption stays at zero and recovery from a real crash improves from fifteen seconds to five hundred milliseconds. That combination — better on both axes at once — has not been available anywhere else in this tier.

03

The intuition: a reserved sign, and a numbered ticket

Two ways to keep people from taking your table.

A sign that says RESERVED. It works because people cooperate. It has no authority over anyone who sits down anyway, and — this is the part that matters — it does not stop being a sign when your reservation time passes. The sign cannot tell whether you are still entitled to it. Nobody at the table is checking anything.

A numbered ticket, at a counter with a display. You take ticket 41. The display shows the number being served. When you present ticket 41 and the display says 47, you are turned away — not because you were dishonest, and not because anyone measured how long you had been waiting, but because the counter knows it has already served someone later than you. One comparison of two integers.

A distributed lock, on its own, is the sign. The lock service is scrupulous about who holds it, and the database receiving your write has never heard of it. So the exclusion depends entirely on every client being cooperative and on time — and a paused process breaks the second half without breaking the first.

A fencing token is the ticket, and the essential move is not the number. It is that the counter is the one doing the checking. Put the check anywhere else and you are back to a sign: a rule enforced at a place that does not know the rule is not enforced.

You cannot enforce a rule at a place that does not check it. The lock service knows about locks; the database knows about writes. Every “distributed lock” bug is that sentence, and the fix is always to move the check to where the write lands.

04

The mechanism: three regimes, and why only one of them is a zero

Regime one: the lease alone. The holder pauses at some point in its hold, and the pause has to outlast whatever lease was left. The remaining lease is uniform on the interval, so:

P(corrupt) = (1/L) ∫₀L S(x) dx  ≈  E[pause] / L

The approximation is the useful part, and it holds because ∫₀∞ S(x) dx = E[X] for any positive random variable — so once the lease dwarfs the pause scale the integral has already saturated at the mean. The tail does not enter it. Asserted against the exact integral for all three tails in fencing.test.mjs: at a 30 s lease they agree to within 0.3%.

That has a cheerful consequence. This rate is estimable from a GC log’s mean pause, with no knowledge of the tail, in one division. It is the rare number in this tier that a team can compute in ten seconds — and almost nobody does.

Regime two: check the clock first. The holder re-reads the clock immediately before committing and proceeds only if at least s remains. The dangerous region is truncated rather than replaced:

P(corrupt) = (1/L) ∫sL S(x) dx  ·  work discarded = s / L

Not S(s), which is the tempting form and is wrong: the pause must beat whatever actually remained, which is at least s and usually much more. The wrong form overstates the risk sevenfold at these settings, and the test asserts the difference — because a formula that flatters or slanders a fix by 7× is a formula nobody should trust either way.

And now the sting. That integral runs from the margin outward, so it samples nothing but the tail — and the tail is exactly what you cannot measure. Same lease, same mean pause, same margin:

Pause tail (30 ms mean)P(corrupt), 30 s lease, 5 s marginOne bad write every
Exponential~0never, in practice
Power law k=21.25 × 10⁻⁶2.2 hours
Lognormal σ=22.56 × 10⁻⁵6.5 minutes

Four orders of magnitude between the first and the last, decided entirely by a part of the distribution no dashboard shows. The fix that makes the number small also makes it unknowable — which is the failure-detection lesson arriving from the opposite direction: there the tail set the price of accuracy, here it sets the residual risk of the workaround.

Regime three: the fence. Every grant carries a monotonically increasing token. Every write carries its token. The resource keeps the highest token it has accepted and refuses anything lower.

P(corrupt) = 0  ·  for every lease, every tail, every rate

Swept rather than spot-checked in the test — three tails × five leases × 200,000 acquisitions each, asserting both that zero stale writes were applied and that stale writes were attempted, because a zero with nothing to reject proves nothing.

This is a different kind of statement from the other two, and the reason is worth naming precisely: it stops asking a question about time. “Has enough time passed?” is unanswerable by a process that may have been absent for an unknown interval. “Has someone newer already been here?” is an integer comparison against durable state. The first is a bet. The second is a lookup.

What makes a token valid. Two properties, both easy to assume and each fatal to omit:

  • Monotonic across failover. If the generator can ever reissue a number it already gave out, a stale holder can hold a token the resource will accept. This is a consensus property, not an atomicity one.
  • Compared and stored atomically with the write. A read-then-write check reintroduces this exact race one level down, which is a remarkably bad place to reintroduce it.

Read the first column against the third. Every lease TTL is wrong. Shorten it to recover quickly from a crash and the corruption rate rises in exact proportion; lengthen it to make corruption rare and everything queues behind a dead holder for minutes. There is no value that is good at both, because one cost is L/2 and the other is 1/L, and no setting minimises both terms of that pair.

This is the same trapped-knob shape as the failure detector’s timeout, and it has the same escape denied — with one difference that changes everything. A failure detector’s two errors are both irreducible. Here, one of them is not an error at all once the resource checks a token, so the fence deletes a column from this table and the trap disappears with it. Set the lease by how long you are willing to wait after a crash, and stop thinking about the other axis. That is the only place in this tier where a knob becomes free.

Where a token comes from. The distinction that decides whether a design is safe or merely looks safe:

The last two are the traps, and they fail differently. Redis INCR is atomic on one node, but Redis replication is asynchronous — a failover can promote a replica missing recent increments, and the counter goes backwards, reissuing a token the resource has already accepted. A wall-clock timestamp is not monotonic across machines at all; clock skew and NTP steps mean two holders can produce tokens that order wrongly. A counter is not a timestamp.

Both are worse than having no fence, and the reason is not the failure rate. It is that a design with no fence has a known risk somebody might eventually compute, while a design with a broken fence has a safety argument everybody trusts.

What to do.

  • Compute mean pause / lease for your existing locks right now. It is one division and the numerator is in your GC log. If the answer times your acquisition rate is more than “never”, you have been corrupting data.
  • Put the check at the resource. If it is a database, that is a conditional update on a generation column, and you almost certainly already have the machinery. This is the whole fix.
  • Take the token from something with consensus behind it — ZooKeeper, etcd, a database sequence. Not a cache, and not a clock.
  • Then shorten the lease. Fencing makes the safety axis vanish, so the only remaining question is recovery time, and that argues for a shorter TTL than you would otherwise dare.
  • Audit every write path. One batch job or admin script that writes without a token voids the guarantee for the whole resource.
  • If the resource genuinely cannot be fenced, say so out loud and make the operation idempotent instead, so a duplicate is harmless rather than merely rare. That is a real answer. “We used a lock” is not.
06

The trade-off: what a fence costs, and the one it cannot pay

Fencing is unusually close to free on the axes this tier normally trades, which makes it important to be precise about what it actually costs.

You must be able to modify the resource. This is the real bill and it is sometimes unpayable. The check has to live at the point of effect, so the resource needs a conditional write. Databases have had this forever under the name optimistic concurrency control. Object storage did not — S3 was the canonical unfenceable resource until conditional writes arrived in 2024 — and plenty of legacy endpoints, filesystems and third-party APIs still do not. When the resource cannot check, no amount of correctness in the lock service recovers the guarantee, and the honest fallback is idempotency, not a longer lease.

The token has to be threaded through everything. Every write path, including the ones nobody remembers: the migration script, the nightly job, the support tool, the endpoint from before the lock existed. A single unfenced path does not degrade the guarantee, it voids it, because the high-water mark it never advanced no longer describes the resource’s state.

Fencing gives safety, not liveness. The fenced-out holder’s work is simply gone — the write was refused and nothing retried it. Something has to notice the rejection and redo it, which means the operation must be safe to repeat. The fence converts silent corruption into a visible error, which is an enormous improvement and is not the same as converting it into success.

And what no fence can pay for: it does not stop the race, only the damage. The band in which two clients both believe they hold the lock still opens, at exactly the same rate, and inside it both of them still act.

Read that against the equivalent diagram in the failure-detection lesson: same participants, same pause, same band, and the final arrow points the other way. The whole of this lesson is one row.

07

In the wild

Kleppmann, “How to do distributed locking” (February 2016) is the source of this lesson’s framing and of the diagram that made it widely understood. It was written as a response to Redlock, and its central claim is the one this page is built on: a lock protects nothing unless the resource checks a fencing token, so any lock protocol that cannot produce one cannot be made safe by improving the protocol. antirez’s reply, “Is Redlock safe?”, is worth reading beside it — the disagreement is largely about which assumptions are reasonable, and the exchange is the clearest illustration available of how much a safety argument depends on its stated model. Redlock’s own documentation acknowledges it does not provide fencing tokens.

ZooKeeper is the canonical answer, and the reason is structural rather than feature-by-feature: every znode carries a version, every setData can be conditioned on it, and the zxid is monotonic across leader changes because that is what the consensus protocol is for. “Use ZooKeeper for locks” and “use ZooKeeper because it can give you a token you can fence with” are the same recommendation, and only the second one explains itself.

Kafka’s leader epoch is fencing under a different name, added specifically because a stale leader’s log could be accepted and cause divergence. Kubernetes fences with resourceVersion on every update, so a controller writing from a stale cache gets a conflict rather than clobbering. Google’s Chubby paper (Burrows, OSDI 2006) discusses both sequencers — its name for fencing tokens — and the alternative of having the lock server invalidate the resource directly, along with why sequencers won.

The hardware ancestry is where the word comes from. In storage HA, fencing means physically cutting a suspect node off from the shared disk — SCSI persistent reservations, or STONITH (“shoot the other node in the head”), which removes power from a machine that might merely be slow. That is the same problem and the same admission: you cannot ask a possibly-paused node anything, so the only reliable move is to make its future actions ineffective. A fencing token is STONITH performed by the resource rather than by a power controller, at software speed and without the collateral damage.

DDIA ch. 9, “The Truth Is Defined by the Majority” and “Fencing off zombies” (p. 374), is the reference treatment. Its process-pause discussion — a JVM stopped for minutes, a suspended VM, a laptop closed mid-transaction — is what makes the case that this is an ordinary occurrence rather than an exotic one.

Model and numbers — every figure computed by src/sim/fencing.mjs and asserted in src/sim/fencing.test.mjs (17 tests): the survival integral against hand-computed closed forms and a 400,000-sample Monte Carlo for the lognormal, each regime’s rate against a simulated race of the actual event sequence, the mean-pause-over-lease identity across all three tails, and the zero-corruption claim swept over three tails × five leases × 200,000 acquisitions with the requirement that stale writes were attempted in every cell.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A lock service enforces mutual exclusion on the lock, not on the resource, so a lock the resource does not know about is advisory and correct only if every client is punctual
  • A paused process cannot detect that it paused, so no client-side check can be trusted to have happened recently
  • The naive corruption rate is approximately mean pause divided by lease TTL, because the integral of a survival function is the mean — so the tail does not enter it
  • Checking the clock before writing reduces the rate by orders of magnitude but not to zero, and hands the rate over to the tail, where it becomes unknowable
  • Checking the clock costs s/L of acquisitions, whose work is done and then discarded
  • Without a fence the lease TTL is trapped: short leases corrupt, long leases block everyone behind a crashed holder
  • A fencing token makes corruption exactly zero at every lease, tail and rate, because the resource rejects any token below the highest it has seen
  • Fencing does not reduce the rate at which stale writes are attempted, only whether they are applied
  • A token source must be monotonic across failover and checked atomically with the write, which is why Redis INCR and wall-clock timestamps are unsafe token sources

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Your lock service is provably correct and never grants the same lock to two holders at once. Explain how two clients still write to the resource.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the lock service's guarantee is about the lock, and the write goes to the resource, which has never heard of the lock. Client A acquires a lease, stops for longer than the lease — a GC pause, a suspended VM, an fsync behind a full queue — and the service correctly grants to B when the lease expires. B writes. A resumes with no way to know any time passed and writes too. Every component behaved to specification. The mutual exclusion was advisory, and an advisory lock is correct only if every client is honest and punctual. A pause makes an honest client unpunctual, undetectably.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Estimate the applied-stale-write rate for a 30 s lease on a JVM whose GC log shows a 30 ms mean pause, at 100 acquisitions a second. What makes this estimate possible without knowing anything about the tail?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    mean pause ÷ lease = 30 ms / 30 s = 10⁻³, so one acquisition in a thousand, so one bad write every ten seconds. It needs no tail information because the corruption condition is "the pause outlasts the lease remaining", the remaining lease is uniform on the interval, and ∫₀^∞ S(x) dx = E[X] for any positive random variable — so once the lease dwarfs the pause scale the integral has saturated at the mean. All three tails in the rig agree to within 0.3% at this lease.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A holder re-reads the clock and refuses to write with less than 5 s of a 30 s lease left. Give both the benefit and the two costs.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Benefit: the rate falls from 10⁻³ to 1.25×10⁻⁶, a factor of 800, because the integral now runs from the margin outward instead of from zero. First cost: it is still not zero — the pause can land between the check and the write, and no ordering of those two operations removes the window. Second cost: s/L of acquisitions are thrown away, 16.7% here, each having done its work before declining to commit it. And a third, worse than either: the rate is now set entirely by the tail, so the same lease and the same mean pause give 1.16×10⁻⁴ on an exponential tail and 7.6×10⁻³ on a lognormal one — a fix whose residual risk you cannot measure.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Why is a fencing token's guarantee zero rather than merely small, when everything else in this tier is a probability?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because it stops reasoning about time. The resource keeps the highest token it has ever accepted and refuses anything lower, so a stale write is rejected by comparison of two integers — a fact that does not depend on how long the pause was, what the delay tail looks like, or whether any clock was right. Every other mechanism here asks "has enough time passed?", which is unanswerable. This one asks "has someone newer already been here?", which is a lookup.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Someone proposes using Redis INCR to generate fencing tokens. What breaks, and why is it worse than having no fence?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Redis replication is asynchronous, so a failover can promote a replica missing recent increments and the counter goes backwards — reissuing a token the resource has already accepted. The fence then admits precisely the write it exists to reject. Worse than no fence because it is silent: a design with no fence has a known risk that someone might compute, while a design with a broken one has a safety argument everybody trusts. A token source must be monotonic across failover, which is a consensus property, not an atomicity property — and INCR only provides the second.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    What does fencing NOT give you, and what has to be built alongside it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It gives safety, not liveness. The fenced-out holder's work is simply lost — the write was refused and nothing retried it — so something must detect the rejection and redo the work, which means the operation has to be safe to repeat. It also requires that every write path carries the token: one admin script, one batch job, one legacy endpoint that writes without a token and the guarantee is gone for the whole resource, because the high-water mark it never advanced no longer reflects reality.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team runs a nightly batch job guarded by a lock in DynamoDB with a 10-minute TTL. The job takes about 4 minutes. Twice this quarter the report came out with doubled figures. They propose raising the TTL to 30 minutes so the lock cannot expire mid-job. Will that work?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It reduces the rate and does not remove the cause, and it makes the other failure much worse. Rate: the corruption probability is roughly the pause-over-lease ratio, so tripling the TTL cuts it about threefold — from twice a quarter to perhaps twice a year, which will read as "fixed" and is not. Meanwhile a job that dies at minute one now blocks the next attempt for up to 29 minutes instead of 9, and this is a nightly job, so the outage is a missed report. The actual fix is at the resource: write the report under a condition on the lock's generation number, so the second writer's PutItem fails rather than doubling the figures — and DynamoDB conditional expressions already do exactly this. Then set the TTL by how long you are willing to wait after a crash, which is the only question left, and it wants a shorter TTL rather than a longer one.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a permission granted at one place cannot be enforced at another place that does not check it, so the check has to live where the action lands. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The abstraction is enforcement at the point of effect — a credential is only as good as the last party that verifies it, and expiry is meaningless to anyone who does not look. Instances: a hotel key card that still opens the door after checkout because the lock was never told; a building pass collected on paper at reception while the turnstile reads only the card; a "reserved" sign on a restaurant table, which has no authority over anyone who sits down; a revoked TLS certificate that keeps working because clients do not check revocation; a fired employee whose VPN account was disabled while their SSH key stayed in authorized_keys; a prescription that a pharmacy fills without checking whether it was already filled elsewhere. The tell in every case: someone can name the authority that granted permission, and nobody can name the thing that checks it at the moment it is used.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
