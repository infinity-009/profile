Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cross-AZ traffic explained well nowhere else

Eight services across three zones cross a billable boundary 5.3 times per request, and cutting a replica makes it worse

Availability zones exist so that one datacentre failing does not take you down, and the bill for that is invisible in the architecture diagram — because the diagram draws a replica as a box and the charge is on the arrows between them. Replication, the line everyone blames, is 1.5% of it.

01

A line on the bill nobody could point at in the code

Nine tiers. One region. Every call goes to a hostname the team owns, over a private network, with no internet anywhere in the path. The architecture diagram is nine boxes and about a dozen lines, and it is accurate.

The invoice has a line called inter-zone data transfer, and it is larger than the line for the database the whole system exists to serve. Nobody can point at the request that produced it, because every request in the system looks free.

Here is where it comes from, and it is one multiplication. The service takes 10,000 requests a second. A request touches eight services after it lands, and each service-to-service call moves about 4 kB of request plus response. The deployment is spread across three availability zones, because that is what a serious deployment does, and every service’s instances are spread across all three.

So when service A calls service B, the load balancer picks an instance of B. One zone in three is A’s own. Two hops in three leave the zone.

Eight hops at two-thirds is 5.33 boundary crossings per request. Add the hop from the edge load balancer into the fleet and it is 6.0 of 9. At 4 kB a hop that is 218 MB/s of traffic that exists only because the fleet is spread out — about 574 terabytes a month, on a diagram that draws it as eight lines with no cost attached.

The team’s first theory was replication factor 3, because that is everyone’s first theory. The replica writes in this deployment are 4.1 MB/s — 1.5% of the total. They then dropped to replication factor 2 to test it, and the bill went up.

The architecture diagram draws a replica as a box, and the charge is on the arrows between them. A zone is not a property of a box or of a line; it is a property of the instance that answered, chosen at request time by a load balancer that is not in the drawing.

About every price on this page. The byte rates, hop counts and ratios here are computed in src/sim/topologycost.mjs and asserted in its tests. The dollar figures are not. They use a round illustrative input — a cent per gigabyte, a dime per instance-hour, as of 2026-08, confidence low — because this page has no way to verify a cloud price and an unverified price is worse than none. Every finding below is a ratio, a multiplier or a structural claim that survives both prices being wrong. And one uncertainty is a factor of two on its own: some providers meter the sender only and some meter both ends. Check your own bill; that uncertainty is part of the lesson.

02

The obvious fix is fewer replicas, and it makes the total larger

The reasoning is clean and almost everyone does it. Cross-zone traffic comes from spreading replicas across zones. Replication factor 3 means two of every three replica writes leave the zone. Drop to two replicas and you cut that in half.

The first half is true: (RF − 1)/RF is 67% of replicated write bytes at RF=3 and 50% at RF=2. The conclusion does not follow, and the model says the total goes the other way. Find out why by dragging replication factor below.

Predict A request touches eight services. Each service's instances are spread evenly over three availability zones, and a caller picks one without regard to zone. On average, how many of the eight hops cross a zone boundary?
A hop lands in the caller's own zone with probability 1/3, so it crosses with probability 2/3, and 8 × 2/3 = 5.33. “About 2.7” is 8 × 1/3 — the hops that stay local, which is the complement and the easiest thing to compute by accident. “None” is what the architecture diagram says, and it is the belief this lesson is about: same region and private network mean no internet charge, which is a different line item from zone-to-zone transfer.
Show it without answering

Three things in there are worth saying out loud.

Replication factor 2 costs more than 3, and replication factor 5 costs less. At 10,000 rps with 9 reads per write, the totals are 281.8, 278.7, 273.3 and 269.1 MB/s for RF 2, 3, 4 and 5. Write fan-out rises with RF exactly as expected. What falls faster is reads: a quorum read finds a local replica only when the reader’s own zone holds one, and at RF=2 a third of the zones hold none, so a 2-of-3-style read crosses 1.33 boundaries instead of 1.00. Reads outnumber writes nine to one, so the read term is 4.5× the write term and it decides the direction. Cutting a replica bought a bigger bill and a worse failure budget.

The crimson block is the finding, not the amber one. Turn locality on and 245.8 MB/s vanishes. What is left is 32.9 MB/s that no routing policy touches, and the bar shows it in exactly the same place and at exactly the same width as before. That invariance is the definition of the split, and it is asserted rather than drawn: topologycost.test.mjs checks that turning locality on moves zero irreducible bytes.

The bottom bar is why this is not an advert for locality. Nine extra instances appear — 81 against 72 — because each zone now rounds its own capacity up on its own. At this traffic level that trade returns about 20×. Drop the traffic to a few hundred requests a second and it returns 0.2×, and the same configuration everybody copies from a blog post becomes a way to spend more money.

03

The intuition: three buildings, and a courier charge on every handoff

Your company has grown, and you split it across three offices in the same city. Not for space — for survival. If a building floods you lose a third of your people, not all of them.

There is one rule you did not think about when you signed the leases: anything that moves between buildings goes by courier, and the courier charges per envelope. Inside a building, walking a document down the corridor is free.

Now a customer order needs eight approvals. Each approval can be given by whoever is on duty in that role, and each role has people in all three buildings — you staffed it that way deliberately, so no single building can hold up the company. The dispatcher hands the form to whoever is free.

One time in three, the next person is in the same building. Two times in three, it goes by courier. Eight approvals, five and a third courier trips. The org chart shows eight approvals. The courier bill shows 5.33, and the org chart is what everyone reviews.

Three more things fall out of the picture, and they are the whole rest of the lesson.

The fix is obvious, and its price is not the one people name. Tell the dispatcher to prefer someone in the same building. Courier trips collapse to nearly zero. The price everyone names is the idle staff — each building has to be able to absorb a flooded building’s share, so you employ more people than the work needs. But that was already true before you touched the dispatcher: surviving a flood is why you employ them, and it costs the same whoever the dispatcher picks. What the new rule actually adds is rounding. A role that needs one person in total now needs one in each building, because preferring the local one is worthless if there is nobody local. Three people for one person’s work. For a role that needs fifty, you round up by nothing at all.

Some trips can never be avoided. The company secretary is one person, and only they can apply the seal — so every sealed document travels, whatever the dispatcher prefers. And the whole reason you have three buildings is that the master ledger is copied into all three: copying it is what the other buildings are for, so that traffic is not waste, it is the product.

And the cheapest arrangement is one building. Zero courier charges. That is the trade in one sentence, and it is why nobody does it.

Every cross-zone byte is either a choice you made about routing or a consequence of wanting to survive a zone failure. Separating those two piles is the entire practical output — because you can negotiate with the first and you can only re-architect the second.

04

The mechanism: one probability, and the two things it does not apply to

Everything starts with one hop and one probability. A caller in some zone calls a service whose instances are spread evenly over AZ zones, and picks one without regard to zone.

P(one hop crosses a boundary) = 1 − 1/AZ

Two thirds on three zones, half on two, three quarters on four. The zone is not in the DNS name, the service definition or the diagram — it is a property of the instance the load balancer happened to choose.

E[crossings per request] = k × (1 − 1/AZ)

For a request that makes k hops. Note what is absent: the shape of the path. A chain (gateway → a → b → c) and a fan-out (gateway → a, b, c) have the same expectation, because every hop’s destination zone is uniform and independent of its source’s. Both shapes are enumerated over every zone assignment in topologycost.test.mjs and they agree to nine decimal places.

At k=8 and three zones that is 5.33, and the entry hop makes 6.0 of 9. This is the number the lesson exists for, because it is the number an architecture review has no way to see.

Now the traffic that probability does not describe

A routing policy chooses among equivalents. That is the whole of its power and the whole of its limit. When the destination is specific — this replica, that leader, the one aggregator — there is nothing to choose and the crossing is structural.

cross-zone replica writes per write = RF − (Σz nz²) ÷ RF  ·  which is RF − 1 when RF ≤ AZ

nz is the replicas in zone z under correct spreading. With RF at or below the zone count every zone holds at most one, so one replica write is local and RF−1 cross — a share of (RF − 1)/RF of all replicated write bytes, which is a property of the topology and not of the data size. Checked against enumeration over every coordinator choice for all RF ≤ 7 and AZ ≤ 5.

Two consequences of that formula are worth more than the formula.

W does not appear in it. A quorum write of 2-of-3 sends the payload to all three replicas and waits for two. Lowering the consistency level buys latency and moves no bytes. There is no W parameter anywhere in topologycost.mjs, and a test says so.

Packing replicas into one zone crosses nothing. crossZoneReplicaWrites is 2.0 on three zones, 1.33 on two and 0 on one. So this line is not an accident of replication — it is exactly the price of zone fault tolerance, priced in bytes, and the arithmetic proof is that the cheapest placement is the one that defeats the purpose of the zones.

Three more structural lines, each irreducible for its own reason:

  • Anti-entropy and repair are pairwise, not fan-out from a coordinator, so their multiplier is the share of replica pairs in different zones: 1 − Σ C(n_z,2) / C(RF,2). With RF at or below the zone count that is 1.0 — every byte of repair traffic crosses a boundary, which is the worst possible multiplier and is what correct placement buys.
  • Quorum reads contact R replicas and at most one is local, so R−1 crossings are structural. And at consistency ONE there is still a remainder: 1 − min(RF,AZ)/AZ, which is zero only when the replication factor covers every zone. RF=2 on three zones strands a third of even the weakest reads, and no routing fixes it — only more replicas.
  • A singleton — a partition’s leader, a lock service, a sequencer, a metrics aggregator — takes 1 − 1/AZ of its traffic across a boundary, permanently, because there is no local instance to prefer. This is the cleanest statement of the asymmetry: locality routing needs a choice, and a singleton is the absence of one.

The definition that makes the split usable

reducible = removable by changing where you route  ·  irreducible = removable only by changing RF, the consistency level, or the fault tolerance

Not “fixed” and “variable” — who can fix it. A quorum write is irreducible to a platform team and negotiable to whoever owns the durability requirement. Sorting the bill this way tells you which conversation to have, which is more useful than a total.

And the other side of the ledger

headroom = AZ ÷ (AZ − 1)

Capacity you must own as a multiple of the capacity your traffic needs, to survive losing one zone. 1.5× on three zones, 2× on two, 1.33× on four. It is a continuous lower bound: integer instance counts make it worse, so pooledInstances searches for the smallest fleet that survives losing its largest zone rather than trusting the formula. At 5 instances of demand it wants 7.5 and needs 8.

The numbers: where the bill actually goes, and when any of it matters

The deployment from slot 1, decomposed. Three zones, RF 3, 10,000 rps, 10% writes, 4 kB a hop, 2 kB a replicated payload, quorum reads of 2 of 3.

Turning locality on takes the total from 278.7 MB/s to 32.9 MB/s — 8.5× — and the irreducible share goes from 11.8% to 100% by construction, because the only thing that changed is the part routing could reach.

The log tier is the interesting column. Its produce hop cannot be routed around, because a partition has exactly one leader and the producer must write to it — a mesh hop and a produce hop look identical on a diagram and sit in different columns here. Its replication is two remote copies of every byte, by definition. The only routable line is the consumer fetch, and it is routable only because the ability to read from a nearby follower had to be added to the protocol, for exactly this reason. When 80% of a bill is irreducible, tuning routing is not the lever; the lever is a different durability mechanism, which is why a class of Kafka-compatible systems exists whose entire pitch is replacing broker-to-broker replication with a write to object storage.

Two useful per-unit figures fall out, and both are ratios rather than prices:

  • One 4 kB hop over three zones is 2.73 GB per million requests. Nine hops is 24.6 GB per million requests — about 50 cents per million requests at the illustrative price, for crossings alone. That is the unit-economics primitive: a hop has a price per million requests and an architecture review can add them up.
  • An extra instance must remove instance-hour price ÷ (per-GB price × billed directions) gigabytes per hour to pay for itself — 5 GB an hour, or about 1.4 MB/s, at the illustrative inputs, and twice that if only one direction is billed. Scale both prices together and it does not move, which is the property that makes it safe to quote. For a 4 kB hop it puts the locality threshold at roughly 500 requests per second through the path, and — because both the saving and the penalty scale with the number of tiers — that threshold is independent of how many services you have.

The trade-off: every lever that reduces the bill removes something you bought zones for

There are exactly five ways to make this number smaller, and each one gives back part of the reason the zones were there.

LeverWhat it removesWhat it costs
Zone-aware routing88% of a chatty mesh’s billper-zone rounding (up to AZ−1 instances per service), a smaller candidate set for load balancing, and a failover path that must actually work
Fewer zones25% of crossings going 3 → 2headroom rises from 1.50× to 2.00× — every instance’s worth of capacity now needs a twin sitting idle in the other zone
Pack replicas into one zonethe whole replication linezone fault tolerance — the entire point
Read at ONE instead of a quorum18.4 MB/s, the largest irreducible linestale reads, which is the quorums lesson’s bill, not this one’s
Larger payloads, fewer hopscrossings are per hop, so merging services removes them wholesalethe reason the services were separate, which is usually organisational rather than technical

Four of those are worth stating properly, because the arithmetic is not the obvious one.

The 50% headroom is the price of zones, not of locality. This is the number I expected to come out differently, and the model corrected it. The natural story is “locality concentrates load, so each zone needs 50% spare, so locality costs you idle compute.” The 50% is real — headroomFactor(3) = 1.5 — but a zone-blind fleet pays exactly the same, because losing a zone deletes that zone’s instances regardless of how traffic was routed. What locality actually adds is per-zone rounding: each zone must round its own capacity up independently, which is bounded by AZ−1 instances per service. Per service, at 5 instances of demand it is 9 against 8 — over nine tiers, 81 against 72, or 12.5%. At 50 instances of demand it is 75 against 75, or nothing at all. At 1 it is 3 against 2, or 50%. So the penalty is a property of service size, and it is worst for exactly the deployments that generate the most crossings — a mesh of many small services.

Locality is a preference, and a preference that silently becomes a pin is an outage. Every number above assumes that when a zone dies its traffic fails over to the others. If the routing policy has no failover configured, the survivors were sized for their own traffic and the dead zone’s share has nowhere to go — a zone failure now drops a third of requests instead of shedding a third of capacity. The headroom arithmetic is only true if the failover exists, and the failover is the part nobody tests.

Locality shrinks the pool your load balancer chooses from, and that shows up in the tail. With thirty instances in one pool a single slow one touches a thirtieth of requests. Split that into three local pools of ten and it touches a tenth of its own zone’s traffic. Zone-aware routing does not change how a balancer picks; it changes how much of the traffic one bad instance can reach, and it does so by a factor of the zone count. The p99 a large shared pool was smoothing gets worse, and that cost appears on no invoice — which is why it is the one that surprises people after the change ships.

And the honest ceiling: none of this makes the boundary cheaper. Every lever above moves traffic off a boundary or removes a boundary. The irreducible floor for a three-zone, RF-3, quorum-read deployment is around 12% of its zone-blind bill and it does not go to zero without giving up something structural. If that floor is still too expensive, you are not looking at a routing problem or a tuning problem — you are looking at a design that has to change, which is the point at which a number becomes an architecture decision.

07

In the wild

Everything below is a structural claim — that a mechanism exists and why it was built. No prices, because none can be verified here, and cloud pricing changes.

The charge itself is a documented line item, and the details differ per provider. The major clouds publish data-transfer pricing that distinguishes traffic staying inside one zone from traffic crossing between zones in the same region, so a figure from one is not a figure for another. What varies, and what you must check on your own account, is whether the charge applies to the sender, the receiver, or both; whether it is waived inside particular managed services; and how it interacts with load balancers, NAT and private endpoints. That variation is a factor of two or more, which is why this lesson reports multipliers.

Zone-aware routing is a first-class feature in every serious service mesh, and it exists for this bill. Envoy implements zone-aware routing, which biases a cluster’s load balancing toward endpoints in the client’s own zone and falls back when the local zone lacks healthy capacity. Istio exposes the same idea as locality load balancing, with explicit failover priority so that a preference does not become a pin. Kubernetes has it at the Service level as topology-aware routing and, more recently, trafficDistribution: PreferClose. The presence of failover configuration in all three is the interesting part: the feature’s designers knew that pinning without a fallback converts a cost optimisation into an availability regression.

Cassandra separates the two decisions this lesson separates, in its configuration. NetworkTopologyStrategy with a rack-aware snitch places replicas in distinct racks, which in cloud deployments are mapped to availability zones — that is the placement that makes every repair stream and every majority read cross a boundary. Separately, the driver’s routing policy decides which coordinator a client talks to, which is the reducible hop. And LOCAL_QUORUM is a per-datacentre majority, not a per-rack one, so inside one region it still crosses zones by construction. Three knobs, three different columns of the bill.

Kafka is the clearest worked example, because the fix had to be added to the protocol. Replication is leader-to-follower across racks, and a producer must write to the partition’s one leader, so both of those are structural — the produce hop is a singleton hop. Consumers were also required to read from the leader until KIP-392, “Allow consumers to fetch from closest replica”, whose stated motivation is cross-zone transfer cost; it works by having brokers advertise a rack and clients declare client.rack, with a pluggable replica selector. That is this lesson’s reducible/irreducible split appearing as a protocol change: the consumer side had a choice available and nobody had exposed it, and the producer side never had one.

And the design response, when the floor is the problem. A class of Kafka-compatible systems — WarpStream and AutoMQ among them — replaces broker-to-broker replication with a write to object storage, so the durability that used to require two cross-zone copies is provided by a service that is already regional. Whether that trade is right for a given workload depends on the latency it adds and the operational model it changes, and this lesson makes no claim about the economics. What it is a clean example of is the last row of slot 6: when the irreducible share is 80%, the lever is not the routing policy, and a cost number has become an architecture decision.

  • A singleton — a leader, a lock service, a sequencer, a partition's one leader — receives (1 − 1/AZ) of its traffic across a boundary and no routing policy can fix it, because there is no local instance to route to
  • The cheapest topology is one zone, so the cross-zone replication line is exactly the price of zone fault tolerance and not an accident of it

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A request touches eight services. Each is spread evenly over three zones and callers pick an instance without regard to zone. How many boundary crossings, and why is the architecture diagram no help?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Each hop lands in the caller's own zone with probability 1/3, so it crosses with probability 2/3, and 8 × 2/3 = 5.33 crossings per request. The diagram is no help because it draws eight lines and a line has no zone on it — the zone is a property of the instance that answered, which is chosen at request time by a load balancer the diagram does not contain. The same count holds whether the path is a chain or a fan-out from one gateway, because every hop's destination zone is uniform and independent of its source's.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A team's cross-zone bill is large, so they drop replication factor from 3 to 2 to cut it. What happens, on a read-heavy workload, and why?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The bill goes up. Write fan-out falls — 1 remote replica instead of 2 — but with RF=2 only two of the three zones hold a replica, so a third of readers have no local copy and a quorum read now crosses 1.33 boundaries instead of 1.00. At nine reads per write the read term is 4.5× the size of the write term, so the total rises by about 1%, and they have also given up a failure of tolerance. The bill is not monotonic in RF: on this workload RF=5 is cheaper on the wire than RF=3.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Which parts of a three-zone deployment's cross-zone traffic can zone-aware routing remove, and which can it not? State the rule, not a list.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It can remove any hop whose destination has an equivalent instance in the caller's own zone — service-to-service calls, the entry hop, a read that any replica can serve. It cannot remove a hop whose destination is specific: a quorum write must reach replicas in other zones, because that is what the other zones are for; anti-entropy must compare two replicas, which by correct placement are in different zones; a singleton has one instance. The rule is that routing chooses among equivalents, so it reaches exactly the traffic that had a choice.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    "Zone-aware routing saves egress and costs you 50% idle compute." What is wrong with the second half of that sentence?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The 50% is real and it is not caused by locality. Surviving one zone of three requires AZ/(AZ−1) = 1.5× the capacity your traffic needs, and a zone-blind fleet pays it too — losing a zone still deletes that zone's instances. What locality actually adds is per-zone rounding: each zone must round its own capacity up independently, which costs at most AZ−1 instances per service. Per service, at 5 instances of demand that is 9 against 8 — 12.5%, or 81 against 72 over nine tiers; at 50 it is 75 against 75, or nothing; at 1 it is 3 against 2, or 50%.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    You are asked whether locality routing is worth turning on. What single number decides it, and why is it quotable when a dollar figure is not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The byte rate each extra instance has to remove to pay for itself, which is instance-hour price ÷ (per-GB price × billed directions) — about 1.4 MB/s at a dime an hour and a cent a gigabyte both ways. It is quotable because it is a ratio: if both prices are wrong by the same factor it does not move. Multiply by the extra instances and compare with the reducible byte rate. For a 4 kB hop over three zones that threshold lands at roughly 500 requests per second through the path, independent of how many services are in it.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A Spark job shuffles 4 TB between executors spread over three zones, and the platform team proposes pinning the whole job into a single zone. Use this lesson's split. Is that the same trade as pinning a request path, and does the answer change?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Same arithmetic, opposite conclusion. A shuffle is all-to-all, so 2/3 of the shuffle bytes cross a boundary by the same 1 − 1/AZ — about 2.7 TB of 4 TB — and it is entirely reducible, because any executor will do and there is no singleton and no quorum in the path. The difference is what pinning costs. Pinning a serving path costs you the ability to lose a zone while serving; pinning a batch job costs you a retry, because a job that dies with its zone can be re-run. That is why single-zone placement is the default advice for shuffles and the wrong advice for a request path: the reducible fraction is identical and the price of reducing it is not. It also explains why the cluster autoscaler for a batch pool and for a serving pool want opposite topologies.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: redundancy is priced on the interactions between the copies, and the drawing of the system shows the copies. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Multi-site manufacturing. Two plants instead of one survives a fire, and every sub-assembly that moves between them is freight — the org chart shows two plants and the logistics ledger shows the lanes. A hospital network with one MRI. Redundant wards, a singleton scanner, so every scan is a patient transport: the same "there is nothing local to route to" that a lock service has. Grid interconnection. Control areas share spinning reserve so one generator tripping is survivable, and sharing reserve requires transmission capacity that is built and paid for whether or not it carries anything — the headroom line, exactly. A team split across two offices. Conway's law with a per-message price: the structure is drawn as two boxes and the cost is in the coordination that crosses between them. In every case the tell is the same — the artefact everyone reviews is a picture of nodes, and the money is on edges nobody drew.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
