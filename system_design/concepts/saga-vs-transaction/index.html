Tier 8 · Architecture and patterns Saga vs transaction explained well nowhere else

The seat was sold to nobody for a second and a quarter

A saga replaces one atomic transaction with a sequence of local ones plus an undo for each. The guarantee it offers is real and it is not atomicity: either everything committed, or a prefix committed and was undone. Read that for what it does not say. The intermediate states are visible — that is the whole point, because releasing locks is what makes them visible — and some undos are not unreliable but impossible.

Read first — isolation-anomaliesreplication-lag

01

Four services, a declined card, and a seat that belongs to nobody

A booking. Four services, four databases, no shared transaction between them:

  1. Seats — reserve 14A.
  2. Email — send “your seat is confirmed”.
  3. Payments — charge £180.
  4. Ticketing — issue the boarding pass.

Each call takes about 250 ms. Each one fails about 0.1% of the time — three nines, which is better than most services manage. You run 180 bookings a second.

The card is declined at step 3. So you undo: un-send the email, release the seat. The first of those is not a thing you can do.

That is the interesting failure, and it is not the one that gets asked about. Here is the one that does not even look like a failure: on the 99.6% of bookings that succeed, for how long is seat 14A marked sold while no order exists? Step 1 commits at 250 ms and the order is not real until step 4 commits at 1000 ms. Any request in that window reads a seat that is sold to nobody, and every service involved returns the honest truth as it stands.

At 180 bookings a second, work out how many seats are in that state right now before reading on. It is not a small number and it is not on any dashboard, because nothing is broken.

02

The obvious fix — compensate — and what it does not fix

The standard answer is the saga: give every step an undo, and when a step fails, run the undos backwards. Garcia-Molina and Salem formalised it in 1987 and the guarantee is exactly this: either all of T₁…Tₙ committed, or some prefix T₁…Tⱼ committed and then Cⱼ…C₁ committed.

Read what that does not say. It does not say atomic. The paper is explicit that a saga is a non-atomic unit and that its intermediate states are visible to other transactions — because the entire motivation was to let a long-running transaction release its locks, and releasing locks is precisely what makes the intermediate states readable. The visibility is not an implementation defect to be engineered away. It is the feature, seen from the other side.

Predict A 4-step saga, 250 ms a step. You move the customer-visible write from step 1 to step 4 so customers never see a partial state. What happens to the total time-per-saga during which some invariant is false?
Step j's invariant is false for the durations of steps j+1…n, so the total over all j is d·Σ(n−j) = d·n(n−1)/2 — 1.5 s at four steps and 250 ms, with no reference to the order anywhere in it. Moving the visible write to the end takes its window to zero and hands the 0.75 s to whichever invariant is now first. That makes it good advice and not a reduction: you chose who gets the long window. Cutting the total needs fewer or faster steps.
Show it without answering

Two things the instrument says that the guarantee does not.

The window is a duration, and it has an occupancy. Step 1’s invariant is false for 0.75 s on the success path. At three reads a second against that entity, 2.25 concurrent readers see it. By Little’s Law at 180 sagas a second, 135 bookings are in a visibly partial state at every instant — that is the answer to slot 1, and it is 75% of everything in flight.

Some sagas never finish either way. The saga failed forward, tried to undo itself, and a compensation failed too. There is no retry that fixes it: retrying a compensation that may have half-applied is exactly the promise a compensation cannot make. At four steps, 0.1% step failures and 1% compensation failures, that is 1 saga in 16,818 — which sounds like nothing until you multiply by your throughput and get 925 a day that need a person. And if one single compensation is impossible rather than unreliable, it is 15,970 a day.

03

The intuition: the kitchen has already started

You order the fish. The waiter writes it down, the till records it, the kitchen ticket prints, and the chef starts. Four minutes later the fish is off — the last portion went to another table.

Nobody can undo that. What they can do is compensate: come back, apologise, offer you the chicken, void the fish line, refund if you had paid. And notice what compensating leaves behind. There is a void line on the till roll. Your table has been told the fish was available when it was not. The chef threw away four minutes. “Ordered fish, then voided” is not the same state as “never ordered fish” — a manager reading the till roll at the end of the night can tell the difference, and so can you.

Now the two costs, in the same room.

For those four minutes, the till said you ordered fish and it was false. A second waiter reading the till, the runner, the expediter, anyone checking covers — they all read something true when written and false when read. Nothing was broken. And the apology cannot be un-said: that step has no undo at all, so if the chicken is also off, there is no sequence of actions that returns the evening to its starting state. Somebody has to come to the table and improvise. That is a stuck saga, and the improvising person is the 925 a day.

04

The mechanism: 3n states, and one combinatorial quantity twice

A transaction has two observable states. Not-yet and done. Whatever it contains, whatever it touches, readers outside it see one or the other — that is what isolation buys, and it is the thing being sold here.

An n-step saga has 3n. Three phases, and the middle one is the one people forget:

PhaseStatesWhy
forwardn + 1k of n steps applied, k = 0…n
compensatingnrolling back, k of the original steps still applied
stuckn − 1the undo of step k failed, so steps 1…k stay applied

A compensating state with k steps applied is not the same state as a forward state with k steps applied, and that subtlety is the whole difference between 3n and 2n. A compensation is a semantic undo and it leaves its own trace: a release record, an audit row, a refund line on a statement. Readers can tell the two apart, and so can customers. Four steps gives twelve states a concurrent request can observe, against a transaction’s two.

The stuck states run k = 1…n−1 rather than 0…n, and the reason is mechanical: an orchestrator stops at the first compensation that fails, because continuing to undo on top of an unknown state is worse than stopping. So a stuck saga always strands a prefix of its steps, never a scattered subset.

Where a saga stops, term by term

With per-step success p and per-compensation success q:

P(fails at step i)       = p^(i−1)·(1 − p)
P(compensated | fail i)  = Π_{j<i} q_j        every undo it needs succeeds
P(stuck | fail i)        = 1 − Π_{j<i} q_j    at least one undo fails

Failing at step 1 needs zero compensations, so its stuck term is exactly zero — nothing had committed. Summing gives the three outcomes, and none of them is computed by subtracting the others from 1: a stuck rate of 6 × 10⁻⁵ obtained as a residual between two numbers near 1 loses eleven digits to cancellation, and it is the number the whole model exists to produce.

The rule of thumb, which is the shape rather than the digits

With p^(i−1) ≈ 1 and 1 − q^(i−1) ≈ (i−1)g, the sum collapses:

P(stuck) ≈ f · g · n(n−1)/2

Quadratic in the number of steps. Doubling a workflow’s length roughly quadruples the rate at which it strands itself, at unchanged per-step reliability — 4 steps to 8 multiplies it by 4.59. Accurate to within a few percent while f and g are both under about 5%, and it over-estimates, which is the right direction for a rule of thumb.

The conservation law, which is the useful one

Step j’s invariant is false for the durations of steps j+1…n. Summing over every invariant the saga breaks:

total exposure = d · Σ(n−j) = d · n(n−1)/2

The same n(n−1)/2. That is not a coincidence and it is not deep: both quantities count ordered pairs of steps, because both ask “for how many later steps is this earlier step’s effect already committed and not yet resolved”. It is why a saga’s cost grows quadratically in its length in two independent senses, and why add one more step to the workflow is not a linear decision.

And the expression contains no reference to order, so reordering cannot reduce the total. It only decides which invariant gets the long window.

Note which lanes are healthy. Seats and Payments behave perfectly throughout — every value they return is true when they return it. The failure is not in a service.

05

The numbers: what the transaction would have cost instead

The comparison the saga is usually justified against is two-phase commit, and it is usually justified on availability alone, which is the smaller half of the case.

Availability, for four participants at three nines each. 2PC needs every participant and the coordinator up for the whole of one attempt, so commit availability is a^(k+1) = 99.501%. The shape is the same as W = N in the quorum lesson and it has the same consequence: fault tolerance zero. One participant down and nothing commits.

Lock-seconds, which is the half usually left out. Every participant holds its write locks from its own prepare until the coordinator’s decision arrives — so each one holds for the slowest participant’s work plus a round trip, and there are k of them:

That is the structural difference and not a tuning one. Under 2PC, one slow participant lengthens k locks; under a saga it lengthens one.

The in-doubt window, whose probability is tiny and whose blast radius is not. Between the last vote and the durable commit record there is a ~15 ms interval in which a coordinator crash leaves every participant holding locks with no unilateral right to decide. Per transaction that is about 6 × 10⁻⁹. But the locks then stay held for the whole coordinator outage — at four conflicting transactions a second, a ten-minute outage blocks 2,400 of them. And on PostgreSQL an orphaned prepared transaction also pins the xmin horizon, so vacuum stops making progress cluster-wide for the duration; this is why max_prepared_transactions ships at 0 and you must deliberately turn it on.

Where the nines argument actually goes wrong

Both patterns retry, and on paper retrying closes most of the a^(k+1) gap. What retries do not close is that outages last minutes. Several retries inside one minute are all inside the same outage and are not independent attempts. The count of genuinely independent attempts is the retry horizon divided by the outage duration — and the horizons differ by three orders of magnitude for a structural reason:

  • A distributed transaction’s horizon is its lock timeout, tens of seconds, because waiting means holding locks.
  • A saga’s horizon is the business’s patience, hours, because a durable orchestrator that waits is holding nothing.

Against a two-minute outage, a 30-second lock timeout gives the transaction one real attempt and a one-hour horizon gives the saga 30. Quote the optimistic figure that assumes independent retries and you get 99.97% for the transaction; count attempts honestly and you get 93.2%.

The per-saga costs are identical — the window is 0.75 s either way, and 2.25 readers see it either way. What changes is whether the aggregate is a rounding error or a team. At a hundred bookings a day, use a transaction if you can and a saga with a manual runbook if you cannot, and stop; 925 interventions a day is a staffed on-call function, and that is the number that should decide whether the workflow gets shortened.

06

The trade-off: you bought the ability to wait, and paid in being wrong

Every account of this pattern lists what you gave up as atomicity, which is true and is not specific enough to act on. Three things, in the order they will bite.

You gave up isolation, not just atomicity, and it is the one you did not price. Atomicity is preserved in a weakened form — the saga does reach all-or-a-compensated-prefix. Isolation is not preserved at all: 3n states are readable where 2 were. Every anomaly in isolation-anomalies is available to you again, at application level, with no database to blame — and the anomaly window is not a scheduling accident measured in microseconds, it is your own step durations, 0.75 s of it, by design.

You gave up the guarantee that “undo” exists. A transaction’s rollback is a mechanical property of the log. A compensation is a business operation you have to write, and the model’s honest input is per-step rather than one scalar, because some compensations are not unreliable — they are impossible. q = 0 for the confirmation email is the correct model and not a pessimistic one. Then: making all your compensations ten times more reliable takes the unresolvable rate from 925 a day to 93; doing the same with one impossible step in the chain takes it from 15,970 to 15,552, which is 2.6%. So the useful question is never how reliable your compensations are. It is which step cannot be undone, and can it go last — because a step at position n is never compensated at all.

What you bought is not nines. It is the right to wait. Both patterns retry; the saga’s advantage is that its retry horizon is hours rather than seconds, and that is not a tuning choice, it is because a durable orchestrator that waits holds no locks while a prepared transaction that waits holds all of them. That is the trade in one line: a saga can wait, and the price of being able to wait is that the data is wrong for as long as it waits. Which is the same trade as the anomaly window, arriving from the other direction — and it means the pattern is correct precisely when a temporarily-wrong answer is cheaper than a temporarily-unavailable one, and wrong when it is not. A seat reservation qualifies. A ledger balance a regulator reads does not.

The mitigations worth knowing, and what each one actually costs:

  • Semantic locks — a PENDING status on the row, so readers can see that a value is provisional. This is the honest fix and it converts an invisible anomaly into a visible one, at the cost of every reader having to handle a third state. It does not shorten the window; it makes it legible.
  • Reorder so the irreversible step is last. Free, effective for the invariant you moved, and worth doing — but the total is conserved, so be clear you moved the exposure rather than removing it.
  • Fewer steps, or faster steps. The only two things that reduce d·n(n−1)/2. The quadratic says the step count is the stronger lever: halving n divides the exposure by about 4.
  • Make the whole thing one transaction. Still available, and it is the right answer more often than the pattern’s popularity suggests — if the entities live in one database, the argument above is a list of costs with nothing on the other side. An entity split across services makes every write to it a saga whether or not anyone called it one, so this decision was frequently made by a boundary somebody drew years ago. See the-distributed-monolith.
07

In the wild

The paper is 1987 and it is short. Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, Sagas (SIGMOD 1987), written about long-running transactions in a single database rather than about microservices. Its motivation is the one above: a transaction that runs for a long time should not hold its locks for that long, so let it commit in pieces and supply compensations. Everything the pattern is criticised for today is stated in the paper as a consequence, including the visibility of intermediate states.

Pat Helland’s “Life beyond Distributed Transactions: an Apostate’s Opinion” (CIDR 2007) is the argument that scale-out forces this on you whether or not you like the pattern: once data is partitioned into entities that cannot be locked together, there is no atomic multi-entity operation to have, and workflow plus compensation is what remains. It also names the design rule that follows — decide your entity boundary first, because it decides which operations can be transactions.

Gregor Hohpe’s “Starbucks Does Not Use Two-Phase Commit” (2004) is the same argument as an observation: the barista starts your drink before your payment clears, and when the card is declined the compensation is a business decision — remake, refund, or give it away — not a rollback. The essay’s point is that the physical world runs on compensation because holding locks across a queue of customers is not available, and the interesting engineering is in choosing the compensations.

Caitie McCaffrey’s work on the saga pattern (2015) is the best-known production account, from the Halo 4 statistics service: sagas run by a durable orchestrator, with the practical findings that compensations must be idempotent, that the orchestrator’s own log is the thing that must not be lost, and that steps which cannot be compensated need to be identified as a design activity rather than discovered in an incident.

Durable execution is the pattern productised. Temporal, and Cadence before it, exist to make the orchestrator’s journal the primitive — workflow code recorded step by step and replayed deterministically after a crash, which is what makes the “hours of retry horizon” in slot 5 available without someone writing a state machine by hand. The costs move rather than disappear: workflow code must be deterministic, activities must be idempotent, and versioning a workflow while executions are in flight becomes its own problem.

  • Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, Sagas (SIGMOD 1987) — the guarantee, and the explicit statement that a saga is not atomic and its intermediate states are visible.
  • Pat Helland, Life beyond Distributed Transactions: an Apostate’s Opinion (CIDR 2007) — why partitioning removes the atomic multi-entity operation, and why the entity boundary is the decision.
  • Gregor Hohpe, Starbucks Does Not Use Two-Phase Commit (2004) — compensation as a business decision rather than a rollback.
  • Caitie McCaffrey, Applying the Saga Pattern (2015) — the production account, and the practice of identifying non-compensable steps deliberately.
  • Model: src/sim/saga.mjs — the outcome closed forms, the exposure closed form, and the 2PC lock arithmetic. Graded in src/sim/saga.test.mjs, where a 200,000-trial simulation that walks the mechanism step by step reproduces the closed forms, the conservation law d·n(n−1)/2 is asserted exactly, and every figure quoted on this page is pinned so prose and model cannot drift.
  • One deliberate omission: the simulation also shows that plugging the mean window into 1 − e^(−rD) over-states the fraction of sagas anybody notices, by 10% at constant step durations and by 23% when steps are bursty, because the expression is concave in D. It is a real result and it is Jensen’s inequality rather than anything about sagas, so it is left in the model and out of the lesson.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A saga guarantees either all steps committed or a prefix committed and was compensated — which is not atomicity, and the paper says so
  • An n-step saga has 3n observable states against a transaction's 2, because "reserved then released" is a different observable state from "never reserved"
  • A compensation is a semantic undo that leaves its own trace — a refund line, a release record, an audit row — and not a rollback
  • The inconsistency window is a duration rather than an instant, so the expected number of concurrent readers who see a partial state is r × D and it is not small
  • Total exposure across every invariant is d·n(n−1)/2 and is independent of step order, so reordering moves the window onto a different invariant and never shrinks the total
  • The stuck rate — failed forward, then failed to undo — is ≈ f·g·n(n−1)/2, so it is quadratic in the number of steps
  • One impossible compensation dominates every unreliable one, because it converts every downstream failure into a stuck saga regardless of how good the others are
  • Two-phase commit needs every participant and the coordinator up for one attempt, so its commit availability is a^(k+1) and its fault tolerance is zero
  • The saga's real advantage is that it can wait, because a durable orchestrator that waits holds no locks — and the price of waiting is that the data is wrong for as long as it waits

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A four-step saga completes 99.6% of the time on the first pass and every failure is compensated automatically. What has it still cost you, and how would you put a number on it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The window. Step 1's invariant is observably false from the moment step 1 commits until the saga finishes or step 1 is undone — at 250 ms a step that is 0.75 s, not an instant. At three reads per second per entity that is 2.25 concurrent readers seeing a state that is about to stop being true, and by Little's Law at 180 sagas per second there are 135 entities in a visibly partial state at every instant. None of that appears in the success rate, which is why quoting the success rate is the standard way to make this pattern look free.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Someone proposes putting the customer-visible write last so the customer never sees a partial state. Does the total exposure fall?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — by exactly zero. Step j's invariant is false for the durations of steps j+1…n, so summing over every invariant gives d · n(n−1)/2, which contains no reference to the order. At four steps and 250 ms that is 1.5 s of broken invariant per saga however you arrange them. The advice is still good, because it moves 100% of the exposure off the invariant a customer can see — but it moves it onto whichever invariant is now first, and reducing the total needs fewer steps or faster steps. The n(n−1)/2 says which of those pays: going from 8 steps to 4 divides the exposure by 4.7, not by 2.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your compensations succeed 99% of the time. You invest in making them succeed 99.9% of the time. One of them — the confirmation email — cannot be undone at all. What did the investment buy?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Almost nothing, and this is the finding that matters most. With every undo merely unreliable, the tenfold improvement takes the unresolvable rate from 925 sagas a day to 93 — a full 10×. With one undo impossible, the same investment takes it from 15,970 to 15,552, an improvement of 2.6%. An impossible compensation converts every downstream failure into a stuck saga on its own, so it sets a floor that no amount of reliability elsewhere can go under. The engineering question is therefore never "how reliable are our compensations" — it is which of our steps cannot be undone, and can it be moved last.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Why is a saga's advantage over a distributed transaction not really about the nines?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because both retry, and on paper retrying closes most of the a^(k+1) gap. What retries do not close is that outages last minutes, so several retries inside one minute are all inside the same outage and are not independent attempts. The number of genuinely independent attempts is the retry horizon divided by the outage duration — and the horizons differ structurally, because a transaction that waits is holding locks while a durable orchestrator that waits is holding nothing. A two-minute outage against a 30-second lock timeout gives the transaction one real attempt; against an hour of business patience it gives the saga 30. So the saga's advantage is that it can wait — and the price of being able to wait is that the data is wrong for as long as it waits, which is the same trade as the window, arriving from the other direction.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A team replaces a multi-row database transaction in one service with a saga across three services, and reports the migration a success because the error rate did not change. What did they not measure, and what would you ask them to look for in their support queue?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    They measured the outcome and not the window, and the outcome is the part a saga preserves. Ask for three things. One: reads of the entity that land mid-saga — a support ticket saying "it showed as confirmed and then it wasn't" is a customer who read state 5 of 9 and there is no bug to find, because every service returned the truth as it stood. Two: the count of manual interventions per week, because the stuck state has no automated exit by construction and it does not present as an error — the saga logged success on every step it managed to run. Three: whether any step is semantically irreversible, because one such step is worth more to the stuck rate than every retry policy combined. The honest report is not "the error rate is unchanged", it is "we converted a class of failures that the database handled into a class of failures a person handles, and here is the weekly headcount".
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: an operation too long or too distributed to hold everything still for is broken into independently-committed pieces, each with an explicit undo — so the operation becomes observable half-done, and some of the pieces turn out to have no real undo at all. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A house-buying chain. Nobody can hold every seller, buyer, lender and solicitor still until all of them are ready, so the transaction is decomposed: offer accepted, survey commissioned, mortgage offered, contracts exchanged, completion. Each piece commits locally and is visible to everyone — the house is marked "sold subject to contract", which is precisely a saga's intermediate state, readable by other buyers and true only conditionally. Most pieces have compensations, and they are semantic rather than atomic: you withdraw the offer, which is not the same as never having made it, because the seller now knows what you were willing to pay. And the survey fee has no compensation whatsoever — it is the un-sendable email of the chain, which is why an English chain collapsing leaves everyone materially worse off rather than merely back where they started, and why exchange of contracts exists as a deliberate point past which the pieces stop being independently undoable.

    Other instances: a surgical operation, where anaesthesia, incision and resection each commit and the patient is observably mid-procedure, and where the incision's compensation is a scar rather than an undo. A wedding, whose invitations cannot be un-sent, which is exactly why the irreversible step is placed as late as the calendar allows. And a newspaper correction, the canonical semantic undo: it does not remove the false claim from the readers who saw it, it appends a second record, which is why "reserved then released" and "never reserved" are different states in the model above.
    Did you have that?

Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
