Tier 7 · Security, privacy, compliance Deletion at scale explained well nowhere else

The privacy policy promised deletion in 30 days and the disaster-recovery policy kept backups for 35

A row lives in about eighty places and a DELETE reaches eight of them. The rest expire on their own schedule, and if any of those schedules outlives your deletion promise, the promise is false by arithmetic rather than by accident. There are exactly three honest resolutions and two of them are expensive.

Read first — replication-lagenvelope-encryption

01

The deletion job ran, reported success, and the auditor found the row anyway

The requirement arrived as one sentence: erase a user’s data within 30 days of the request. The implementation was a job: delete the rows, cascade the foreign keys, log the completion. It ran nightly, it reported 100% success for eight months, and it was correct about everything it could see.

Then an auditor asked to see the state of the system on a chosen day, which meant restoring a backup — and the deleted user was in it, complete. So was their row in the search index, their aggregate in the warehouse, and their original event in the stream.

Nothing had failed. Backup retention was 35 days for a disaster-recovery requirement that predated the privacy policy by three years, and 35 is larger than 30. A backup taken the day before any request keeps that data for 35 more days. There is no ordering of events in which the promise held — and the job’s success metric measured the eight copies it could reach out of roughly eighty.

Show it without answering

Three things to take from it.

The reachable set is the minority, and it is the only set anyone instruments. A deletion job’s success rate is measured over the copies it knows how to address, which is why it can be 100% and wrong at the same time.

The two panels move independently. Replicas and regions multiply the dots without touching the durations; retention moves both. A “reduce our copies” project and a “keep our deletion promise” project are different projects, and only one of them is the compliance one.

The band is a duration, not a probability. There is no configuration in which a 35-day backup satisfies a 30-day promise, and no monitoring that will catch it, because nothing is failing.

03

The intuition: a letter you sent to fourteen people

You wrote something and asked for it to be forgotten.

The person you wrote to deletes their copy immediately — that is the primary. Their two colleagues who were cc’d delete theirs when told, because they are in the loop and reachable — those are the replicas.

Then there is the rest.

The office keeps a printed archive of everything received, boxed by month, kept for three years. There is no procedure for opening a box to remove a page; the procedure is that boxes are destroyed after three years. That is your backup, and the only thing that touches it is the calendar.

Someone made a summary of the month’s correspondence for a meeting. Your letter is a line in it. The summary has no link back to your letter, and remaking it is not something anyone will do. That is your warehouse.

The switchboard log recorded that a letter arrived from you, when, and from where. It is append-only by design, because it exists to be trustworthy. That is your event stream.

So: you can be forgotten by the people, and not by the paper. Three ways out, and everyone who has faced this has picked one of them:

  • Keep the boxes for less time than you promise — a decision about how much history the business can afford to lose, made by someone whose job is not privacy.
  • Keep a list of retractions and go through it every time a box is reopened — correct, and it only works if nobody ever reopens a box in a hurry.
  • Write in a cipher, one key per correspondent, and burn the key — every copy becomes noise at once, in every box, including the ones you were never allowed to open. And if you gave one key to a hundred correspondents, burning it silences all hundred.

Deletion in a distributed system is not an operation, it is a property you have to design for before the data is written. What decides whether you can keep a deletion promise is the lifetime of the longest-lived copy — and what decides how finely you can keep it is the granularity of whatever you can destroy.

04

The mechanism: a census, a maximum, and three resolutions

copies = 1 + (replicas × regions − 1) + caches + ⌊retention / snapshot interval⌋ × regions + derived + logs

A DELETE reaches the first three terms. Nothing about it reaches the rest, because an immutable snapshot has no update path and a derived dataset has no reference back to its source.

feasible by deletion alone ⟺ max(every retention period) ≤ SLA

A maximum, not an average — one long-retained log defeats it however good the backups are. Asserted in erasure.test.mjs, including the case where 7-day backups still fail because a log is kept 400 days.

tombstones to carry = deletion requests per day × longest retention

Little’s Law again: a rate times a duration is a population. And note the duration is the backup’s, not the SLA’s — you must be able to re-apply a deletion to the oldest restorable copy.

The three resolutions, and what each actually commits you to.

ResolutionReaches every copyStanding costFails when
Shorten retention below the SLAyes, by expiryless restorable history — a DR decision, not a privacy onethe business needs the history, which is usually

Only the first makes DELETE sufficient on its own. The second is correct and depends on a procedure being followed during exactly the kind of emergency in which procedures are skipped. The third is why envelope encryption shows up in privacy architecture long before anyone is worried about cryptanalysis — and it inherits that lesson’s hard constraint, because you cannot shred more finely than the data key you already wrote.

And the copies a DELETE misses do not all miss for the same reason, which matters because the fixes differ:

  • Snapshots and backups are immutable by design. Nothing will ever reach into one. Only expiry and re-derivation apply.
  • Derived datasets — search index, warehouse, feature store, aggregate table — hold a transformed copy with no live link to the source. Deletion has to become an event each of them consumes, and every new derived dataset is a new place to implement it.
  • Append-only logs are trusted because they cannot be edited. The design move is not to delete from them but to keep nothing identifying in them: write a pseudonymous id and delete the mapping.
  • Caches are reachable but easy to forget, and a cache with a long TTL and no invalidation on delete will happily serve a deleted user’s profile for hours.

That last row is the one to argue with a stakeholder about. Weekly snapshots delete 60 of the 82 copies from the census and improve the promise by nothing, because the surviving snapshot is kept just as long. Copy count is an audit and cost problem. Survival time is the compliance problem. They respond to different levers.

What to set.

  • Write down every retention period in the system and take the maximum. That number, not the privacy policy, is your true deletion window today. Include application logs, the message broker’s retention, the CDN, and anything an analytics vendor holds.
  • Make the deletion promise longer than your longest retention, or make retention shorter. These are the only two ways DELETE alone is honest. Most teams discover their policy was written by someone who did not know the backup schedule.
  • Publish deletion as an event and make consuming it a requirement for any new derived dataset. Otherwise every new index, model or dashboard silently widens the gap.
  • Decide the crypto-shred granularity before the data is written, because it cannot be made finer afterwards without re-encrypting everything. If you owe per-subject erasure, a subject cannot share a key.
  • Put the post-restore deletion replay in the restore runbook, not in a document about privacy. It will be needed at 4 a.m. by someone dealing with a different emergency.
06

The trade-off: the promise, the history, and the granularity you already fixed

Shortening retention buys a true promise and sells recoverable history. This is a real trade and it belongs to the business, not to the privacy team: 35 days of backups exists because somebody imagined an incident discovered in week four. Cutting to 28 days is an explicit decision to be unable to recover from that. It is often the right call, and it is never a technical detail.

Tombstones buy correctness and sell operational reliability. The mechanism is sound and its weak point is human: the replay has to happen after every restore, and restores happen during incidents. You are buying a compliance guarantee with a promise about behaviour under stress, which is the weakest currency in the building. Automating the replay into the restore tooling converts it back into an engineering guarantee, and that is the only version worth having.

Crypto-shredding buys reach and sells a decision you must make years early. It is the only resolution that touches copies you cannot address, which is why it is the standard answer at scale. What it costs is that the data key’s granularity — chosen for the KMS request rate, quite possibly by someone optimising a bill — is now the finest deletion you can perform. A key shared across a thousand subjects makes per-subject erasure impossible, and there is no migration that splits it without re-encrypting the data.

And the constraint that is not a trade-off: what you cannot do is delete from an immutable store. Snapshots, append-only logs and write-ahead logs are trusted precisely because they cannot be edited, so the design move is never “reach in and remove” — it is to arrange that the identifying thing was never in there, or was encrypted with a key you can destroy. Deciding that after the data is written is the expensive case, and it is the usual one.

07

In the wild

GDPR Article 17 is narrower than “delete everything”, and the narrowness is the engineering opening. The right to erasure is qualified — by legal obligations to retain, by freedom of expression, by archiving in the public interest — and regulators’ guidance has consistently accepted that data in backups need not be reached into immediately, provided it is not restored into live use and expires on a defined schedule. That is why the tombstone-and-replay design is a compliance answer rather than a fudge: what is being promised is that no restore reintroduces the data, not that the tape is scrubbed.

NIST SP 800-88 names Cryptographic Erase as a sanitisation technique: destroy the key and the ciphertext is unrecoverable. This is the standard that makes crypto-shredding citable in an audit rather than a clever idea, and it is why per-tenant and per-subject key hierarchies appear in SaaS architecture documents that have nothing else to say about cryptography.

Cassandra’s tombstones are this lesson’s mechanism as a database feature, including its failure mode. A delete writes a tombstone, tombstones must survive gc_grace_seconds — long enough for every replica to have seen them — and a partition full of tombstones makes reads slow enough to page someone. It is a working demonstration that “deleted” is itself a piece of data you now have to store, replicate and eventually collect.

Kafka’s log compaction with a null value — a tombstone record — is the same idea in a log, and its constraint is instructive: compaction eventually removes the key, but “eventually” is a function of segment roll and compaction scheduling rather than a promise you can quote. If a deletion SLA depends on a compacted topic, the SLA depends on a background process’s throughput.

And the recurring incident shape is worth remembering, because it is not a bug. A team deletes correctly, a restore is performed for an unrelated reason, and the data returns. Nothing failed; the restore did exactly what restores do. The only defences are the replay step and the key you destroyed, which is why both belong in the restore runbook rather than in a privacy document nobody reads at 4 a.m.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A row exists in the primary, every replica, every snapshot, every derived dataset, the caches and the append-only log, and a DELETE reaches only the live copies
  • Feasibility is decided by the single longest-lived copy, so the log kept for a year defeats a deletion promise that the backups would have satisfied
  • Fewer copies is not shorter exposure: weekly instead of daily snapshots divides the copy count by seven and changes the survival time not at all
  • There are three resolutions — shorten retention below the SLA, replay deletions after every restore, or destroy the key — and only the first makes DELETE sufficient on its own
  • A tombstone set is a rate times a duration, sized by the longest backup rather than by the SLA: 5,000 requests a day against 35-day backups is 175,000 open tombstones
  • Crypto-shredding reaches copies you cannot address, which is why it is the standard answer, and it is only as fine-grained as the data key
  • Deletion must propagate to derived datasets explicitly, because a search index or warehouse row has no foreign key back to the thing that was deleted

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Three replicas in two regions, daily snapshots kept 35 days, a search index, a warehouse, an event stream and two cache tiers. How many copies of one row exist, and how many does a DELETE reach?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    82 copies. A DELETE reaches 8 — the primary, the five other live replicas, and the two caches if they are invalidated at all. The other 74 are 70 snapshots (35 days × 2 regions), three derived datasets and the append-only log, and none of them has any mechanism that responds to a DELETE. Under 10% coverage is the normal case, not a badly run system.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why is a 30-day deletion promise against 35-day backup retention false by arithmetic rather than occasionally?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a backup taken the day before the request is kept for a full 35 days from that moment. The worst case is always the retention period, so the data provably exists on day 31 for every request. It is not a race that usually goes your way — there is no version of the schedule in which it is satisfied. Either retention comes below the SLA, or something other than DELETE has to do the work.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A team moves from daily to weekly snapshots and reports that they have reduced deletion exposure sevenfold. What did they actually change?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The number of copies, from 70 to 10. Not the exposure, which is set by retention and is still 35 days: a weekly snapshot taken the day before the request keeps the data just as long. Copy count is a cost and an audit-surface problem; survival time is the compliance problem. Confusing them is the most common way a deletion review passes while the promise stays false.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You keep backups for 35 days for a genuine DR reason and cannot shorten them. Name the two remaining resolutions and the standing cost of each.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Tombstones: record every deletion and replay the set after any restore. Cost is a set sized by requests-per-day × longest-retention — 175,000 entries at 5,000 requests a day — and a post-restore step that must never be skipped, including at 4 a.m. during the incident that caused the restore. Crypto-shredding: destroy the key and every copy becomes unreadable at once, including copies you cannot address. Cost is that the data key's granularity is now your deletion granularity, which had to be decided before the data was written.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Why does a search index or a warehouse need deletion propagated explicitly, when a read replica does not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a replica applies your write log — the deletion is a replicated operation — whereas a derived dataset holds a transformed copy with no continuing relationship to the row it came from. A warehouse row assembled by last night's ETL has no foreign key back to the deleted record, and the ETL that would have omitted it does not re-run over history. So the deletion has to become an event that each derived dataset consumes, and every new derived dataset is a new place deletion has to be implemented deliberately.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team stores every user event in an append-only log kept for two years for analytics, and promises deletion in 30 days. They cannot delete from the log — it is immutable and that is the point. What are their real options?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not "delete from the log". Either keep no identifier in it — write a pseudonymous subject id and hold the mapping from real identity to that id in a mutable store, so deleting the mapping makes the log rows unattributable, which is the standard design and depends on the mapping genuinely being the only link; or encrypt the identifying fields per subject and destroy the key, which is crypto-shredding applied at field granularity inside an immutable file; or compact the log, which means it was never really immutable and now has a rewrite path with its own correctness problems. The general shape: when the store cannot forget, move the thing that must be forgotten into a store that can, and leave only a reference behind.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a promise about the whole system is only as good as its longest-lived component, and the component that decides it is usually one nobody counted. Name instances outside data deletion.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A release's rollback window bounded by the oldest client version still in the field. A certificate rotation bounded by the pinned copy in a mobile app nobody updates. An API's deprecation date bounded by the one batch integration that runs quarterly. A schema migration's compatibility window bounded by the longest-retained message in a queue. A building's fire rating bounded by its worst door. In every case the reflex is to average or to survey the common case, and in every case the answer is a maximum over a set whose membership you have to go and establish.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items.
