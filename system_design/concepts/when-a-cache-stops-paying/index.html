Tier 9 · Cost, capacity, economics Cache economics explained well nowhere else

The hit rate went from 69% to 93% and the bill went up by $194 a month

A cache pays when its hit rate clears a break-even set by the ratio of two costs — and that break-even rises as you make the cache bigger. Hit rate grows like a logarithm, rent grows like a straight line, so the size that maximises savings sits an order of magnitude below the size that maximises hit rate. Past a certain point a 94.7% hit rate saves exactly nothing.

Read first — cache-evictionback-of-envelope

01

The dashboard improved and the invoice got worse

A product catalogue. 600,000 items, and traffic shaped the way real traffic is shaped: a few items carry most of the reads. A billion requests a month, of which 2% are writes. Each item renders to about 40 KB of joined JSON, and serving one from the origin costs about $0.40 per million requests — so the read path costs $392 a month with no cache at all.

Somebody adds a cache. Memory rents at roughly $20 per GB-month, so a 2 GB cache holds about 50,000 items and costs $39.90 a month. Hit rate: 69%. The bill drops from $392 to $161. Everybody is pleased and the ticket is closed.

Six months later a review notices the hit rate is “only 69%” and files the obvious follow-up: get it above 90%. It works. The cache grows to 16.4 GB, the hit rate climbs to 93%, and the graph on the wall goes up and to the right.

The bill goes from $161 to $355 a month.

Nothing is broken. Every number in the last two paragraphs is correct at the same time. The cache is more effective and it is also nearly worthless: the whole saving has gone from $231 a month down to $37. Grow it a little further — 18.5 GB, a 94.7% hit rate — and the bill is exactly $392, the same as having no cache at any size. The hit-rate panel, which is the only instrument most teams have pointed at a cache, reads 94.7% and rising the entire way down.

Every dollar figure on this page is an illustrative input, dated 2026-08, low confidence, and almost certainly wrong for your bill by some factor. Substitute your own — the arithmetic is built so that nothing here depends on the absolute levels. Every finding below is a break-even, a ratio, or a structural claim that survives every price being wrong.

02

Raise the hit rate — and watch the money go the other way

The belief this lesson is breaking is not exotic. It is the one everybody holds: a higher hit rate is a better cache, so the way to improve a cache is to make it bigger. The first half is true. The second half stops being true at a size you can compute, and the arithmetic that finds that size is arithmetic nobody does, because the hit rate is on a dashboard and the cache’s rent is on a different team’s invoice.

The rig below draws both halves at once. The teal curve is the hit rate a given cache size reaches. The crimson curve is the hit rate that size has to reach to cover its own cost. The shaded gap between them is the money, because the saving per read works out to (1 − writes) × (hit rate − break-even) × cost per origin request — so where the curves cross, the saving is zero, and the strip underneath says so in dollars.

Predict A 2.0 GB cache (50,000 of 600,000 items, $39.90/month of rent) reaches a 69% hit rate and cuts a $392 origin bill by $231. You grow it to 16.4 GB ($327.41/month of rent) and the hit rate rises to 93%. What happens to the monthly saving?
Origin requests avoided go from 69% of $392 to 93% of $392 — from $271 to $365, a gain of $94. Rent goes from $39.90 to $327.41, a rise of $288. Net: $271 − $40 = $231 becomes $365 − $327 = $37. The first option is the reasoning almost everyone applies and it is wrong because it prices only one of the two things that changed. The last option is the right shape one step too far: the saving does cross zero, but not until 18.5 GB and a 94.7% hit rate — so at 93% you have given away 84% of the saving and are still, just, in profit.
Show it without answering

Three things in that rig are worth naming out loud.

The two curves have different shapes, and that is the entire lesson. Hit rate rises like a logarithm of cache size. Rent rises like cache size. A logarithm and a straight line cross exactly once, and everything past the crossing is money spent to make a metric look better.

At a low origin cost ratio the cache pays only inside a window. Push the ratio to 2 — both tiers metered, so an origin request costs twice a cache operation — and the cache is a net loss below 8,514 entries and again above 145,828. In hit-rate terms it pays only between 52.8% and 80.8%, and nowhere else. The lower bound is the one that surprises people and it has nothing to do with memory: a cache that rarely hits still charges you for the lookup on every read.

The teal curve is the optimistic one. It is the ceiling — the request share of the most popular C keys, which no eviction policy can beat, from the same model that drives cache eviction. A real LRU lands well below it: raced on this workload, 22% to 38% below, depending on size. So every crossing in the rig happens at a smaller cache in reality than the picture shows. The argument only gets stronger with a real policy, which is why the optimistic curve is the safe one to draw.

03

The shelf beside your desk

You keep some books on a shelf beside your desk instead of walking to the library. Three things are true about that shelf and only the first one is obvious.

The shelf earns its keep on the books you actually open. A book you reach for twice a week saves you two walks a week. A book you opened once in 2019 saves you nothing and is occupying space you are paying for, because desk space near a person is the most expensive space in the building.

You glance at the shelf on every trip, including the trips where the book is not there. That glance is small and it is not free, and it happens on every lookup, not just the successful ones. So if the shelf almost never has what you want, you have not made your trips faster on average — you have added a step to every single one of them. There is a success rate below which the shelf is worse than not having a shelf, and the fact that the shelf is sometimes right does not save it.

A bigger shelf helps, and it helps less each time. The first metre holds the five books you use daily. The second metre holds books you use weekly. The tenth metre holds books you might open next year. Doubling the shelf never doubles the walks you avoid, because you shelved the useful books first — and the tenth metre costs exactly as much rent as the first one did.

Put those three together and there is a size beyond which the shelf costs more than the walks it saves, and it is nowhere near the size that holds every book you own.

There is a fourth that an individual never faces and a team always does: you cannot give the library back. However good the shelf gets, the day it burns down you still need the library to handle every request — so you cannot cancel the library’s lease and count that as a saving, which means most of what the shelf appears to save was never yours to bank.

04

The break-even hit rate, and the two curves that cross

Solve it symbolically first

Per read, a cache saves you the origin request it avoided and costs you three things: the cache operation itself, which you pay on hits and misses; the cache’s rent, spread over the reads it serves; and the invalidation the write traffic forces. Set saving equal to cost:

h × c_o = c_c + a + v ⟹ h* = (c_c + a + v) / c_o

c_o — cost of one origin request. c_c — cost of one cache operation. a — cache rent divided by reads. v — invalidation cost divided by reads. h* is the break-even hit rate: below it the cache loses money.

Look at what that expression is before putting numbers in it. It is a ratio of costs, so multiplying every price in your estate by any constant leaves it unchanged. A price list that is uniformly three times wrong moves nothing. What moves it is the relative price of the two tiers, and that is set by something structural rather than by a vendor: how each tier is billed.

Both tiers are…Origin ÷ cache cost per requestBreak-even before any rent
billed as hardware you own~800× — the ratio of a 20 ms join to a 25 µs GET0.13%
origin metered, cache owned~80×1.25%
both metered per operation~8× — both charge for a small key lookup12.5%

The third row is a real and common configuration, and it is the one nobody checks: a serverless cache in front of a serverless store has to clear a 12.5% hit rate before renting a single byte of memory. Two rows of the regime table at the end of this lesson sit on it, and one of them is a do-not-cache.

The identity that makes one number enough

Write the whole read-path bill out and one term cancels:

saving ÷ (the bill you would have paid without a cache) = h − h*

Exactly, not approximately. Every cost a cache adds — rent, invalidation, a second replica, a fan-out — appears only as a rise in h*, which is why one number summarises the whole decision.

Two corollaries fall straight out, and both are quantities people guess at.

A miss costs more than no cache. The bill with a cache is (1 + h* − h) times the bill without one, so at a zero hit rate you pay exactly 1 + h* — the excess is the break-even. A cache below its break-even is not “less good than hoped”, it is a line item that made the total larger. The 24 GB version of the catalogue cache above has h* = 122%, which means no hit rate whatsoever pays: it runs at 98% and costs 24.6% more than having no cache.

The break-even is not a property of your prices alone — it grows with the cache. Rent enters h* divided by reads, so doubling the memory doubles that term. This is why “our break-even hit rate is 0.13%” is a true and useless sentence: it is the break-even of an empty cache.

Why the hit rate is expensive to raise

Request popularity is Zipf-like, which is the finding cache eviction turns on and Breslau and colleagues established for web traffic in 1999. The request share of the C most popular keys out of N is (C^(1−s) − 1) / (N^(1−s) − 1), whose limit at s = 1 is ln C / ln N. What the model actually sums is the discrete version, H_C / H_N — the same shape offset by Euler’s γ, which is why that constant appears in the check below. So:

hit rate grows like the LOGARITHM of cache size; rent grows like cache size

Measured at s = 1 over 600,000 keys: each 10× of memory buys between 16.3 and 16.6 points of hit rate, four decades running, matching the analytic ln 10 ÷ (ln N + γ) = 16.6 to three decimals. Read the other way, each further +10 points of hit rate costs 4.00× the memory, then 4.01×, then 4.01×.

That factor of four is worth sitting with, because it is the shape of every “improve the hit rate” project. Points are additive; the memory that buys them is geometric. At the more realistic s = 0.9 it is not a clean logarithm — the multiple drifts from 3.6× down to 2.3× across the range — but the character is identical, and both are measured rather than asserted.

So there is a best size, and it is not the biggest one

Hit rate has no interior maximum: more memory always raises it, right up to holding the whole keyspace. Savings do have one, because the two curves grow at different rates. The condition is a marginal one and it is the most actionable line on this page:

cache the C-th key only while reads × (1 − w) × p_C × c_o > rent of one entry

p_C is the C-th most popular key’s share of requests. Rearranged: an entry earns its keep only if it is read more than rent-per-entry ÷ cost-per-origin-request times per period.

With the illustrative inputs, a 40 KB entry rents for $0.0008 a month and an origin request costs $0.0000004, so an entry must be read 2,000 times a month — once every 22 minutes — to be worth holding; 2,041 once the 2% write ratio is taken off it. That single ratio decides the size of your cache. Searched numerically and cross-checked against an algebraic inversion of the same condition, the answer for this workload is 49,879 entries: 2.00 GB, a 69.3% hit rate, and $231.07 a month saved out of a $392 bill.

The three costs that are missing from the arithmetic everybody does

The first one is already above: a miss pays twice, so there is a floor under the hit rate before memory is even mentioned. The other two are the ones that turn a positive answer negative.

Invalidation scales with writes, not reads. A write has to remove or replace the cached copy, and that has two prices. The obvious one is the message. The one that matters is this: if writes carry the same popularity distribution as reads, then for every key the write-to-read ratio is w/(1−w), and a read hits only if some read has repopulated the entry since the last write. For Poisson arrivals that probability is λ/(λ+μ), which collapses to a constant:

hit rate is multiplied by (1 − w), for every key at once

w is writes as a share of all traffic. Verified two ways: a seeded Poisson race on one key, and an LRU trace with writes deleting entries. Against a real LRU it is pessimistic by 4 to 12 points, because a write to a key that was not resident costs nothing.

The cache is a second system, and it can fail. Which means the fallback has to serve a cold cache, and a cold cache means full traffic at the origin — see cache stampede for what that arrival looks like. So the origin fleet cannot be sized for (1 − h) × peak; it has to be sized for peak. And that matters exactly in proportion to how your origin is billed: the metered part of an origin bill is saved the moment a request does not arrive, and the provisioned part is only saved by decommissioning something you have just agreed not to decommission. The bound is one line:

a cache saves money only while provisioned share of the origin bill < 1 − h* ÷ h

No price appears in it. How much of your bill may be capacity you cannot give back is decided entirely by how far above its break-even the cache is running.

At the savings-maximising size that bound is 85% — comfortable. At the size chasing a 95% hit rate it is 10%, because h* has climbed to 84%. Chasing hit rate does not only shrink the saving; it destroys the cache’s tolerance for the estate it is actually running in.

Regime one: no popularity structure. One row per active user, and each user reads their own — sessions, profiles, carts, settings. Popularity is flat over the active set, which makes the decision all-or-nothing, because the marginal entry is worth exactly the average entry. With these inputs the average entry gets 1,633 reads a month against the 2,041 it needs to clear its own rent — 80% of what it needs — so no size pays and the saving falls monotonically as the cache grows. Hold the whole keyspace, reach a 98% hit rate, and pay 24.6% more than having no cache. Cache eviction shows that flat access makes the eviction policy irrelevant; the same premise makes the cache a net loss. Bisecting for the boundary: below a Zipf exponent of about 0.07, no cache size pays at any price on this page.

Regime two: write-heavy against a metered cache tier. Half the traffic writes. Capacity could deliver a 56.2% hit rate; invalidation multiplies it by (1 − 0.5) down to 28.1%; and the break-even, which now carries an invalidation term as well as rent, is 29.5%. The hit rate sits 1.4 points below the break-even, so the cache costs 1.35% more than no cache at every size. The same prices with no write traffic would have banked 48.2% of the bill — the write ratio alone flips it. Regime three: an origin you cannot decommission. This one is not about the cache’s hit rate at all. Here is where the theoretical saving goes in an estate where 60% of the origin bill is provisioned capacity and the fallback must survive a total cache loss.

33.6% of the gross saving is banked. Against the theoretical saving that ignores the fallback requirement — $217.93 — the surviving fraction is 35.9%, and the cache you should buy is 2.8× smaller than the one the theoretical arithmetic chose, because a hit is worth less so fewer entries clear their rent. Turn the provisioned share to 100% and the best size is zero; run the 49,879-entry cache anyway and you are $40.40 a month worse off than with no cache, which is −17.5% of a promised $231.07.

The number I expected to be different

I expected invalidation traffic to be what kills caching for write-heavy workloads. It is not, and not by a little: against a cache tier you own, 500 million invalidations a month cost $0.25, while the hit-rate damage the same writes cause is $56.24 — a factor of 225. The write-side cost of a cache is almost entirely a read-side effect, and the term everyone names is the term that does not matter. It only becomes first-order when the cache tier bills per operation (where the ratio falls to 2.2) or when one invalidation fans out. That correction changed how this lesson is written: the fan-out table above exists because the naive framing had to be measured before it could be reported, and it turned out to be almost entirely wrong.

06

The trade-off: this arithmetic can talk you out of a cache you need

Everything above prices exactly one thing — dollars on a read path — and that is not why most caches exist.

A cache that loses money can be the right cache. It is bought for the p99, and latency is absent from every formula here. A 20 ms origin query behind a 0.3 ms cache at a 69% hit rate is a different product from the same query at 40%, and no amount of “the marginal entry does not clear its rent” changes that. The honest reading of this lesson is not make caches smaller; it is know which of the two things you are buying, and stop reporting one while paying for the other. The failure mode of taking this page too literally is a cost win that arrives as a latency regression two sprints later, blamed on something else.

The optimum is flatter than the arithmetic makes it look. Saving at 20,000 entries is $220 against $231 at the optimum — 95% of the benefit for 40% of the memory. A number computed to five digits invites precision that is not there, and the right operational move is usually to sit somewhere on the plateau with headroom, not on the peak.

Popularity is assumed stationary, and it is not. The Zipf curve here is fixed. Real catalogues have launches, seasons and news cycles, so the set of keys clearing the rent threshold moves, and a cache sized exactly to today’s curve is undersized next Tuesday. Eviction policy is what tracks that movement — which is the whole of cache eviction — and this lesson deliberately sizes against a ceiling that no policy achieves.

Two of the model’s terms are known to be wrong in known directions. The hit-rate ceiling is optimistic: a real LRU raced 22–38% below it, so every break-even arrives at a smaller cache than drawn. The (1 − w) invalidation factor is pessimistic by 4 to 12 points, because a write to a non-resident key costs nothing. They push in opposite directions and they do not cancel; both are measured in the test file rather than assumed away.

And the prices are the weakest thing on the page. They are declared inputs, dated, marked low confidence. What that costs is any claim of the form “a cache costs $X” — and it is why every finding here is a break-even, a ratio, or a shape. The one price-derived quantity that carries real weight is the origin-to-cache cost ratio, and that is structural: it is set by whether each tier is billed as hardware or per operation, which is an architectural choice rather than a number on an invoice.

One thing this lesson does not model at all: cost attribution. Every figure above assumes you can divide your bill by a request path. Most organisations cannot, and until they can, none of this arithmetic is executable — which is a prerequisite problem rather than a caching one.

07

In the wild

The logarithmic hit-rate curve is not folklore. Breslau, Cao, Fan, Phillips and Shenker showed in 1999 that web request popularity follows a Zipf-like distribution and derived the consequence that cache hit ratio grows logarithmically in cache size. That result is the reason the two curves in the rig must cross rather than merely converge, and it is why the finding survives being wrong about every price: a logarithm against a straight line has one crossing whatever the constants are.

Cold-cache capacity is a documented operational constraint, not a hypothetical. Nishtala and colleagues’ account of Facebook’s memcache tier describes bringing a cold cluster into service and the load that a cold cache puts on the persistent store — the reason a cluster is warmed from a running one rather than switched into traffic empty. A tier that cannot be turned on cold is a tier whose backing store is still sized for full traffic, which is finding three of this lesson stated as an operational procedure.

Managed caches increasingly offer both pricing shapes, and switching shape moves the break-even without changing a line of code. Several now sell the same cache either by the node-hour or by the request. The node-hour shape makes c_c negligible and puts the entire break-even in the rent term, so it rewards high traffic against a small keyspace. The per-request shape puts a floor under the hit rate — c_c / c_o, before any memory is rented — so it rewards a high hit rate and punishes a speculative cache. Same code, same hit rate, different answer, and the migration between them is usually described as a billing change.

Read/write cost asymmetry is built into the metered stores directly. DynamoDB bills reads and writes as separate capacity units, priced differently and covering different item sizes, which makes the invalidation term of this lesson explicit in a price list rather than hidden in a hit-rate panel: a cache in front of such a store is charged twice for every write — once by the store and once by the invalidation — and the second charge lands on a tier whose dashboards only report reads. Check the current rates against your own bill; the structural point is the separation, not the multiple.

And the practice that gets this right outside computing is warehouse slotting. A forward-pick face near the packing bench saves a walk into reserve; each face costs space; and slotting decisions are made on picks per slot per period against the cost of a slot — which is reads-per-entry × cost-of-a-miss against rent-per-entry, the same expression this lesson derives. Nobody in that industry proposes maximising the share of picks served from the forward area, because the answer is obviously 100% and obviously wrong.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The break-even hit rate is every cache-side cost per read divided by the cost of one origin request, so it is a ratio and survives the prices being wrong
  • The saving, as a share of the bill you would have paid without a cache, is exactly hit rate minus break-even hit rate
  • Below the break-even a cache is strictly worse than no cache, because a miss pays the cache lookup and the origin
  • Hit rate grows logarithmically in cache size while rent grows linearly, so the savings-maximising size is far below the hit-rate-maximising size
  • An entry earns its keep only if it is read more than rent-per-entry divided by cost-per-origin-request times per period
  • Writes cost a cache twice — an invalidation message, and a factor of (1 − write ratio) on the hit rate — and the second term is the larger one
  • A cache you must survive losing cannot let you decommission origin capacity, so the provisioned share of the origin bill is not available to save

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Your cache hit rate rises from 69% to 93% and the monthly bill rises too. Both numbers are correct. What is happening?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Buying hit rate costs geometric memory for additive points, because request popularity is Zipf-like: the request share of the top C keys grows like the logarithm of C. Measured on 600,000 keys at skew 0.9, going from a 69% ceiling to a 95% ceiling takes 8.2× the memory — 2.0 GB to 16.4 GB — which is $39.90 a month of rent becoming $327.41. The extra 24 points of hit rate avoid about $94 a month of origin requests. You paid $288 for $94. The saving falls from $231 a month to $37, and the hit-rate panel, which is the only instrument most teams have pointed at this, moved in the direction everybody wanted.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A cache runs at a 3% hit rate. Is it merely useless, or is it worse than that, and by how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Worse, and by an amount you can write down. Every read goes through the cache to find out whether the value is there, so a miss pays the cache lookup and then the origin as well. The bill with a cache is (1 + h* − h) times the bill without one, where h* is the break-even hit rate — so at h = 0 you pay exactly 1 + h*, and the excess is the break-even itself. With the illustrative inputs on this page a 24 GB cache holding the whole catalogue has h* = 122%, so at any hit rate it loses: measured, 24.6% more than having no cache at all, at a 98% hit rate.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Write traffic damages a cache in two ways. Which one actually decides anything, and by what factor?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The one nobody counts. The invalidation messages scale with writes and, against a cache tier you own, are almost free — measured at $0.25 a month for 500 million invalidations. The hit-rate multiplier is the real term: if writes carry the same popularity as reads, every key's hit rate is multiplied by (1 − w), which at w = 0.5 costs $56.24 a month of avoided origin requests. That is 225× the message cost. The messages only become a first-order term when the cache tier bills per operation, or when one invalidation fans out to every node or PoP — at a fan-out of 20 against a metered tier the cache stops paying above a 13% write ratio instead of a 92% one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    You compute that a cache will cut origin load by 70%, so you plan to shrink the origin fleet by 70%. What stops you, and how much of the saving is left?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The cache is a second system and it can fail, so the fallback has to serve a cold cache — which is the full traffic. The fleet cannot shrink at all, so the provisioned share of the origin bill is not available to save; only the metered share is. The bound is ps < 1 − h* ÷ h: at a 69% hit rate against a 10% break-even a cache tolerates a bill that is up to 85% provisioned, and at a 93% hit rate against an 84% break-even it tolerates only 10%. At a fully provisioned origin the realised saving is negative at every cache size, and at the size the theoretical arithmetic chose you are $40.40 a month worse off than with no cache — −17.5% of the saving you were promised.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    An index is a cache of an ordering. Apply the same four terms to "should I add this index?" and say what each one becomes.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The mapping is exact. Cost per origin request becomes the cost of the scan the index avoids. Cost per cache request becomes the index probe, paid by every query the planner points at the index whether or not it is selective — so a non-selective index is the low-hit-rate cache: probe plus heap fetch, strictly worse than the scan alone. Rent becomes the index's pages, and it is linear in rows while the queries it helps are not. Invalidation becomes write amplification: every insert and every update of an indexed column maintains it, which is why the write-heavy verdict transfers unchanged. And the fallback is the same too — you cannot drop the ability to sequentially scan, so the base table's capacity is not decommissioned by adding an index. The break-even is selectivity × scan cost > probe cost + pages ÷ queries, which is the same expression with different nouns.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a shortcut consulted on every attempt but useful on only some of them pays only if its success rate exceeds its own cost divided by the cost of the thing it avoids — and buying a higher success rate usually costs geometrically for additive gains. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Warehouse slotting is the closest one, and the industry solved it the same way: a "forward pick" face near the packing bench saves a long walk into reserve storage, each face costs shelf space that could hold something else, and a slow-moving line does not earn its face — so slotting is done by picks-per-slot per period against the cost of a slot, which is exactly "an entry must be read more than rent ÷ cost-of-a-miss times per period". Others: the express checkout lane, which every shopper glances at (cost paid on every attempt) and only some qualify for; a triage nurse, who is consulted by every arrival and only reroutes some, so a triage step whose reroute rate is too low adds delay to everyone; medical screening, where the test is run on everybody and the useful output is confined to the prevalence, which is why screening a low-prevalence population fails a cost-benefit test that the identical test passes in a high-prevalence one; and carrying an umbrella, paid every day and used on some. In every case the two failure modes are the same two: the success rate is too low for the shortcut to clear its own cost, or you spent so much raising the success rate that the shortcut costs more than the thing it was avoiding.
    Did you have that?
