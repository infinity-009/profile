Tier 1 · The network The layers, briefly explained well nowhere else

A 20-byte voice sample ships with 54 bytes of headers, and the layer that breaks a tunnel is never the one being debugged

Seven layers exist so each one can be reasoned about, and changed, without touching the others — but almost every real decision only touches two or three of them. This lesson puts a number on the two places that separation actually bites: header overhead, which is 73% of a 20-byte VoIP sample and under 1% of an 8 kB file, and path MTU, where adding a tunnel silently eats bytes at layer 2 or 3 while every symptom shows up as a mysterious timeout at layer 7 — three layers and, often, a different team away from the change that caused it.

Read first — byte-budgets

01

A tunnel that adds isolation, and a customer whose uploads just started failing

You put a service behind an overlay network for tenant isolation — a one-line change in the deployment config, reviewed and approved without controversy. Two weeks later: one customer’s large uploads fail intermittently. Your load balancer shows healthy backends. No errors appear in any application log. The customer’s smaller requests work perfectly.

Nothing in the change that caused this touched anything an application developer would call “networking.” It touched a number three layers below the one anyone was looking at, and the failure surfaced at a layer three above where the change happened. This lesson is about why that gap exists, and how to close it before it costs a week.

02

Assume the layers are equally relevant, and measure how much of a decision each one actually holds

The seven-layer model gets taught as a stack you climb through for every packet — physical, link, network, transport, session, presentation, application — and the instinct that follows is that a competent engineer should be equally fluent in reasoning at all seven. That instinct mismeasures the real skill. Almost every design decision depends on facts at two or three layers, and the rest are fixed plumbing you pay for without examining. The skill is knowing which two or three a given decision touches — because when you get that wrong, you debug at the wrong layer for a very long time.

Two concrete places this bites, both worth a number:

Predict A VoIP call sends a 20-byte audio sample over RTP/UDP/IPv4 every packet. What fraction of the bytes actually on the wire are protocol headers, not audio?
Ethernet (14) + IPv4 (20) + UDP (8) + RTP (12) = 54 bytes of fixed header wrapped around 20 bytes of audio — 54 of 74 total bytes, 73%. This is not a contrived worst case; it's the textbook reason VoIP historically needed header-compression schemes (RFC 2508, RFC 3095) on constrained links, and the same shape — fixed overhead dominating a small, frequent payload — repeats at every layer this lesson touches.
Show it without answering
03

A mezzanine floor, and a fire exit nobody recalculated

An architect adds a mezzanine level to a building — more usable floor space, a purely structural decision, signed off by a structural engineer who confirmed the load-bearing capacity was sound.

Nobody recalculated egress: how many people the existing stairwells can safely evacuate within the fire code’s time limit, now that the building holds more occupants. Structural capacity and evacuation capacity are different “layers” of a building’s design, usually owned by different specialists, and the renovation passed its own inspection cleanly. The failure surfaces months later, in an unrelated fire-safety audit, as a violation that looks disconnected from a floor added long before.

That is the MTU bug, outside computing. A change made cleanly at one layer, correct on its own terms, silently invalidates an assumption several layers away — and the two facts are far enough apart, in both time and which expert is looking, that nobody connects them until an audit (or an outage) forces the connection.

04

The mechanism — a fixed cost per layer, and a budget that shrinks without anyone touching it

Every layer below your payload adds a header, and headers are fixed regardless of payload size. Ethernet: 14 bytes. IPv4: 20. IPv6: 40 — exactly 20 more, always. TCP: 20. UDP: 8. TLS 1.3, per record: 22 (a 5-byte header, a 16-byte authentication tag, and a 1-byte inner content type — RFC 8446 §5.2). A fixed cost is a larger fraction of a smaller payload, which is why the ratio in slot 2 looks alarming at 20 bytes and irrelevant by a few kilobytes — the same “framing beats ratio” shape transport-compression showed for compressors, here showing up in the wire protocol itself.

A tunnel adds a header UNDER the layer everyone already accounts for, which is what makes it invisible to an application-layer debugging session. VXLAN’s well-known “set your MTU to 1450” is not a rounded safety margin — it is the tunnel’s own header stack, summed exactly:

VXLAN header        8 bytes
outer UDP header     8 bytes
outer IPv4 header   20 bytes
outer Ethernet hdr  14 bytes
                    ────────
                    50 bytes  →  1500 − 50 = 1450

And the failure mode when nobody adjusts for that shrinkage has its own mechanism. Path MTU discovery (PMTUD) relies on an oversized, “don’t fragment” packet triggering an ICMP “fragmentation needed” message back to the sender, which then shrinks future packets. If any firewall on the path filters that ICMP message — common, well-intentioned security hardening — the sender never hears about it. TCP’s retransmit timer fires, resends the packet at the identical size, and it is dropped identically, forever. No error surfaces at any layer above the drop, because nothing was told there was a problem to report.

06

The trade-off — you cannot skip the layers, only choose which ones cost you attention

None of this argues for collapsing the stack, or for memorizing every header field. Two honest limits on what this lesson’s numbers can tell you:

Real headers are often larger than the ones used here. TCP with common options (timestamps, SACK) runs closer to 32–40 bytes than the bare 20 used in these tables; IPv6 extension headers can add more on top of the fixed 40. The shape of every result here — small payloads pay a disproportionate tax, tunnels subtract from the same fixed budget — holds regardless; the exact percentages shift a little with your actual header set. Measure your own traffic if the ratio itself is what you’re optimizing.

Knowing which layer a fact lives at doesn’t remove the cost of a bad boundary between two teams. The mezzanine-and-egress story in slot 3 isn’t fixed by a better org chart — structural and safety concerns genuinely benefit from separate expertise. What layering buys you is a checklist of what to reconcile when either side changes: adding a tunnel should trigger “did we recompute MTU,” the same way adding a floor should trigger “did we recompute egress.” The stack’s value is not that it lets you ignore six layers — it’s that it tells you exactly which one to go check when you change the seventh.

And the fix for the MTU failure mode is at the layer that broke, not the layer that noticed. TCP MSS clamping at the tunnel’s own edge fixes the problem for every connection through it, permanently, without depending on any downstream firewall’s ICMP policy ever being right. Chasing it by asking a customer’s ISP to stop filtering ICMP is asking someone else to fix a problem you introduced — technically possible, operationally a dead end.

07

In the wild

VoIP’s header-overhead problem is old enough to have its own dedicated fix. Casner and Jacobson’s Compressing IP/UDP/RTP Headers for Low-Speed Serial Links (RFC 2508, 1999) exists entirely because the 54-byte overhead this lesson computes was, on the low-bandwidth links common at the time, a real fraction of total link capacity — and the Robust Header Compression successor (RFC 3095, 2001) is still deployed on cellular networks today for the same reason, on much faster links, because per-packet overhead never stopped being real, it just stopped being the bottleneck everywhere except where radio spectrum is the scarce resource.

VXLAN’s exact overhead is specified, not folklore. Mahalingam et al., VXLAN: A Framework for Overlaying Virtualized Layer 2 Networks over Layer 3 Networks (RFC 7348, 2014), defines the 8-byte VXLAN header sitting inside a UDP/IP/Ethernet envelope — the same 50-byte sum this lesson derives from first principles, which is why every major cloud and virtualization vendor’s networking documentation quotes an MTU adjustment in that exact neighborhood without needing to cite a source.

The ICMP-filtering failure mode has a name and an RFC of its own, because it kept happening. Lahey, Enhancing TCP’s Loss Recovery Using Limited Transmit aside, the more directly relevant document is Mathis and Heffner’s Packetization Layer Path MTU Discovery (RFC 4821, 2007), written specifically because firewalls filtering ICMP made classic PMTUD (RFC 1191) unreliable enough in practice that TCP needed a way to discover path MTU using probes it could send and interpret without depending on ICMP at all — a protocol-layer fix for an operational-layer problem, itself a small instance of this lesson’s argument.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The stack is layered so each layer can change without the others noticing — but any one design decision usually only depends on facts at two or three of the seven
  • Every layer below your payload adds a fixed header, and a fixed cost is a LARGER share of a smaller payload — a 20-byte VoIP sample carries 54 bytes of RTP/UDP/IPv4 header, 73% of the wire
  • The same fixed overhead falls under 1% once the payload passes about 8 kB, so the header-overhead question only matters for small, frequent messages, not bulk transfers
  • IPv6 costs exactly 20 bytes more than IPv4 at every payload size, because the header sizes differ by exactly that much and nothing else in the stack changes
  • A tunnel (GRE, VXLAN, IPsec) adds its own header UNDER the IP layer everyone already accounts for, so it shrinks the usable MTU without changing any number an application ever looks at
  • VXLAN's well-known "lower your MTU by 50" rule is exactly its header stack — 8 (VXLAN) + 8 (outer UDP) + 20 (outer IPv4) + 14 (outer Ethernet) — not a rule of thumb, an exact sum
  • Path MTU discovery relies on an ICMP message the sender needs to hear; if a firewall filters that message, an oversized packet is dropped identically on every retry, forever, with no error visible to the application
  • The failure this produces — TCP hangs, HTTP requests time out, nothing in the app logs — is diagnosed at layer 7 while its cause sits at layer 2 or 3, which is the entire argument for knowing which layer a fact lives at

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Someone argues your microservices architecture needs a "session layer" because two services need to coordinate multi-step state. Do you need to design a new protocol layer?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — you need a fact to live somewhere, and the seven-layer model is a filing system for facts, not a checklist of things you must build. The OSI session layer (layer 5) is real in theory and almost entirely unused in practice; TCP already gives you an ordered byte stream, TLS already gives you a secure channel, and multi-step application state is exactly what your application layer (HTTP, gRPC, whatever) is for. "We need a session layer" nearly always means "we need to put this state somewhere explicit" — a database row, a signed token, a stateful service behind a sticky route — which is an application-layer decision wearing a networking-layer's name. The lesson of the stack is not "use every layer," it's "know which layer a given fact belongs at," and most facts belong two or three layers up from where people first reach for.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Your team ships a UDP-based protocol for tiny IoT telemetry — one 20-byte reading per message. A reviewer says "that's inefficient, look at the overhead." Are they right?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Numerically yes, and it may not matter. A 20-byte payload over UDP/IPv4/Ethernet carries 42 bytes of header — 68% overhead. But overhead is a fraction of bytes moved, and bytes are often not the binding constraint for telemetry: battery life is dominated by radio-on time per transmission (largely fixed, regardless of payload size, on many radios), and link cost is often billed per-message on cellular IoT plans, not per-byte. The reviewer is right about the number and possibly wrong about which number is the one that costs money — check the actual constraint (battery curve, per-message billing, spectrum/airtime budget) before optimizing a ratio that might not be on the critical path at all.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A cloud provider's docs say "set your VXLAN-encapsulated instances' MTU to 1450." Where does 1450 come from, and what breaks if you leave it at 1500?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It's an exact sum, not a rounded guideline: VXLAN header (8) + outer UDP header (8) + outer IPv4 header (20) + outer Ethernet header (14) = 50 bytes, and 1500 − 50 = 1450. Leave the inner MTU at 1500 and any packet that actually uses the full frame becomes 1550 bytes on the wire — over the physical network's real 1500-byte limit — and gets fragmented or dropped depending on whether fragmentation is allowed and whether path MTU discovery's ICMP signal reaches the sender. The number is not a safety margin; it is the tunnel's own header cost, computed once and then quoted everywhere.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Users behind one specific corporate VPN report that small requests work fine but large file uploads hang forever. Your load balancer shows healthy backends and no errors. Where do you look?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not at layer 7, and not at your load balancer — look at path MTU discovery between the VPN gateway and your edge. A VPN tunnel (IPsec or similar) adds its own header underneath IP, shrinking the usable MTU below 1500 the same way VXLAN does; a small request fits inside the reduced MTU and works, while a large upload eventually sends a full-size, "don't fragment" packet that doesn't fit. If a firewall anywhere on that specific corporate network filters the ICMP "fragmentation needed" message, path MTU discovery gets no signal, the oversized packet is dropped identically on every retransmission, and the connection simply hangs — no error, because nothing was ever told there was a problem. This is diagnosed with a packet capture showing repeated identical-size retransmits and no incoming ICMP, not with anything your application or load balancer logs will show, which is why it survives so many rounds of "it's not us."
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Why does DNS still use UDP with a 512-byte legacy size limit, when TCP would remove the size limit entirely?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the layer a decision is made at should match the layer the cost is paid at, and for the overwhelming majority of DNS queries, TCP's cost (a handshake, connection state, more round trips) is paid on every query to solve a problem (message size) that essentially never occurs — most answers fit comfortably under even the legacy 512-byte figure. This lesson's numbers show the mechanism: a small UDP/IPv4 query already carries 56–68% overhead before you add TCP's own handshake and per-connection state. DNS's actual answer is neither "always UDP" nor "always TCP" — it's EDNS0 (RFC 6891), which raises the practical UDP size ceiling for the common case and falls back to TCP only when a response is legitimately large (DNSSEC, many records) — the fix lives at the layer where the actual constraint (occasional large responses) occurs, not one layer up applied to every query.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    Your team migrates a service to run inside an overlay network (VXLAN) for multi-tenant isolation. Two weeks later, one customer's large file uploads start failing intermittently, but only for customers on a specific ISP, and only for uploads over roughly 1.4 MB. Diagnose it, and say what you'd change.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Two separate facts have to both be true for this exact symptom, and finding one without the other explains a different bug.

    First: the overlay network shrank your usable MTU without anyone touching an application setting. VXLAN's encapsulation costs 50 bytes — 8 (VXLAN) + 8 (outer UDP) + 20 (outer IPv4) + 14 (outer Ethernet) — pushing the real usable inner MTU from 1500 down to 1450 unless every hop involved was explicitly reconfigured for it. Nothing about "we added an overlay network for isolation" reads, to most engineers, as "we changed a byte limit," which is exactly why this class of bug survives code review: the change that caused it and the layer where it manifests are three hops apart.

    Second: the specific ISP filters the ICMP message path MTU discovery needs. This is why it's ISP-specific rather than universal — some networks along the path allow "fragmentation needed" ICMP through and PMTUD works, quietly costing one extra round trip per connection while the sender learns the smaller size; others filter it (common security-hardening advice, misapplied), and for exactly those customers, an oversized "don't fragment" packet is dropped on every identical retry, forever, with the connection hanging and no error surfacing anywhere in your stack.

    "Only over roughly 1.4 MB" is a red herring in the wrong direction — the trigger isn't total file size, it's whether any single **packet** in the transfer reaches the new, lower MTU, which for a bulk upload is nearly guaranteed once the TCP window opens up past the first few segments; small uploads simply never send a full-size packet and never hit the boundary.

    What to check: a packet capture on an affected connection, looking for identical-size retransmits of a packet at or near 1500 bytes with no inbound ICMP type-3-code-4 in response. What to change: don't chase the ICMP filtering (you don't control that ISP's firewall) — fix the MTU at the layer that actually changed it. Either clamp the inner MTU explicitly to 1450 (or the correct value for whatever encapsulation you're running) at your overlay's edge, or enable TCP MSS clamping on the tunnel endpoints so TCP itself negotiates a segment size that already accounts for the tunnel overhead, removing the dependency on PMTUD (and therefore on that ISP's ICMP policy) working at all.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system is deliberately layered so each level can be reasoned about independently, but a change made at one level can silently violate an assumption a level several steps away depends on, and the failure then surfaces far from its cause, in a form that looks unrelated. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A building's structural renovation and its fire-safety rating. An architect adds a mezzanine floor (a structural, load-bearing decision) without recalculating egress — how many people can evacuate through existing stairwells in the code-mandated time. The two facts live at genuinely different "layers" of the building's design (load-bearing capacity vs. occupant-safety planning) and are usually owned by different people. The renovation passes structural inspection cleanly; the failure only shows up later, in a fire-safety audit, as "this building now has too many occupants for its exits" — a fact that reads, to anyone not already looking for it, as completely unconnected to a floor added months earlier. Exactly the shape of the MTU bug: the change and its consequence are separated by both time and which "layer" of expertise is looking.

    Other instances: a company reorganizing reporting lines (an org-chart, "structural" layer change) that silently breaks an informal escalation path someone relied on for incident response (an "operational," several-layers-up assumption) — nobody updates the incident runbook because nobody thought of the reorg as touching it. And changing a recipe's oven temperature for a new oven (an "equipment layer" fix) that silently invalidates a baking time written for the old one (a "procedure layer" fact that assumed the old temperature), producing a burnt result that reads as "I followed the recipe" rather than "two facts that used to travel together got separated."
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
