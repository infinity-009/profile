Tier 7 · Security, privacy, compliance ReBAC and Zanzibar explained well nowhere else

Every request asks a graph reachability question first, and nesting groups four deep put four round trips in front of all of them

"Can this user read this document?" is not a lookup. The answer is not stored anywhere — what is stored is relations, and the answer is whether a path exists. So authorization is a graph traversal on the hot path of every request, its latency is the nesting depth rather than the number of groups, and the obvious fix creates a staleness window with a failure mode ordinary caches do not have.

Read first — replication-lagtail-at-scale

01

Two teams became teams-of-teams, and every request got four milliseconds slower

The sharing model was simple and worked: a document belongs to a folder, a folder grants access to a group, and a user is a member of groups. can(user, read, doc) was a join, it took about 2 ms, and nobody thought about it again.

Then customers started nesting. Engineering contains Platform, which contains Storage, which contains the on-call rotation. Access granted to Engineering has to reach a member of the rotation, so the check stopped being a join and became a walk: find the user’s groups, find their parent groups, and so on until either the folder’s group appears or the graph runs out.

Four levels of nesting is four sequential round trips, because you cannot ask about level three until level two tells you which groups to ask about. At 2 ms a hop that is 8 ms, on every request in the product, before a single line of application code runs. The p99 was much worse than 8 ms, because a level with a dozen groups in it is a dozen concurrent lookups and a batch finishes when its slowest member finishes.

So they precomputed it: for every user, every group they are transitively in. Checks went back to one lookup. And six weeks later a customer reported that someone removed from a group had read a document created after they were removed.

That second incident is not the same kind of bug as the first, and the difference is the point of this lesson.

02

One slider moves the latency and the other does not

Predict A check walks 4 levels of nesting. You raise the groups checked at each level from 1 to 12, taking lookups per check from 4 to 22,620. What happens to the check's mean latency, assuming each level is batched into one round trip?
Latency is depth × RTT, and depth did not change. Every group at a level is known before that level starts, so the whole level is one batched round trip whether it holds 1 group or 12. The 22,620 is throughput, capacity and cost — real, and not what the user is waiting for. What does get worse is the p99, because a batch finishes when its slowest member does.
Show it without answering

Three things to take from it.

Depth is the only thing on the latency axis. Every product decision that deepens the hierarchy — sub-teams, folder trees, tenant-of-tenant, roles that inherit roles — spends the request budget of every user, including users with no nesting at all.

Fanout is not free, it is just not latency. 22,620 lookups per check at 10,000 checks a second is 226 million lookups a second, which is a fleet. And it is a tail-at-scale problem the moment you care about p99 rather than the mean.

The teal bar is flat, and that is what makes precomputation tempting. It stays 2 ms at depth 8 and fanout 12. Everything hard about this topic is on the write side of that bar.

03

The intuition: a phone chain you cannot skip ahead in

You need to know whether Bob may enter the members’ lounge. You do not have a list of people allowed in the lounge — you have a rule that the Sailing Club may use it, and a set of memberships.

So you phone Bob’s clubs. He is in three: Chess, Cycling, Junior Sailing. None of them is the Sailing Club, but Junior Sailing might be affiliated with it. You cannot know which clubs to phone next until this call ends — that is what the call is for.

Two facts fall out immediately.

Phoning thirty clubs at once costs one call’s worth of waiting. You know all thirty names, so you dial them together. Breadth is cheap in time and expensive in phone bills.

Phoning four levels deep costs four calls’ worth of waiting, however few clubs are at each level. Nothing about batching helps, because the next number is the previous answer.

So you build a list: everyone who may use the lounge, worked out in advance. One glance, no calls. And the moment you write that list down, you own a copy of the truth, and the truth moves.

Now the part that is not about staleness in the ordinary sense. You strike Bob off the list at noon. At 12:00:30 someone books the lounge for the Sailing Club’s private dinner. At 12:01 the doorman is holding a copy of the list printed at 11:59.

Bob gets into a dinner that did not exist while he was still allowed in. There is no moment in history at which his admission was correct — the doorman combined a new booking with an old list, and that combination never existed. A slightly fresher list makes this rarer. It does not make it wrong-free.

An authorization answer is derived, not stored. Deriving it lazily costs a round trip per level of nesting; deriving it eagerly costs an index that must be maintained and a window in which it disagrees. The window is not merely stale — it can produce answers that no consistent version of the data would ever have produced.

04

The mechanism: relation tuples, reachability, and one ordering hazard

Store relations, not answers. Zanzibar’s unit is a relation tuple — roughly object#relation@subject, as in doc:11#viewer@group:4#member — and a check is a reachability query over them.

lookups per check = Σi=1..d fi  ·  latency = d × RTT

d is nesting depth, f groups per level. The two formulas have different variables in them, which is the entire design space: work is exponential in depth and fanout, waiting is linear in depth alone. Both asserted in authz.test.mjs.

precomputed closure = direct memberships × nesting depth  ·  check = 1 × RTT

Each direct membership implies one edge per level above it. This is the idea behind Zanzibar’s Leopard index, which exists specifically because deeply nested groups made the recursive walk too slow.

one membership change rewrites (members of the group) × depth edges

More if the group has subgroups beneath it. The index is a derived dataset, so every write to the relation graph is a fan-out write to the index.

And the hazard that is not about freshness. A stale price is a wrong number. A stale access list combined with new content is a different kind of failure: it answers a question that no version of the data was ever able to answer.

Zanzibar calls this the new-enemy problem and gives it two forms: ignoring the order of ACL updates, and applying an old ACL to new content. The fix is not a shorter TTL, it is a snapshot token — the client hands back an opaque timestamp of the state it last observed (a zookie), and the check is evaluated at or after that point. A replica that has caught up answers locally; only a lagging one waits.

The scale it is actually done at. Zanzibar reports more than 2 trillion relation tuples, over 10 million client checks per second, a 95th-percentile latency under 10 ms, and 99.999% availability across three years, serving Calendar, Cloud, Drive, Maps, Photos and YouTube. Two numbers in that sentence are worth holding together: the p95 budget is 10 ms, and the table above spends all of it on five levels of nesting. That is why the Leopard index exists.

What to set.

  • Cap nesting depth explicitly, as a product decision. It is the only variable on the latency axis, and it grows by itself as customers model their org charts. A limit of, say, 5 is a decision; no limit is also a decision, made by whoever files the deepest hierarchy.
  • Batch every level. A per-node round trip turns depth 5 at fanout 3 from 10 ms into 726 ms. This is the single biggest implementation mistake and it is invisible at test-data scale.
  • Precompute only the part that is deep. The closure of a shallow, wide graph buys ~nothing and costs a lot of maintenance. Leopard exists for deeply nested groups specifically.
  • Decide the consistency mode per call site, not globally. A thumbnail grid and a “publish to the world” button do not need the same guarantee, and the second one is where a 30 ms leader read is obviously worth paying.
  • Thread a snapshot token through your API before you need it. Retrofitting it means changing every read that feeds a check — which is most of them.
06

The trade-off: three costs, and only two of them are on the same axis

Deriving at read time buys correctness and sells latency, per level. Nothing is stale because nothing is stored, and every level of nesting is a round trip charged to every request. This is the right default until depth grows, and depth grows because of decisions made by customers rather than by you.

Deriving at write time buys a flat 1 × RTT and sells write amplification and freshness. 20 million edges to keep correct, 200 rewritten per membership change, and a derived dataset that can be behind. Note the asymmetry with a normal cache: you cannot lazily fill this on a miss, because “no path found” is indistinguishable from “not computed yet” — a denied check and a cache miss look identical, and guessing wrong in one direction is a security hole and in the other an outage.

The staleness window is not the same cost as the ordering hazard, and conflating them is how teams ship the bug. A window means an answer is out of date. The new-enemy problem means an answer is incoherent — assembled from two versions that never coexisted. Shrinking the window is a performance project; closing the hazard needs a version to evaluate against, which is a protocol change. If you take one thing from this lesson: when the correctness argument depends on a race being unlikely, there is no correctness argument.

What a snapshot token costs, honestly. It is opaque, so clients must store and return something they cannot interpret; it couples your read path to your check path forever; and it makes “check this on behalf of a user, offline, from a batch job” awkward, because a batch job has no last-observed version and must therefore choose between the leader and the risk. The alternative — full consistency everywhere — is a 30× latency increase on the most frequent operation in the system, which is why nobody does it.

And the thing that is not a trade-off: cap the depth. Both branches get cheaper as depth falls, and depth is the one variable here that is a product decision rather than an infrastructure one.

07

In the wild

Zanzibar (Pang et al., USENIX ATC 2019) is the reference, and worth reading for two sections in particular: the new-enemy problem and the reasoning that leads to a snapshot token rather than to stronger reads, and Leopard, the index built specifically because recursive traversal of deeply nested groups could not hold the latency budget. Its reported figures — 2 trillion+ tuples, 10M+ checks/s, sub-10 ms p95, five nines over three years — are the numbers to judge a design against, and it is built on Spanner, which is how it gets snapshot reads at a timestamp at all.

SpiceDB and OpenFGA are open-source implementations of that model, and both expose the consistency choice directly — SpiceDB’s ZedTokens and OpenFGA’s consistency parameter are the zookie by another name. If you want to see the trade-off as an API rather than as prose, read their consistency documentation: the options are the three rows of the table above, and the docs are explicit that the fast one is the wrong default for security-relevant reads.

AWS Cedar and Amazon Verified Permissions take the other branch — policy as a small, auditable, versioned artifact evaluated against attributes, rather than a large emergent relation graph. It is a genuinely different answer with a different failure mode: a Cedar policy is reviewable and a relation graph is not, while “who can see this document” is one query in Zanzibar and a search problem under policy evaluation. Real systems run both, and the boundary between them is one of the more interesting design decisions in this tier.

Google Drive’s sharing behaviour is this lesson’s user-visible surface. Share a folder with a group containing a group, and the propagation delay you occasionally notice is the index catching up. That it is usually instant and occasionally not is the honest signature of a precomputed closure, and it is a reasonable thing to ship — for documents. The same design under a “who can approve a payment” check would not be.

Model and numbers — every figure computed by src/sim/authz.mjs and asserted in src/sim/authz.test.mjs (7 tests): the f-ary tree lookup count against hand enumeration, the claim that latency depends on depth and not on fanout, the 22,620-lookups-at-8 ms case, the 12.4× penalty for failing to batch a level, and the new-enemy window closing only when the gap exceeds the lag.

Pang et al., “Zanzibar: Google’s Consistent, Global Authorization System”, USENIX ATC 2019 — relation tuples, the new-enemy problem, zookies, the Leopard index, and the reported scale and latency figures quoted here. usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/pang

Corbett et al., “Spanner: Google’s Globally-Distributed Database”, OSDI 2012 — the snapshot reads at a timestamp that make a zookie mean anything.

SpiceDB documentation, “Consistency” — ZedTokens and the minimize-latency / at-least-as-fresh / full-consistency options, an implementation of the three rows of this lesson’s consistency table.

OpenFGA documentation — a CNCF project implementing the Zanzibar model, including its relationship-tuple format and consistency options.

AWS Cedar / Amazon Verified Permissions documentation — the policy-based alternative, for the comparison in slot 7.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • An authorization answer is not stored; relations are stored, and a check is a graph reachability query on the hot path of every request
  • Check latency is the nesting depth in sequential round trips, because you cannot query level n+1 until level n has answered
  • Fanout is work rather than latency — a level batches into one round trip — but it becomes a tail problem, because a batch finishes when its slowest member does
  • Precomputing the transitive closure turns depth d into one lookup, at the cost of an index whose size is the closure and which one membership change rewrites in bulk
  • The new-enemy problem is applying an old access list to new content, which is a causality violation rather than a freshness complaint
  • A snapshot token pins the check to a version the client has already observed, buying correctness at replica latency instead of leader latency
  • Zanzibar reports more than 2 trillion relation tuples and over 10 million checks a second at a 95th-percentile latency under 10 ms, which is the budget a real design is held to

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why is a permission check a traversal rather than a lookup? Answer in terms of what is stored.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because what is stored is relations — user 7 is a member of group 4, group 4 is a member of group 2, group 2 is an editor of folder 9, document 11 lives in folder 9 — and the question "can 7 read 11" is whether a path exists between them. Nobody wrote down the answer, so it has to be derived, on every request, before any application work begins.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Nesting depth 4 with 12 groups per level is 22,620 lookups. Depth 4 with 1 group per level is 4 lookups. Why do both take the same 8 ms?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a level batches and depth does not. Every group at level 3 can be looked up in one round trip, since you already know all of them. You cannot begin level 4 until level 3 answers, because level 3's answer is which groups to ask about. Latency is depth × RTT; the 22,620 is throughput and cost, which matter to your capacity plan and not to the user waiting.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Given that batching makes fanout free on latency, why is a 22,620-lookup check still a latency problem in production?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because a batch finishes when its slowest member does, so a wide level samples the tail of the lookup latency distribution thousands of times. This is the tail at scale applied to authorization: at 22,620 lookups you are drawing deep into the p99.99, and the mean of the batch stops describing anything. Wide is free in the mean and expensive in the p99 — which is the percentile a permission check is judged on, because it gates the request.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A team precomputes the transitive closure so every check is one lookup. What did they buy, what did they take on, and what is the size of the new problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    They bought depth × RTT → 1 × RTT: 8 ms becomes 2 ms and stops growing with the org chart. They took on a derived dataset: 100k groups of 50 members nested four deep is 20 million expanded edges, and one member leaving one group rewrites 200 of them — more if that group has subgroups. And a derived dataset has a staleness window, which for an access list is not a cosmetic problem.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Bob is removed from a group at t=0. A document readable by that group is created at t=0.5 s. Bob's check at t=1 s hits a replica 3 s behind. What happens, and why is "shorten the replication lag" the wrong framing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The replica still has Bob in the group, so the check returns ALLOW for a document that did not exist when he was removed. He was never permitted to see it under any version of the access list — the check combined a new object with an old ACL, which no single snapshot would have produced. That is the new-enemy problem, a causality violation. Shortening the lag narrows the window without closing it, and correctness that holds only when a race is unlikely is not correctness. The fix is to evaluate the check at a snapshot at or after the version the client already observed.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    What does a snapshot token (Zanzibar's zookie) actually buy, compared with reading from the nearest replica or from the leader?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The latency of the replica with the correctness of the leader, relative to what this client has seen. The client hands back the timestamp of its last observed state; the check is evaluated at or after that timestamp, so a replica that has caught up answers locally and only a lagging one has to wait. Reading any replica is 1 ms with a multi-second window; going to the leader is 30 ms with none; the token is 1 ms with none. It is not free: someone has to thread an opaque token through the API and back, which is the part teams skip.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team replaces nested groups with a policy engine: attributes on the user and the resource, evaluated by a rule like "allow if user.department == resource.department". Does this escape the traversal problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Only if the attributes are already in hand. The engine evaluates a rule in microseconds, so the cost moves to fetching the attributes the rule mentions — and the moment a rule says resource.owner in user.managedTeams you are back to a reachability query, just expressed in a condition rather than in tuples. What the policy model genuinely changes is where the answer is defined: a rule is small, auditable and versioned, while a relation graph is large, emergent and impossible to review as a whole. The honest split is that policy engines are better at expressing intent and relationship graphs are better at answering "who can see this", and real systems run both — which is why AWS ships Cedar and Google ships Zanzibar rather than one of them.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a question whose answer is derived rather than stored can be answered lazily, at query time, or eagerly, at write time — and the eager version is always faster to read and always becomes a dataset that can be wrong. Name instances outside authorization.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A database's materialized view versus the query it replaces. A social feed's fan-out on write versus fan-out on read. A search engine's inverted index versus scanning documents. A build cache versus recompiling. Denormalised customer names copied onto orders. A newspaper's pre-set front page versus setting type on the news. In every case the eager form buys read latency, costs write amplification, and introduces a window in which the derived copy disagrees with the truth — and the interesting engineering is always in what the window is allowed to do, not in how small it is.
    Did you have that?
