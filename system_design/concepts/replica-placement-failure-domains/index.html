Tier 3 · Storage and state Replica placement

A naive cluster's odds of losing something to a 1% correlated failure climb from 63% at 200 nodes to 99.995% at 500 — replication factor and failure fraction both unchanged

Replication factor N says how many copies of your data exist. It says nothing about WHERE those copies sit — and where turns out to dominate durability as a cluster grows. Naive random placement draws every item's copyset from a pool of combinations that grows combinatorially with cluster size, so a correlated failure of just a few nodes gets more and more distinct ways to land squarely on one item's entire copy set — even though the replication factor and the FRACTION of the cluster failing never change. This lesson computes that rise, computes the bounded-copyset scheme that stops it, and separately computes the simpler question of whether one item's own copies share a single rack or AZ at all.

Read first — quorumsmulti-az-multi-region

01

The cluster got bigger, nothing else changed, and the odds of losing something went from a coin flip to a near-certainty

A storage team runs 200 nodes, replication factor 3, and plans for a 1% simultaneous correlated failure — a rack losing power, a bad kernel update rolling out cluster-wide. Under naive random placement, where every item’s 3-node copyset is drawn independently at random from the whole cluster, the computed probability that some item loses all 3 of its copies at once is 63.2%.

They grow to 500 nodes for more capacity. Same replication factor, 3. Same failure fraction, 1% — which is now 5 nodes instead of 3, but still exactly 1% of the cluster. The probability that some item loses all its copies at once: 99.995%.

Nothing about how much redundancy exists changed. Nothing about how much of the cluster can fail at once changed. The cluster just grew — and growing it made data loss more likely, not less.

02

Assume more nodes means more places to hide, so growth can only help

The instinct: a bigger cluster has more nodes to spread copies across, so any one item’s data should be harder to hit, not easier. That instinct is about a single item’s own copies — spread one item’s 3 replicas over 500 candidates instead of 200 and each specific replica is indeed harder to target directly. But naive placement isn’t drawing one item’s copyset once; it’s drawing a fresh one for every item, uniformly at random from an ever-growing pool of possible combinations — and a correlated failure only has to match any one of those many draws, not a specific one.

Predict A 20-node cluster keeps every item 3 ways (R=3). A correlated event takes down exactly 3 nodes at once — the smallest failure that could possibly destroy a full copyset. Naive placement draws each item's copyset from all 1,140 possible 3-node combinations; a copyset scheme instead reuses only 30 designated combinations for the same cluster. Roughly what's the probability SOME item is fully wiped out, under each?
With K=R=3, the chance one specific 3-node combination exactly matches the 3 failed nodes is 1 in C(20,3)=1,140. Naive treats all 1,140 combinations as live copysets, so across that many nearly-independent 1-in-1,140 draws the chance at least one matches converges to 1 − 1/e ≈ 63.2% — the same 'coupon collector' shape that shows up anywhere a large number of low-probability trials accumulate. The copyset scheme only ever reuses 30 designated combinations, so exposure shrinks to about 2.6% — roughly 24× lower, purely from bounding how many combinations exist, with the replication factor and the failure size completely unchanged.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — 350 nodes, replication factor 3, a 1% simultaneous failure (4 nodes) — naive placement draws from 7,084,700 possible copysets and loses something with probability 98.2%. The copyset scheme, reusing only 580 designated combinations on the identical cluster, puts that at 0.033% — about 3,000× lower — with neither the replication factor nor the failure fraction touched. Drag cluster size up and the naive curve keeps climbing toward 1; the scheme’s stays orders of magnitude below it across the same range.

03

Fixed carpools versus a fresh lottery every morning

A company organizes carpools: groups of 3 employees who ride to work together. If carpool groups are reassigned fresh every day, at random, from the whole workforce, then as the company hires more people, there are more and more distinct 3-person combinations that could exist behind any wheel on any given morning. If 3 specific colleagues happen to catch the same flu on the same day — a correlated event, nobody’s fault — the question is whether that exact trio was, on some day, somebody’s actual carpool. The more distinct combinations the random assignment could ever produce, the more likely it is that some combination the company generated over time was exactly those 3 — leaving that rider stranded.

If instead the company runs the same 30 fixed carpool pods, always, no matter how many employees join, a 3-person flu outbreak can only possibly match one of those 30 pods — never one of the millions of combinations the random scheme could theoretically produce. Growing the company doesn’t grow the number of pods that could go down together; it only adds people into the existing, bounded set of pods.

More nodes to spread copies across does help ONE item’s own replicas. It does nothing for the cluster-wide count of DISTINCT combinations a correlated failure could match — and naive placement lets that count grow without bound, while the copyset scheme deliberately does not.

04

The mechanism: a hypergeometric hit probability, and two very different copyset counts

Everything here is closed form — the same choose() binomial coefficient quorum.mjs already uses for R+W>N and shuffle-sharding.mjs reuses for its own combinatorics, aimed at a third question: not “do two sets intersect” and not “is one tenant’s whole shard inside the bad set,” but “how many distinct copysets exist, and how likely is a correlated failure to match one of them.”

copysetCount(naive) = C(clusterSize, R)

Every item’s copyset is an independent, uniformly random R-of-N draw, so the pool of distinct combinations that could ever be realized is every combination there is. Combinatorial in cluster size: C(20,3)=1,140, C(200,3)≈1.31 million, C(2000,3)≈1.33 billion.

copysetCount(scheme) = ceil(scatterWidth / (R−1)) × floor(clusterSize / R)

The Copysets-paper construction, simplified: fix a small scatter width S (how many OTHER nodes any one node is ever grouped with, regardless of cluster size), lay down ceil(S/(R−1)) permutations of the cluster, and chop each permutation into non-overlapping groups of R. Linear in cluster size — one more group every R nodes — not combinatorial, because S never grows with the cluster.

copysetHitProbability(N, R, K) = C(N−R, K−R) / C(N, K), K ≥ R

The exact hypergeometric probability that one SPECIFIC R-node copyset is entirely contained in a uniformly random K-of-N simultaneous failure — checked in the test file against a brute-force enumeration of every K-subset for small N, not just asserted.

dataLossProbability = 1 − (1 − p)^M, computed as −expm1(M · log1p(−p))

The standard “at least one of M roughly-independent rare events” shape, with M the scheme’s copysetCount and p the hit probability above. The expm1/log1p form matters, not just style: at a large cluster M is in the billions while p is correspondingly tiny, and computing 1 − p directly rounds to exactly 1 in double precision — silently collapsing the whole result to 0. This is a real bug this lesson’s own model hit and fixed, not a hypothetical one.

singleDomainLossProbability = domainFailureRate (all N copies, one domain) = domainFailureRate ^ N (N copies, N different domains)

A separate, simpler question about one item’s own N copies — independent domain failures, the same independence assumption multi-az-multi-region.mjs already leans on for AZ/region blast radius.

06

The trade-off: the copyset scheme is not a strictly better version of naive placement

Bounding copyset count costs rebuild parallelism after an ordinary, single-node failure. Naive random placement’s one real advantage is that a lost node’s data is scattered across a huge number of distinct peers — when it needs to be rebuilt, every one of those peers can push a piece of it in parallel, so recovery from a routine single failure is fast. The copyset scheme deliberately narrows how many distinct peers any given node ever shares data with (the bounded scatter width), which is exactly what caps the correlated-failure risk — and exactly what means fewer nodes are available to help rebuild after a single, uncorrelated failure. The scheme is trading routine-case rebuild speed for worst-case correlated-failure durability, not getting both for free.

It needs placement-aware assignment, not hash(item) % nodeCount. Naive placement can pick each item’s replicas independently, with no shared state. The copyset scheme requires every node to know which small set of designated groups it belongs to and to assign every new item’s copyset from that fixed set — real coordination overhead a naive scheme never has to pay.

Failure-domain spread and copyset-count bounding are independent purchases. A cluster can do one without the other: rack/AZ-aware placement (spread one item’s N copies across N different domains) protects against a single domain event without saying anything about how many DISTINCT combinations of nodes exist cluster-wide, and a bounded copyset scheme can still, by policy mistake, keep all of one item’s copies in a single rack. multi-az-multi-region.mdx already covers the deployment-level version of domain spread; this lesson’s contribution is the cluster-wide copyset-count question that operates independently of it.

Neither purchase removes the other’s failure mode. A cluster with perfect rack-awareness and an unbounded naive copyset pool still has data loss probability climbing toward certainty as it grows. A cluster with a tightly bounded copyset scheme that carelessly puts one item’s N copies in a single rack still loses that item outright the moment that rack goes down. Both levers have to be pulled.

07

In the wild

The Copysets paper’s own evaluation targeted real, deployed systems, not a toy model — Cidon et al. built and evaluated their scatter-width-bounded scheme against production storage systems rather than a synthetic benchmark, which is why the paper’s central claim — the same replication factor, the same failure rate, and orders of magnitude fewer expected data-loss events — is an engineering result, not just a combinatorics exercise.

Rack- and AZ-aware placement, this lesson’s simpler failure-domain-spread half, is already standard practice in widely deployed systems — Hadoop’s HDFS and Apache Cassandra both ship placement strategies that explicitly spread a block’s or a partition’s replicas across racks (HDFS) or racks and datacenters (Cassandra’s NetworkTopologyStrategy) rather than placing them at random, which is exactly the all-in-one-domain-versus-spread comparison this lesson’s second model computes — without necessarily also bounding the cluster-wide copyset count the way the Copysets paper does.

The two halves of this lesson answer two different audit questions about the same replication factor. “Are this item’s own copies spread across failure domains?” is a per-item placement check. “How many distinct combinations of nodes could a correlated failure possibly hit, cluster-wide?” is a fleet-wide design check. A system can pass the first and still fail the second, which is exactly why ★[audit] items in this curriculum ask both questions rather than either alone.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A copyset is a specific set of R nodes that together hold every replica of some piece of data — losing every node in a copyset at once loses that data permanently, no matter how large the rest of the cluster is
  • Naive random placement draws each item's copyset independently and uniformly from all C(clusterSize, R) possible combinations, so the pool of distinct copysets a correlated failure could hit grows combinatorially with cluster size even though R never changes
  • At a FIXED replication factor and a FIXED fraction of the cluster failing at once, naive placement's probability that some item's copyset gets fully hit rises toward certainty as the cluster grows, because the number of ways to pick R doomed nodes out of the K failed ones, C(K,R), grows fast as K grows with the cluster
  • The copyset scheme (Cidon et al., USENIX ATC 2013) bounds the damage by reusing a small, deliberately fixed set of designated node-groups for every item's copyset instead of drawing a fresh combination each time — its designated-copyset count grows only linearly with cluster size, not combinatorially, so the same correlated failure has far fewer combinations left that it could possibly match
  • Failure-domain spread is a separate, simpler question about ONE item's own N copies: all N in the same rack or AZ means a single domain failure destroys all of them at once (P = domain failure rate); spread across N different domains, losing the item needs every one of those domains to fail together (P = domain failure rate ^ N)
  • Copyset count and failure-domain spread are independent levers — a scheme can bound copyset count cluster-wide while still keeping one item's copies in a single rack, and a cluster can spread every item across racks while still drawing each item's copyset from an enormous, uncapped pool of combinations

  1. Cued recall

    A team runs naive random placement at N=200 nodes, R=3, modeling a 1% simultaneous correlated failure (rounded up to 3 nodes, the replication factor itself). They grow to N=500 nodes for more capacity, keeping R=3 and the same 1% failure fraction (5 nodes now, still 1% of the cluster). What happens to the probability that a correlated failure destroys some data, and why, given that neither R nor the failure fraction changed?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It rises from about 63.2% to about 99.995% — computed by dataLossProbability, not estimated. Nothing about how many copies exist (R=3) or what fraction of the cluster can fail at once (1%) changed. What changed is the cluster's own size: at N=500 there are far more distinct 3-node combinations (C(500,3) ≈ 20.7 million against C(200,3) ≈ 1.3 million) that some item's copyset could be drawn from, AND the absolute failure count K grew from 3 to 5 nodes — so the number of ways to pick 3 doomed nodes out of the 5 failed ones, C(5,3)=10, is ten times C(3,3)=1. Both effects push the same direction: a bigger cluster, at an unchanged replication factor and an unchanged failure fraction, is closer to guaranteed data loss under naive placement, not further from it.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    The copyset scheme's designated-copyset count grows only linearly with cluster size — `permutations × floor(clusterSize / R)` — even though it keeps adding designated groups as nodes are added. Why doesn't that undermine the "bounded" claim the way naive's C(N,R) growth does?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the two are growing at fundamentally different rates, not just different starting points. Naive's C(clusterSize, R) grows like clusterSize^R — for R=3 that is roughly cubic, so a 10x larger cluster gives roughly 1,000x more possible copysets. The scheme's count grows like clusterSize^1 — linear — because the number of PERMUTATIONS (`ceil(scatterWidth / (R-1))`) never changes; only the number of same-sized groups within each permutation grows, one group at a time, in step with the cluster. A 10x larger cluster gives the scheme roughly 10x more designated copysets, not 1,000x — which is why the gap between naive's probability and the scheme's widens by orders of magnitude as clusters grow, even though both counts are technically "growing."
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `quorums.mdx` establishes that R + W > N guarantees a read set and a write set intersect — nothing more. A team argues, "our cluster runs N=5, W=3, R=3, satisfying R+W>N, so our data is safe from a correlated multi-node failure too." Is that reasoning sound?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it conflates two independent guarantees. R+W>N is about CONSISTENCY: it guarantees a read cannot miss the most recently acknowledged write, on the assumption that the nodes holding that write still exist to be read from. It says nothing about whether those nodes could be wiped out simultaneously by one correlated event. Durability against a correlated K-node failure is a PLACEMENT question — whether the specific R nodes holding a given item's copies happen to all fall inside the failed set — governed by copyset count and failure-domain spread, not by quorum arithmetic. A cluster can satisfy R+W>N perfectly and still lose data outright if a correlated failure happens to hit one full copyset; the quorum rule has nothing to say about that, because it assumes the replicas it is counting are still there.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: spreading many independent items across randomly combined groups, without bounding how many distinct groups can exist, means a fixed-size shock has more and more chances to land exactly on one whole group as the population grows — even though no single item became any less protected. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Bank loan concentration risk. A bank spreads its lending across many borrowers, and diversification is supposed to protect it: no single default should sink the bank. But if loans are extended without bounding how many borrowers share a correlated risk factor — the same industry, the same regional economy, the same collateral type — then as the loan book grows, the number of distinct "clusters of borrowers who could all default from one shock" grows too, uncapped. A regional downturn (the correlated failure) gets more and more chances to exactly match one of those clusters and wipe it out entirely, even though no individual loan got riskier. Regulators and risk managers respond exactly like the copyset scheme: concentration limits — capping exposure to any single industry, region, or counterparty group — deliberately bound the number of distinct "clusters that could all go bad together" instead of letting an ever-growing loan book generate combinations nobody bounded on purpose.
    Did you have that?
