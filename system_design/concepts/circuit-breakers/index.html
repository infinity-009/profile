Tier 6 · Running it in production Circuit breakers explained well nowhere else

A circuit breaker does not protect the thing you point it at

It protects the caller, not the dependency — the same way a household breaker protects the wiring, not the appliance. Which is why a well-tuned one can save everything except the calls it is guarding, and a badly tuned one is a 15-second outage you inflicted on yourself.

Read first — retry-storms

01

The recommendations got slow, so the login page went down

A checkout API. It has 24 worker threads. Two kinds of request arrive:

  • 100 per second that call a recommendations service. That service normally answers in 50 milliseconds.
  • 40 per second that do not call anything — they read from a local cache, render a page, check a session. About 10 milliseconds of work each.

The client timeout on the recommendations call is 1 second, which is 20 times its normal response time. Generous, defensible, and it never fires: measured over 200,000 calls at this load, zero timeouts.

Now recommendations gets slow. Not down — slow. Its mean response time goes from 50ms to 1.25 seconds, because a cache went cold, or a query plan flipped, or its own dependency is having a day. Every call to it now runs past the 1-second timeout.

Count threads. A call that runs to timeout occupies one worker thread for a full second, so by Little’s Law:

threads occupied = requests per second × time in flight = 100 × 1.0s = 100

The pool has 24. It needed 24 when calls took 60ms; it needs 100 when they take a second. Nothing about the traffic changed.

The pool is exhausted. And here is the part that gets you paged at 3am rather than merely noticed: the 40 requests per second that never touch recommendations also need a thread from that pool. Measured in the rig below, during a 10-second fault the unguarded caller served about 21% of them. Four out of five requests to parts of the product that have nothing to do with recommendations were refused.

Your health check is one of those requests. So is your login page. So, in the version of this that takes down a company, is the endpoint your load balancer uses to decide whether this instance is alive.

The recommendations service was never down. It was slow, and slowness travelled up the call graph and became an outage two services away.

02

Put a breaker in front of it — then try to tune it

The standard answer: wrap the call in a circuit breaker. When the failure rate crosses a threshold, stop making the call at all and fail immediately. Netflix’s Hystrix made this the default pattern in the industry, with defaults of 50% failures, at least 20 requests to judge on, and a 5-second wait before trying again.

The rig runs two identical callers against the same sick dependency. The top trace in each band (crimson) is unguarded; the bottom (teal) has the breaker whose knobs are underneath.

Predict A dependency slows from 60 ms to 1 second. Traffic is unchanged at 100 requests per second. How many threads does the arriving traffic now demand?
Little's Law: in-flight = rate × time in flight = 100 × 1.0 s = 100. The pool has 24. Nothing about the traffic changed and nothing about your service changed — a dependency got slow and your thread pool is now 4× oversubscribed. That is how a slow recommendations call takes down a login page.
Show it without answering

Five experiments, and every number below was measured in this model:

  1. Sicken it, default settings. Unrelated traffic: unguarded 21%, guarded 84%. Thread pool completely full for 78% of the fault unguarded, 17% guarded. That 17% is the detection lag, and it is never zero.
  2. Look at the band for calls that do touch the dependency. Both lanes serve about 1%. The breaker did not improve them at all. It made them fail in microseconds instead of a second, and that is the entire mechanism.
  3. Set min requests to 100 and sicken it again. The breaker never opens; the guarded lane behaves exactly like the unguarded one — 18% either way. A failing caller makes fewer calls per second than a healthy one, so a volume threshold sized against healthy traffic can be unreachable during the failure it exists for.
  4. Set stay-open to 15s and press Hiccup — 1.5 seconds of sickness, then perfect health. Over the next 20 seconds the unguarded caller serves 95% of its dependent calls. The guarded one serves 23%, and the breaker refuses 1,487 requests while the dependency is healthy.
  5. Set stay-open to 0.5s and probes to 1, then sicken it for 10s. Watch the breaker state strip stripe: open, half-open, open, half-open. One lucky probe closes it, full traffic floods back into a dependency that is still sick, and it re-opens.

Experiments 4 and 5 are the same knob pulled in opposite directions, and there is no setting that wins both.

03

The intuition: it is a fuse for your wiring, not for the appliance

The electrical metaphor is the right one, but almost everybody takes the wrong half of it.

A household circuit breaker does not protect the toaster. The toaster is already broken. It trips to stop the toaster’s fault from heating the wiring inside your walls and burning the house down. It protects the shared resource, and it does that by disconnecting the faulty thing from it.

Your caller’s thread pool is the wiring. It is shared by every request the service handles, including all the ones that have nothing to do with the sick dependency. A slow dependency draws that shared resource down to nothing, and a breaker’s job is to disconnect it so the rest of the house keeps its power.

A circuit breaker converts a slow failure into a fast one. Slow failures consume a shared resource; fast failures do not. Everything the breaker buys you comes from that one change, and everything it costs you comes from the fact that it has to guess.

The guessing is the part the diagrams leave out. Picture a receptionist who stops transferring calls to a department that has stopped answering its phone. Good: the lines stay free for everybody else. But she cannot know when they are back at their desks, so every so often she tries one call to find out — and in between those attempts she is turning away callers that the department could have handled perfectly well.

That is not a flaw in her judgement. At the moment she decides, “they went to lunch” and “they will be back in ten seconds” produce exactly the same evidence: an unanswered phone.

04

The mechanism: three states and four numbers

A breaker wraps one outbound call and keeps a rolling count of how that call has been going. It has three states.

  • Closed. Calls pass through. Every outcome is recorded. If the failure ratio over the window crosses the threshold and there have been at least minRequests outcomes in it, the breaker opens.
  • Open. Every call is refused immediately, without touching the dependency and without taking a thread. It stays open for a fixed period.
  • Half-open. A small number of calls — usually exactly one at a time — are allowed through as probes. Enough consecutive successes and it closes and clears its window. One failure and it opens again, restarting the clock.

The four numbers are the whole tuning problem, and each one trades against something.

KnobToo lowToo high
Failure thresholdOpens on normal error noiseNever opens on a partial failure
Min requests in windowOpens on 3 out of 5 requests at 4amUnreachable during the failure — see below
Window lengthTrips on a single bad instantDetection lag: it cannot see a fault faster than the window fills
Stay-open periodFlapping: probes flood a sick dependencySelf-inflicted outage after recovery
Probes to closeOne lucky call re-opens the floodgatesSlow recovery, more failed probes

Two lags you cannot tune away

detection lag ≥ one timeout + time for the window’s ratio to cross
recovery lag ≥ stay-open period + probes × round trip

A breaker learns that a call failed only when the call fails, and a timeout is the slowest possible way to learn it. It learns that the dependency is well only from a probe, and it will not probe until its open period elapses.

In the rig, the timeout is 1 second and the window is 2 seconds, and the breaker takes about 1.8 seconds to open. Those 1.8 seconds are why the guarded thread pool is still completely full for 17% of the fault. A breaker is not a guard; it is a reaction.

The volume threshold trap

This one is worth its own paragraph because it is where a carefully configured breaker silently does nothing.

The threshold is a ratio, so it needs a minimum sample size to be meaningful — hence minRequests. The mistake is sizing that minimum against healthy traffic. At 100 requests per second, a 2-second window normally holds about 200 outcomes, so “at least 100” sounds conservative.

But a caller whose calls are all timing out produces outcomes at the rate its thread pool allows, not the rate requests arrive: 24 threads ÷ 1-second timeout = 24 outcomes per second. During the failure the window holds roughly a quarter of what it holds during health. Set minRequests to 100 and the breaker in this rig never opens at all.

Size the volume threshold against the traffic your window will see while broken.

What counts as a failure

The breaker only sees what you tell it. Three rules that follow from the mechanism:

  • A timeout is a failure. A 500 is a failure. A 400 is not. Client errors say your request was wrong, not that the dependency is sick, and counting them lets one badly behaved caller open everyone’s breaker.
  • A caller-side rejection is not a failure either. If your own pool was full so you never made the call, that is evidence about you. Recording it opens the breaker because you are busy, which removes the dependency you needed to get un-busy.
  • A slow success is invisible unless you make it visible. A breaker watching only errors will sit closed while every call takes 900ms of a 1-second timeout. Some implementations add a slow-call threshold for exactly this; otherwise your timeout is the only thing turning slowness into a signal the breaker can read.

One breaker per what?

A breaker is state, and state has a key. Per-dependency is the usual default and the bluntest: one pathological endpoint opens the circuit for every endpoint on that service. Per-endpoint is better and multiplies your state. Per-host is better still and is what proxies do — Envoy’s outlier detection ejects individual hosts from the load-balancing pool rather than opening one global circuit, and caps how much of the pool it is willing to eject so a fleet-wide blip cannot remove the whole fleet.

And every breaker is per caller instance. A hundred callers have a hundred separate opinions about whether the dependency is well, formed from a hundredth of the traffic each. That is a feature — they will not all probe at once — and a problem: no single one of them has enough samples to be confident, which is the volume-threshold trap again, per instance.

05

The numbers: what to set, and when this matters at all

The precondition is a bounded pool and a synchronous call. If your caller has a bounded number of threads, connections or in-flight slots, and it makes a blocking call to something that can slow down, you have this problem. That is nearly every request-response service ever written. It is not a scale question — a 10 rps admin service with 8 threads exhausts them exactly as thoroughly as a 100,000 rps fleet.

The first number to compute is not a breaker setting. It is rps × timeout against your pool size. If that product exceeds your pool, a slow dependency exhausts you and the breaker is a reaction to a design you can also fix directly:

  • Lower the timeout. It is the multiplier in the arithmetic. Going from 1s to 200ms takes the requirement in the example from 100 threads to 20, which fits in 24. Cost: more timeouts on healthy-but-slow requests, and every timeout is wasted work at the far end.
  • Bulkhead the pool. Give the risky dependency its own bounded slice — 8 threads of the 24 — so exhausting it cannot touch the other 16. This is usually the higher-value change, and it composes with a breaker rather than competing.
  • Cap concurrency instead of counting errors. Limit in-flight calls to the dependency and reject past the limit. It degrades continuously instead of switching, and it needs no threshold: the limit is the threshold.

Then the breaker settings. Reasonable starting points, with Hystrix’s classic defaults for reference:

06

The trade-off: the breaker's job is to be wrong quickly

Every request a breaker refuses in the open state is a request that might have succeeded. That is not a bug to be tuned out. It is the mechanism. A breaker is a bet that failing fast for a while is cheaper than the alternative, and the bet is placed with your users’ requests.

The bet is unavoidable because of a genuine information limit: at the moment it decides, a 1.5-second hiccup and the start of a 20-minute outage produce identical evidence. No threshold distinguishes them, because the distinguishing fact is in the future. Everything you tune is choosing which mistake to make:

SettingOptimises forPays with
Short stay-open (0.5–1s)Cheap false positives — 57 requests, not 1,487Flapping, and probes that keep poking a sick dependency
Long stay-open (10s+)Leaving a genuinely sick dependency alone to recoverA self-inflicted outage of healthy traffic
Low failure thresholdFast protection of the caller’s threadsOpening on ordinary error noise
High min requestsNot judging on a handful of samplesPossibly never opening at all
Many probes to closeNot re-flooding a fragile dependencySlower recovery, more failed probe calls

Three further costs that are not on any dashboard.

While open, you stop measuring the thing you are deciding about. The only evidence you have about the dependency is your own probes, so your data about it is thinnest exactly when you most need it. This is a control loop whose sensor switches off when it acts.

A breaker hides the failure from the place that could handle it better. Opening turns a slow dependency into an immediate error, and an immediate error is only an improvement if the caller does something sensible with it — serve a cached value, drop the recommendations carousel, return the page without the personalised block. A breaker in front of a call whose failure you have no answer for just moves the error earlier. The breaker is the cheap half; the fallback is the half that decides whether your users notice.

Its state is per instance and unobservable to everyone else. Two callers can disagree about whether the dependency is up, and neither is wrong — they have different samples. Any alert on “breaker open” has to reason about a distribution of opinions, not a fact.

What people reach for instead, and why

Netflix wrote Hystrix, made the pattern famous, and then moved on from it: Hystrix went into maintenance mode in 2018, and Netflix’s own successor work is concurrency-limits — adaptive concurrency limits borrowed from TCP congestion control, where the client discovers the in-flight limit the dependency can actually sustain and throttles continuously rather than flipping a switch. That is the direction of travel: from a binary state machine with five hand-tuned constants to a control loop with none.

The honest summary of when to use which:

  • Bulkhead first. Bounding what a single dependency can take from a shared pool is cheap, needs no thresholds, and cannot deny a healthy request.
  • A concurrency limit next, if the dependency’s healthy capacity is stable enough to discover. It degrades continuously instead of switching.
  • A breaker when the failure is expensive to keep retrying — a dependency you can genuinely leave alone, with a real fallback for the requests you refuse.
  • Load shedding at the server, always, in parallel. The server knows its own queue depth; every client-side mechanism on this page is a guess about it. Guesses are what you use when you cannot get the real signal, not what you use instead of it.
07

In the wild

Where the pattern comes from. Michael Nygard’s Release It! introduced the circuit breaker to most of the industry, alongside the failure modes it answers — in his vocabulary the “integration point” and the “blocked threads” antipattern, which is precisely the thread exhaustion measured in slot 1. Martin Fowler’s CircuitBreaker bliki entry (2014) is the canonical short description of the state machine, and is explicit that the interesting part is what you do with the failure rather than the detection.

The implementation everyone copied, and its defaults. Netflix’s Hystrix shipped the rolling-window design used in the rig: a ten-bucket window, a request-volume threshold before it will judge, an error-percentage threshold, and a sleep window after which a single request is allowed through as a probe. Netflix put it into maintenance mode in 2018 and pointed users at resilience4j, while its own systems moved to adaptive concurrency limits. Reading the Hystrix defaults next to that decision is the single most useful hour you can spend on this topic.

The modern form is host ejection, not a global switch. Envoy’s outlier detection watches each upstream host and removes the bad ones from the load-balancing pool, with a bound on what fraction of the pool it may eject. Same idea — stop sending work to something that is failing — at a granularity that cannot take out a whole dependency because of one bad instance, and with the decision made in the proxy rather than in every service’s client library.

The advice that competes with it. Google’s SRE chapter on cascading failures spends its effort on deadlines, load shedding and graceful degradation rather than on breakers, and that emphasis is deliberate: a request that is cancelled when its deadline passes never occupies a thread it cannot use, which removes the mechanism a breaker exists to interrupt. If you can propagate deadlines end to end, do that first.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A breaker protects the caller, not the callee — it is a fuse for your wiring
  • In-flight requests are rate × latency by Little's Law, so a latency rise consumes your thread pool without any traffic change
  • Three states — closed, open, half-open — and four numbers to set
  • Detection lag is at least one timeout plus the window crossing its threshold
  • Recovery lag is at least the open period plus probe round trips
  • The breaker's job is to be wrong quickly rather than right slowly

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Why is a breaker described as protecting the caller rather than the dependency?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the failure it prevents is resource exhaustion in your own process. The dependency is already unwell and your traffic is not what is making it so. What the breaker stops is your threads, connections and memory being consumed waiting on it — which is what turns one sick dependency into an outage of everything sharing that pool. It is a fuse for your wiring, not for the appliance.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    What sets the lower bound on how fast a breaker can detect trouble?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    One timeout, plus the time for the error ratio in the window to cross its threshold. A breaker learns a call failed only when the call fails, and a timeout is the slowest possible way to learn that. So a 10-second timeout means a minimum 10-second detection lag, no matter how the thresholds are tuned — which is why timeouts must be set from the dependency's p99, not from a round number.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Why is "be wrong quickly" the right objective for a breaker?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because both errors are asymmetric in cost. Opening when the dependency was actually fine costs you some failed requests you could have served — recoverable, brief, and self-correcting on the next probe. Staying closed when it is not fine costs you the whole thread pool and every unrelated feature sharing it. Given that asymmetry, a fast trigger with occasional false positives dominates a careful one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Near transfer

    A breaker is configured with a 30-second open period, and the dependency recovers 2 seconds after it trips. What does the user experience, and what would you change?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Failures for the remaining 28 seconds, plus probe round trips — the breaker is now the outage. Shorten the open period and rely on half-open probing to do the deciding: a short open period (1–5 s) with a small number of probes recovers in seconds, and if the dependency is still sick the probes re-open it at negligible cost. Long open periods optimise for protecting a dependency, which is not this component's job.
    Did you have that?
  5. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a fast-acting protective cutout with a known false-positive rate beats a slow, accurate one whenever the failure it prevents is much more expensive than the false positive. Where else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Electrical fuses and RCDs, which trip on suspicion and are trivially resettable. Stock-market circuit breakers halting trading on a threshold move. Automotive ABS releasing brake pressure on suspected lock-up. Aircraft stall protection. The design rule generalises exactly: the cheap, reversible, occasionally-unnecessary intervention wins whenever the thing it prevents is catastrophic and irreversible.
    Did you have that?
