Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Maintainability explained well nowhere else

Two of the three things people mean by "maintainable" get worse while nobody touches the code

Maintainability loses every planning meeting for a structural reason rather than a cultural one: the feature is a benefit now and every maintainability property is a cost avoided later. The fix is arithmetic. All three properties the word covers are separately computable, two of them have dates on them — a team at 20% toil on a fleet growing 40% a year is a pure operations function in 4.8 years — and the module boundary everyone reaches for is worth 15× when it seals and 1.1× when a fifth of it leaks.

01

Nobody wrote any bad code, and the team stopped shipping anyway

A team of six, two years in. The product works. Nobody on the team is careless and the code review is genuinely good. Three numbers from the retrospective:

  • 1.2 of the 6 engineers are spent on operational work — patching, failovers, someone’s alert at 3am, a backup that needs verifying. That is 20%, and 20% has always felt fine.
  • Changing one of the product’s 40 features means reading, and re-testing, all 40.
  • The architecture rests on about ten decisions made in the first six months. Each one still looks right, and each one is maybe 90% likely to still look right in three years.

None of those is a bug. Nobody can point at the commit that caused any of them. And every one of the three has a number attached that nobody has computed:

The toil is not 20%, it is 20% now. The estate is growing 40% a year and headcount is not, so half the team is operating in 2.7 years and all of it in 4.8. The blast radius of 40-out-of-40 is not “legacy”, it is a graph at ten times the density at which everything becomes connected to everything. And ten decisions at 90% each is not “we made good calls” — it is a 34.9% chance the architecture as a whole is still right, with one of them expected to be both wrong and unwindable.

Maintainability loses every planning meeting, and it loses for a structural reason rather than a cultural one: a feature is a benefit now and every one of these is a cost later. You do not win that argument by caring more. You win it by noticing that all three have dates and prices.

02

Draw the module boundaries, automate the toil, and watch how little you keep

Both standard fixes work, partially, and the size of “partially” is the whole lesson.

Automate the toil. A tenfold automation win buys ln(10)/ln(1+g) years — 6.8 at 40% growth. Not “solves”; buys. Set fleet growth to flat in the rig below and both deadlines disappear, which is honest: a fleet that stops growing has no toil deadline. Set it back to anything above zero and the deadline returns, further away and still dated. Automation translates a geometric curve; it does not change the exponent.

Draw the boundaries. Eight modules over 40 features cuts the possible interactions from 780 to 108, a 7.2× improvement, and the bottom band of the rig says the measured value is 15.4× — better than promised, for a reason worth understanding. Then drag the estate around and look at the bar labelled 5% leaks. Five percent of couplings crossing a boundary anyway — a shared table, a global config value, an import added on a Friday — takes 15.4× down to 3.2×. That is 85% of the value, gone, for a violation small enough that no reviewer would block it.

Predict You split 40 features into 8 modules of 5. The pair count says that cuts possible interactions from 780 to 108 — a 7.2× improvement. Measured as blast radius, how much does a sealed boundary actually buy?
What a maintainer feels is reachability, not pair count — and reachability has a threshold. A random graph becomes one giant connected component at a mean degree of 1, i.e. a density of 1/(size−1). Forty features at 25% coupling sit at 9.75× that threshold, so the blast radius is the whole estate: 40.0 of 40. The pair count is a smooth ratio; the real effect is a step change, so it understates a boundary that lands on the right side — and would overstate one that does not.

03

A garden, and the tree you planted in it

Think about looking after a garden.

The weeding is toil. It scales with area, so a garden that doubles in size doubles the Saturdays whether or not you plant anything new. Nothing has to go wrong for the work to grow — that is the definition of the thing. Mulch is the automation: it cuts the weeding by a large factor and it does not stop the garden growing, so the Saturdays come back, later.

How much you must understand to change one bed is the accidental cost. If the beds are genuinely separated you can dig one up knowing only what is in it. If the roots have grown through, digging one bed means knowing the whole plot. Same garden, same plants, same essential complexity — a completely different amount of understanding required to touch it. And gardeners know the threshold effect by name: running bamboo. A handful of connections across a boundary and the whole plot is one organism again.

The tree is the one-way door. It cost almost nothing to plant. Removing it in fifteen years costs a machine, a day and a hole where the tree was. Notice when the decision was made: at the moment you knew least about how the garden would actually be used — which is exactly when the premium on reversibility is highest and exactly when it feels most like over-thinking.

Three separate costs, and the reason to keep them separate is that two of them grow while you are away and one does not.

Operability is a geometric curve with a logarithm for a deadline. The single modelling assumption is stated and testable: manual operational work scales with the size of the estate and headcount does not. So

toil(y) = toil₀ · (1+g)^y        deadline: y = ln(1/toil₀) / ln(1+g)

Automation divides toil₀, which translates the deadline by ln(factor)/ln(1+g) — the same number of years no matter when you spend it. Only a change to g touches the exponent, and g is a business fact rather than an engineering one. This is why the honest version of “we’ll automate it” has a date attached.

Accidental complexity is what you left reachable. The domain has n things in it however you build — that is Brooks’ essential complexity and no design removes it. What an implementation chooses is how many of the n(n−1)/2 possible interactions a change can actually traverse. Boundaries cut the count to m·C(n/m, 2) + C(m, 2), which at 40 features in 8 modules is 780 → 108.

But the pair count is the wrong unit, and here is the correction the model forced. What a maintainer feels is reachability, and reachability over a random graph has a threshold: a giant connected component appears at a mean degree of 1, so at density 1/(size−1). Forty features at 25% coupling sit at 9.75× their threshold — one lump, blast radius 40.0 of 40. Five-feature modules at the same 25% sit at exactly 1.0× theirs — fragments, blast radius 2.6. So the measured value of the boundary is 15.4×, over double the 7.2× the counting argument predicted, and the mechanism is not “fewer pairs” but “each side kept under its threshold.”

Two things follow immediately, and they point in opposite directions. Leakage is fatal out of proportion to its size when the estate is over its threshold, because a few cross-boundary edges are exactly what re-merges the components: at 40 features and 25% coupling, 5% leakage costs 85% of the value and 20% costs essentially all of it. The mechanism is worth holding onto rather than the figure — at 2% coupling the same 5% leakage costs only 9%, because there was no giant component for it to restore. Leakage is not bad in itself; it is bad in proportion to what it reconnects. And below the threshold a boundary buys nothing and still costs the hop — so over-modularising is a real failure mode and not merely excess caution.

Irreversibility is an option, priced like one. Expected cost of each door:

E[one-way] = build₁ + p·(undo₁ + rebuild)
E[two-way] = build₂ + p·(undo₂ + rebuild)

so the one-way door must be cheaper to build by at least p·(undo₁ − undo₂). That premium scales with p, which is your ignorance — largest at the start, when one-way doors are most tempting because there is nothing yet to migrate. And the decisions compose: p^k is the chance all of them are still right, which at 90% and ten decisions is 34.9%, and even odds are gone after 6.6.

06

The trade-off — all three purchases cost something, and one of them is usually overbought

The tables above make maintainability look like something simply underfunded. Each of the three has a real price, and one of them is routinely paid when nothing is being bought.

Boundaries cost indirection, a hop, and an argument. Every boundary is a call that used to be a function call, a type that has to be defined twice, and a design discussion every time the boundary is inconvenient. The lesson’s own threshold result says exactly when that price buys nothing: below a density of 1/(size−1) there is no giant component to prevent, so the boundary is pure cost. Set coupling density below 1/(n−1) — 2.6% at the rig’s default 40 features, and only 0.8% at 120 — and every bar collapses to 1×, because the estate was already in fragments and there was nothing to separate. This is the quantitative form of over-modularisation, and it is the failure mode of a team that has read this lesson and not the threshold in it — see when-to-split and the-distributed-monolith, which is what you get when the boundaries are drawn and the reachability is not.

Reversibility costs building a path you hope never to use. A shim, a dual write, a documented rollback, a migration you run in reverse in staging to prove it works — all of it slows the first launch, and if the decision turns out right you spent it for nothing. That is what an option premium is. The failure mode is symmetrical: below the crossover the two-way door is genuinely the wrong buy, and a team that makes everything reversible ships a system where nothing is ever committed to and every abstraction has two implementations, one of which is never exercised.

Automation is code, and it has its own blast radius. The tenfold win is a new system: it needs operating, it fails at 3am, and it is now inside the graph whose reachability you were trying to control. Toil work that ships no feature is also the work most likely to be skipped in review, which is how the automation becomes the least-understood component holding up the most-critical path.

And the largest trade is the one this lesson cannot resolve. Every number above is a later cost with a real discount rate against it, and sometimes the discount is correct: a product that will not exist in two years should not pay a 4.8-year toil deadline any attention at all, and a prototype’s right blast radius is 40 of 40 because reading everything is fast when everything is small. The point of computing these is not that maintainability always wins. It is that “later” stops being a word and becomes a date, and a date can be compared against how long you expect the thing to live.

07

In the wild

The 50% toil cap, and why it is a cap rather than a target. Google’s SRE practice bounds toil at 50% of an SRE’s time and treats crossing it as an event requiring action, not a mood. Read against the arithmetic above, the reason the number is enforced becomes clear: the 50% line is not where the pain starts, it is the last point at which there is still enough engineering capacity left to build the automation that moves the deadline. Past it the team is spending its remaining capacity on the toil that is growing, and the intervention that would help is the one thing there is no longer time for.

Essential against accidental, named in 1986. Fred Brooks’ No Silver Bullet draws exactly the distinction in slot 4: complexity inherent in the problem, against complexity introduced by our representation of it — and argues that decades of tooling attacked the second while the first is where the difficulty lives. The blast-radius model here is a way of measuring how much of the second you have, which is the part Brooks says is attackable.

One-way and two-way doors. Amazon’s internal framing, described in Jeff Bezos’ 2015 and 2016 shareholder letters, is the option-pricing argument without the algebra: most decisions are two-way doors and should be made fast by people close to the work, and the small number of one-way doors deserve slow, senior deliberation. The arithmetic above says what the framing leaves implicit — that the premium scales with p, so the same decision is a two-way door late in a project and a one-way door at the start.

What an unwindable decision costs, with a real figure. Knight Capital’s August 2012 loss — around $440 million in 45 minutes — began with dead code that had sat unused in the deployment for roughly eight years and was reactivated by a flag reused for a new purpose, on a deploy that reached seven of eight servers. Every element is in this lesson: accidental complexity nobody could see the extent of, a change whose blast radius was not what anyone believed, and a position that could not be unwound at any speed once it was open. The SEC’s 2013 administrative proceeding sets out the sequence.

Complexity grows unless work is done to reduce it. Meir Lehman’s laws of software evolution, developed through the 1970s and stated in his 1980 paper, include increasing complexity — a system’s complexity rises as it evolves unless effort is spent specifically to reduce it. That is the empirical version of the “grows untouched” column, observed decades before anyone tried to put a date on it.

  1. Cued recall

    Your team is 20% occupied with operational work and the estate is growing 40% a year. Nobody writes another line of code. What happens?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    You become an operations team, on a schedule. Toil scales with the size of the estate and headcount does not, so the fraction grows geometrically: f(y) = f₀(1+g)^y. That is 1.2 of 6 engineers today, 3.3 of 6 in three years, half the team at 2.7 years and the entire team at 4.8 years. The curve is the fleet growing — no bad code required. And the deadline is a logarithm, ln(1/f₀)/ln(1+g), which is why the situation looks survivable right up until it is not: at 40% growth every doubling of the fleet is under two years and the last doubling is the one that takes the whole team. The only input that removes the deadline rather than moving it is growth reaching zero, which is rarely something you get to choose.

  2. Cued recall

    So automate the toil. How much time does that buy?

    How sure are you, before you look?

Two consequences. First, there is no "we automated it, that's handled" — the same conversation returns on a schedule you can compute. Second, since the payoff is start-independent, doing it early is strictly better: the same 6.8 years, bought while the team still has slack instead of during the incident that consumed it. The exponent is the fleet's growth rate, and the only thing that touches an exponent is a change to how the estate scales.

    The architecture diagram has eight clean modules. About 5% of couplings cross a boundary anyway — a shared table, a global config value, an import added on a Friday. How much of the boundary's value survives?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About 15% of it. The factor falls from 15.4× to 3.2×, and at 20% leakage it is 1.11× — a rounding error on a diagram everyone believes.   6. Near transfer

    A team of eight is asked to cut its cloud bill by 30%. The plan is to move from a managed database to self-hosted on reserved instances, which the spreadsheet says saves 34%. Evaluate the plan with this lesson.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The spreadsheet is measuring one of three costs, and the plan moves the other two in the wrong direction. Three checks, in order of how much they change the answer.

    One: price the toil, because it is the term the spreadsheet omits and it has a date. A self-hosted database is operational work that a managed one absorbed: patching, failover drills, backup verification, version upgrades, the 3am page. If that adds even 10 percentage points to the team's toil fraction, then on a fleet growing 40% a year the 50% line arrives 2.4 years earlier — and at eight engineers, the added toil is 0.8 of a person immediately and 2.2 in three years. Compare that salary cost against 34% of the bill before anything else, because it is usually the larger number and it is the one nobody put on the slide.

    Two: ask which kind of door this is. Managed to self-hosted is close to a one-way door — the undo requires a second migration with the same data-consistency risk, plus the reserved-instance commitment you bought to get the discount. So the break-even test applies: it must be cheaper by p·(undo + rebuild), where p is the chance this turns out wrong within the commitment term. At even a 20% chance of being wrong and an undo that costs what the migration cost, the required discount is far above 34%. The cheap version of this plan that nobody proposes: get the reserved-instance discount without the migration, which is most of the saving at none of the irreversibility.

    Three: check whether the saving is on the essential or the accidental side. If the bill is large because the workload is large, this is essential and self-hosting is a genuine unit-cost play. If it is large because of something accidental — a read pattern that could be cached, a retention policy nobody set, an instance sized for a launch that never came — then the same 30% is available with no operational burden and no door to walk through. Read cost-per-million-requests and find out which before committing, because the two cases have opposite right answers.

    So: quantify the toil delta in engineer-years, price the reversibility premium against the commitment term, and check the bill's composition first. The likely outcome is reserved capacity without the migration, plus one caching change — most of the saving, none of the door.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a thing you built has three separate ongoing costs — the routine attention it demands, how much of it you must understand to change any part, and how much of what you decided cannot be undone — and two of the three grow on their own while the thing sits untouched. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A garden. The weeding is toil and it scales with area, so a garden that doubles in size doubles the Saturdays whether or not you plant anything new — the deadline arrives on its own, and mulch is the tenfold automation that moves it without removing it. How much you must understand to change one bed is the accidental cost, and it is set by whether the beds are separated or the roots have grown into each other; note the threshold behaviour, which gardeners know as running bamboo — a small number of connections across a boundary and the whole plot is one organism. And the tree you planted is the one-way door: cheap to put in, decades to undo, and the premium on getting it right was highest at the moment you knew least about how the garden would be used.

    Other instances: a city's water network. Leak repair scales with pipe-miles, understanding one intersection means understanding the pressure zone it sits in, and the routes chosen in 1890 are still routing water because a pipe under a building is a decision nobody can revisit. A legal code, where compliance is toil that grows with the number of statutes, cross-references are the coupling graph, and constitutional provisions are the one-way doors — which is exactly why amendment procedures are deliberately made expensive: raising the cost of the decision to buy down the chance it is wrong. And a language's spelling, where irregularity is pure accidental complexity, everyone pays the learning cost forever, and the reason it survives is that the door closed centuries ago.

    The instance that fails the analogy is worth having: a hire has toil and irreversibility but almost no accidental-complexity term, because a person is not a graph you must read to change. Not everything with maintenance costs has all three, and knowing which of the three you are actually facing is most of the value of separating them.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
