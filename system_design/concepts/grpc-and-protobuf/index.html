Tier 1 · The network gRPC and protobuf explained well nowhere else

Fifty-nine per cent of that JSON was field names, and gzip had already deleted them

"Protobuf is 3–10× smaller than JSON" is a true sentence about a comparison nobody ships. Almost all of the win is field names going away, a compressor already removes repeated field names, and on a 50-row page the 3.13× you measure uncompressed is 1.18× on the wire. The parts of protobuf worth having are elsewhere: a field number that makes a rolling upgrade safe, and unknown fields that survive a hop — until a proxy re-serialises them away.

Read first — byte-budgetshead-of-line-blocking

01

The payload got 68% smaller and the wire got 15% smaller

An internal service returns a page of 50 inventory rows. Each row has an id, a millisecond timestamp, a boolean, a price, forty bytes of free text and six integer metrics, under field names averaging sixteen characters — warehouse_pallet_count, that sort of thing. As JSON it is 13,525 bytes. The team moves it to protobuf and measures 4,317 bytes: a 3.13× improvement, comfortably inside the “3–10× smaller than JSON” figure that justified the migration.

Then they look at a packet capture, and the saving is not there.

The load balancer has had content-encoding: gzip switched on since 2019, for everything, in both directions. Compressed, the two payloads are 2,894 bytes and 2,458 bytes. The real saving on the real wire is 436 bytes, a ratio of 1.18× — and the uncompressed comparison that authorised the work overstated it by 2.66×.

Nothing here is a measurement error and nothing is a protobuf defect. The migration compared protobuf against a JSON baseline that had not existed in production for six years. And the reason the comparison collapses is visible if you ask a question almost nobody asks about a serialisation format: which bytes, specifically, went away?

7,923 of those 13,525 bytes are field names. Fifty-nine per cent of the document is the same eleven strings, once per row — 550 occurrences in one response. And a general-purpose compressor’s first stage, LZ77, exists to replace the second and every later copy of a repeated string with a short back-reference. Fifty copies of a sixteen-character name cost one copy plus fifty pointers.

So gzip was already doing protobuf’s main trick, on the same data, before anybody proposed anything.

02

Try it: turn the size knobs and watch the win move from 5× to 1×

The obvious defence of the migration is that 3.13× must be a property of the two formats, so if the measurement disagrees the measurement is wrong. It is not a property of the formats. Every number in this lesson comes out of src/sim/wire.mjs, which is a real protobuf encoder — varint keys, varints, zigzag, fixed64, length-delimited submessages, proto3 default omission — with a real decoder beside it so the encoder is graded by round trip, and a real RFC 1951 DEFLATE compressor whose output Node’s zlib inflates in the tests. Nothing here is an estimate of a size.

Point it at three shapes and the ratio does this:

  • One small message, 16-character names, no free text: 269 B against 56 B — 4.80×.
  • The 50-row page above: 13,525 B against 4,317 B — 3.13×.
  • One 16 kB base64 blob in an envelope: 16,121 B against 16,039 B — 1.01×.

Same encoder, same decoder, same schema shape. The spread from 4.8× to 1.01× is entirely the data, and the direction is mechanical: protobuf deletes names, so the win is large when names are a large share of the document and nearly nothing when one blob is.

Predict The 50-row page is 13,525 B of JSON against 4,317 B of protobuf, a 3.13× win — and 7,923 of the JSON bytes are field names repeated across 50 rows. Now compress both sides, which every real deployment does. What is the ratio?
1.18× measured with zlib at level 6, and 1.42× with the model's own fixed-Huffman DEFLATE. It is not equal shrinkage, because the two formats were only ever differing in one category and that category is the one a compressor is best at. LZ77 replaces every repeat of a string with a back-reference, so fifty copies of a 16-character field name collapse to roughly one copy plus fifty pointers — and 86% of everything protobuf saved was those names. What survives compression is the values, and both formats carry those almost identically. So the correct reading of '3–10× smaller' is: smaller than a JSON document you would never send. Note the fourth option is not silly either — on the 16 kB blob the compressed ratio is 1.02× against an uncompressed 1.01×, so the ordering can invert.

03

The intuition: the labels come off the boxes

Forget encodings. A warehouse ships fifty boxes to a shop, and every box needs a description of what is in it.

JSON is writing the description on every box, in words. "warehouse_pallet_count": 12. Anyone can read a box without being told anything in advance, which is genuinely useful — you can pick up one box in a corridor and know what it is. The cost is that you write “warehouse_pallet_count” fifty times, and the words are longer than the numbers they introduce.

Protobuf is numbering the slots instead. Both ends were given the same diagram beforehand, so box 7 is the pallet count and nobody writes that down. Fifty boxes carry fifty numbers instead of fifty phrases. That is the entire saving, and it is a big one — until you notice the second thing the shipper does.

The shipper already compresses the paperwork. He has been reading these manifests for years, and when he sees “warehouse_pallet_count” for the second time he writes “same as line 3”. By the fiftieth box the words cost almost nothing. So the numbering scheme, arriving as a proposal to save paper, saves much less paper than it looks like — because the thing it deletes was already nearly free.

What the numbering does buy is a different property, and it is the one worth having: the words are no longer load-bearing. You can rewrite every description on the diagram, translate it, correct a spelling — and nothing on any box changes, so nobody downstream has to be told. What you must never do is renumber the slots, because there is no way for anyone to notice.

04

The mechanism: tag, length, value — and the tag is the contract

A protobuf message is a flat sequence of fields with no envelope, no separators and no schema in it. Each field is a key varint followed by a value:

key = (field_number × 8) + wire_type

wire_type is 3 bits: 0 varint (the value’s own base-128 length), 1 fixed 64-bit, 2 length-delimited (a length varint, then that many bytes), 5 fixed 32-bit. The key is itself a varint, so field numbers 1–15 take a one-byte key, 16–2047 take two, and 2048 and up take three.

Two consequences follow from that one line, and they are the two halves of this lesson.

The length of a field is computable from its wire type alone

That is why a decoder can walk past a field it has never heard of. It does not need to know what field 12 means to know where field 12 ends — the wire type tells it to read bytes until the top bit clears, or to skip exactly 4 or 8, or to read a length and skip that many. Forward compatibility is not a feature of protobuf; it is a consequence of self-delimiting fields. JSON has the same property in its grammar, and it is the contract that differs: nothing tells a JSON parser it must keep what it could not name, and almost none of them do.

The field name is not on the wire, so the number is the contract

Renaming a field is byte-for-byte free. The model asserts it at zero tolerance: encode {user_id, display_name, balance_cents} and {account_id, label, balance_minor_units} against the same three field numbers and you get the identical 21 bytes. And it is not just free, it is invisible — an old peer decodes a renamed producer’s message and gets correct values under its own old names.

Renumbering is the opposite of free, and the failure has no signal in it. Swap two fields that share a wire type — an int64 balance and an int32 id are both wire type 0 — and the bytes are still 21, still valid, still decode without an exception:

Only the last row is safe, and it is safe by luck. This is why reserved exists in the .proto language, and it is the whole reason a rolling upgrade works at all: during a deploy, two versions of a service exchange messages in both directions, and the thing that keeps them mutually intelligible is that neither one’s names were ever a promise.

A varint stores seven payload bits per byte and spends the eighth as a continuation flag. So the width is ceil(bits / 7), and that single formula sets every interesting boundary:

Above 2²⁸, a varint is worse than a fixed width. Five bytes against four, and it gets worse: above 2⁵⁶ a varint needs nine bytes against fixed64’s eight, and a uniformly random 64-bit value — a hash, half a UUID, a Snowflake id — needs the full ten. Twenty-five per cent worse than the fixed encoding it was supposed to improve on. This is why fixed64 exists and why protobuf’s own guidance is to use it for hashes.

A negative int32 always costs ten bytes. Not “large negatives” — all of them, including −1. Protobuf sign-extends a negative int32 to 64 bits before encoding, so as far as the varint is concerned the value is 18,446,744,073,709,551,615: ten bytes, every time. sint32 applies zigzag first — n ≥ 0 ? 2n : −2n − 1 — which interleaves the two signs so that small magnitudes of either sign stay small. −1 becomes 1, and costs one byte. A factor of ten on the number −1.

Zigzag is not free. It spends one bit, so a positive value sitting just under a byte boundary gets a byte more expensive: 127 costs 1 byte as an int32 and 2 as a sint32. The rule is mechanical and there is no warning if you get it wrong: if the field can be negative, use sint; if it cannot, use int.

And where gRPC enters, which is not the encoding

Protobuf is a serialisation format. gRPC is an RPC protocol that carries it, and the thing worth knowing about gRPC is a transport fact rather than an encoding one: gRPC is defined over HTTP/2 only. There is no gRPC over HTTP/1.1. So adopting it is also adopting one connection per channel, which is one congestion window and one byte order for every concurrent call on it — the subject of the head-of-line blocking lesson, arriving as a consequence of a serialisation decision. And every hop in the path must speak h2 end to end; a load balancer that terminates HTTP/2 and re-originates HTTP/1.1 to the target cannot carry gRPC at all.

The CPU side, which is real and scales with fields rather than bytes

The encode/decode cost here is an operation count priced from the latency table, not a benchmark of any library: one byte-wise pass at the table’s Snappy rung, plus one main-memory reference per field for a reflective JSON parser against one branch per field for generated protobuf code. 06

The trade-off: you gave up readability, you inherited HTTP/2, and you took on two silent failure modes

You gave up being able to read your own traffic. A JSON body is legible in a log, in a browser’s network tab, in tcpdump, and in a bug report pasted by a customer. A protobuf body is not, and neither is it legible without the schema — which means debugging now depends on having the right .proto version to hand, and “the right version” is a question during exactly the rolling upgrade where you most need to look. You can no longer curl your own API. The tooling answers this (grpcurl, server reflection, protoscope) and every answer is another thing to install and keep current.

You inherited HTTP/2, whether or not you wanted it. gRPC has no HTTP/1.1 transport, so this is not a tuning decision:

  • One connection per channel is one congestion window. 200 small calls and one 40 MB export on the same channel share one rate, so the small calls get a fraction of it. No protocol version fixes this — HTTP/3 removes the ordering coupling and leaves the rate coupling exactly where it was. If you want isolation you need separate channels, which is HTTP/1.1’s six connections rediscovered.
  • Every hop must speak h2 end to end. A load balancer that terminates HTTP/2 and re-originates HTTP/1.1 to the target cannot carry gRPC. Neither can most WAF and inspection appliances without configuration. This lands on the network team, and it is the single most common reason a gRPC migration stalls.
  • Browsers cannot speak it directly. The gRPC wire protocol needs trailers and control over frames that browser fetch does not expose, so anything browser-facing needs gRPC-Web and a translating proxy — which is a second protocol, a second set of bugs, and no streaming in the client-to-server direction.

You took on renumbering as a silent-corruption class. Nothing in the toolchain stops you swapping two field numbers, and if their wire types agree there is no error at any layer — the values simply arrive in each other’s places. reserved and code review are the whole defence, and the change that causes it (tidying a .proto so the numbers run in order) looks like housekeeping.

And you took on the intermediary that re-serialises. This is the one that surprises people, because it is a failure of the compatibility mechanism rather than a gap in it:

The old consumer’s decode is clean in both paths. Every value it knows about is byte-identical. The only difference is 236 bytes across 50 occurrences of field 12, which the re-serialising proxy never had a place to put — and a real generated protobuf class does keep unknown fields in a side buffer and re-emit them, so this failure comes from hand-rolled translation layers, from schema-narrowing gateways, and from any hop that converts to an internal type and back. The symptom at the far end is a field that is present or absent depending on which path a request took, and there is no error log anywhere to correlate it with.

The honest summary. Adopt protobuf and gRPC for the schema, the codegen, the streaming and the field-number contract that makes rolling upgrades safe. Those are real and they are what it is for. Do not adopt it for bytes without compressing your JSON first and re-measuring: the number you get will be closer to 1.2× than to 3×, unless your messages are small — in which case the bytes were never your problem either.

07

In the wild

The specification says the thing people quote it as not saying. Google’s Protocol Buffers Encoding reference is direct about all three mechanisms in this lesson. On negatives: “if you use int32 or int64 as the type for a negative number, the resulting varint is always ten bytes long”, with the recommendation to use sint32/sint64 instead. On field numbers: the guide’s Updating A Message Type rules say you “must not change the field numbers for any existing fields”, and that renaming is safe — the name is compiled away. And on unknown fields, proto3 implementations preserve them: that behaviour was removed in proto3’s first release and restored in protobuf 3.5 precisely because dropping them broke proxies, which is as clear a statement as exists that the forwarding property is the load-bearing one.

The size claim’s own origin is modest. Google’s protobuf documentation has described the format as “3 to 10 times smaller” and “20 to 100 times faster” than XML — XML, not JSON, and uncompressed. The number has been carried across two substitutions since, and each one made it less true. Independent benchmarks that compare against compressed JSON land in the range this lesson’s model does: single-digit percentage differences on text-heavy payloads, meaningful differences on small dense ones.

The transport requirement is in the gRPC specification, and it bites in public. The gRPC wire protocol document defines the framing over HTTP/2 and there is no HTTP/1.1 binding. The practical consequence is a documented, recurring class of deployment problem: AWS Application Load Balancer could not carry gRPC until it added explicit HTTP/2 end-to-end support in October 2020, and gRPC-Web exists as a separate protocol with its own proxy requirement because browsers cannot express the wire format. Every one of those is a transport constraint arriving as a bill for a serialisation choice.

And the field-number rule has a public gravestone. The .proto language has a reserved keyword specifically so that a removed field’s number and name cannot be reused, and the style guide explains why in one line: a new field reusing an old number will read the old field’s data from any peer or any stored message that predates the change. Note the second half — stored messages. Protobuf is widely used at rest, in Bigtable cells, in Kafka topics and in on-disk formats, and a renumbering is then not a rolling-upgrade problem that ends when the deploy finishes. It is a permanent misinterpretation of everything already written.

Now put the page away

Everything above this line was the Passive rung — necessary, and the weakest thing on offer. Testing yourself on it will feel worse than rereading it and will hold roughly twice as long. That gap between how it feels and what it does is the entire reason this section exists.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Protobuf's size advantage over JSON is almost entirely the field names disappearing — 86% of the saved bytes on a representative page — and not clever number packing
  • A general-purpose compressor also removes repeated field names, so the advantage over compressed JSON is far smaller than over raw JSON: 3.13× becomes 1.18× at 50 rows
  • The compression collapse needs REPETITION, so it hits multi-row responses and not single small messages: a one-row message keeps 3.31× after gzip
  • The ratio is a property of the payload's shape, not of the formats: once the free-text field is 16% of the JSON document the ratio is already 3×, and a message that is one blob is 1.01×
  • A varint spends 7 payload bits per byte, so it is cheap under 128 and DEARER than a fixed width above 2^28 — a uniformly random 64-bit id costs 10 bytes as a varint against fixed64's 8
  • A negative int32 is sign-extended to 64 bits and therefore always costs the full 10 bytes, which is the entire reason sint32 and zigzag encoding exist
  • The field NUMBER is the compatibility contract and the field name is not on the wire at all, so renaming is byte-for-byte free and renumbering is silent data corruption with no error anywhere
  • Every field's length is computable from its wire type alone, which is what lets a decoder skip a field it has never heard of and is the whole mechanism of forward compatibility
  • Unknown fields survive only if the intermediary FORWARDS the bytes it received; one that decodes with an older schema and re-serialises from its own object model deletes them silently
  • gRPC's real cost is not the encoding — it is that gRPC is HTTP/2-only, so every call inherits one congestion window per connection and every hop in the path must speak h2 end to end

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    You save about 15%, not 68%. Compressed, the two are 2,894 and 2,458 bytes — a ratio of 1.18× against the 3.13× you measured uncompressed, so the uncompressed comparison overstated the win by 2.66×. The reason is that the two formats were only ever differing in one category. Field names are 7,923 of those 13,525 bytes — 59% of the document — and they account for 86% of every byte protobuf saved. DEFLATE's LZ77 stage replaces the second and every later copy of a repeated string with a short back-reference, so fifty repeats of a 16-character field name cost roughly one copy plus fifty pointers. The compressor does protobuf's main trick, on the same data, for free. What remains is the values, which both formats carry almost identically.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Does that mean protobuf's size advantage is always an illusion once compression is on? Name the case where it is not.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — the collapse needs repetition, and a small message has none. A single one-row message of 269 bytes compresses to 202 bytes against protobuf's 61, keeping 3.31×; the same schema at 50 rows keeps only 1.18×. LZ77 can only delete the second copy of a string, so with one copy of each field name there is nothing to match and gzip's 32 kB window finds almost no redundancy in a 269-byte document. The practical reading is uncomfortable for both camps: protobuf's size win is real precisely where bytes matter least — small, frequent control messages — and mostly gone where a payload is large enough for size to be worth an argument. If your traffic is millions of tiny messages, protobuf's framing is worth real money. If it is paginated pages of rows, turn on compression and stop counting bytes.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    You rename a field from balance_cents to balance_minor_units and deploy. Separately, a colleague reorders three fields in the .proto and renumbers them 1, 2, 3 to match. Which change is safe?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The rename is byte-for-byte free; the renumber is silent data corruption. A protobuf field is written as a key varint of (number × 8) + wire_type followed by the value. The name appears nowhere on the wire — it exists only in the generated code, so both schemas encode the same record into identical bytes and an old peer reads a new producer's message correctly under its own old names. Renumbering moves the contract. Two fields that happen to share a wire type — an int64 balance and an int32 id are both wire type 0 — swap silently: the encoder writes 129900 under key 1, the decoder reads key 1 as user_id, and the result is user_id: 129900, balance_cents: 4711 with no exception anywhere in the path. You are only saved when the wire types happen to disagree, and that is luck, not a check. This is why reserved exists in the language, and why field numbers are the one thing in a schema you never tidy.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    A new producer adds one field. An old consumer, and a proxy in between, both hold the previous schema. Nothing errors. What can still be lost, and how much?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Everything depends on whether the proxy forwards bytes or re-serialises objects. Every protobuf field's length is computable from its wire type alone — one byte at a time until the top bit clears, a fixed 4 or 8, or a length prefix and that many bytes — so an old decoder walks past an unknown field without knowing anything about it. That is forward compatibility, and a real generated class also keeps those bytes in an unknown-field set and re-emits them. A proxy that forwards what it received passes all 4,556 bytes on. A proxy that decodes with its own older schema and re-serialises from its own object model emits 4,320: 236 bytes across 50 occurrences of field 12, gone, with a clean decode, a clean encode, a 200 response and no log line anywhere. The failure appears only at the far end, as a field that is sometimes present and sometimes not depending on which path the request took.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Your service stores signed deltas that are usually small and often negative. You declare them int32. What does that cost, and why is the answer so extreme?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Ten bytes each — the maximum width — for every negative value, including −1. A varint carries 7 payload bits per byte with the top bit as a continuation flag, so small numbers are cheap. But protobuf sign-extends a negative int32 to 64 bits before encoding it, so the value becomes 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 as far as the varint is concerned: ten bytes, every time, regardless of magnitude. sint32 exists for exactly this and applies zigzag — n ≥ 0 ? 2n : −2n − 1 — which interleaves the signs so small magnitudes of either sign stay small. −1 becomes 1 and costs 1 byte instead of 10. The cost of zigzag is one bit, so a positive value near a byte boundary gets one byte worse: 127 is 1 byte as an int32 and 2 as a sint32. The rule is mechanical — if a field can be negative, sint; if it cannot, int — and it is the single most common protobuf schema mistake because nothing warns you.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team replaces a JSON-over-HTTP/1.1 internal API with gRPC and measures a 4% improvement in p99, well short of the 30% the proposal claimed. The payloads did shrink. Where should they look, and what is the one number that would tell them whether the migration could ever have delivered?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Two candidates, and the arithmetic separates them cleanly.

    First, was there any bandwidth to win? On a representative page the wire saving after compression is 2,894 → 2,458 bytes, about 436 bytes. On a 10 Gbit/s datacentre link that is 0.35 microseconds of transmission. A same-datacentre round trip is 500 microseconds. The bytes were never the latency, so no encoding change could have moved p99 through that channel — and that is the number to compute before the migration, not after.

    Second, was there CPU to win? Here there genuinely was some: the operation-count model puts JSON at 173 µs and protobuf at 20 µs for that page — 8.5×, and 153 µs saved is 31% of a same-datacentre round trip. But notice the shape of that: it scales with the field count, 602 fields on a 50-row page against 14 on a single row, where the same saving is 0.7% of a round trip. So gRPC pays for itself on large structured responses parsed at high rate, and pays nothing on the small ones — which are usually the majority of calls and therefore of the p99.

    And then the cost side, which the proposal probably did not price. gRPC is HTTP/2-only by specification: there is no gRPC over HTTP/1.1. So every call now shares one connection per channel, one congestion window, and one byte order — which is the previous lesson's finding, arriving as a consequence of a serialisation decision. If the 200 small calls now multiplex behind one 40 MB streaming call, the p99 got worse for a reason that has nothing to do with protobuf. And every hop must speak h2 end to end: an ALB terminating h2 and re-originating h1 to the target simply cannot carry gRPC at all.

    The honest recommendation is to keep gRPC for the schema, the codegen and the streaming, and to stop justifying it with bytes.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: two parties agree on meaning by position rather than by name, which makes the labels free to change and the positions fatal to change — and it only works as long as every intermediary passes on the parts it does not understand. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A shipping container's seal and manifest. The container is identified by a number, and what is inside each numbered slot is meaning agreed in advance; the words on the paperwork can be translated, reworded or printed in another language without touching a thing, because nothing downstream reads the words to decide what is in slot 3. Renumber the slots and the cargo is misdelivered with every document still internally consistent — no alarm goes off, because nothing was violated. And the forwarding property is the load-bearing half: a transhipment port that moves the sealed container onward carries goods it never inspected, whereas one that unpacks and repacks to its own older packing list quietly leaves behind whatever its list has no line for. Other instances: a mail-forwarding service, which passes on letters addressed to people it has never heard of, versus one that "cleans up" unrecognised mail; a legislature's bill numbering, where the title can be rewritten in committee but reusing a repealed act's number breaks every citation to it; the orchestral score, where the instrument on staff four can be renamed but moving the staves scrambles the parts; and a pharmacy's prescription number, where the drug name may be a brand or a generic while the number is what reconciles. The tell in every case is the same: the safe change is the one that alters what humans read, and the dangerous change is the one that alters what the machinery counts on — and the dangerous one usually looks tidier.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
