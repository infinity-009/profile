Tier 1 · The network The real-time menu explained well nowhere else

Your WebSocket sends a message every thirty seconds whether or not anything happened

Polling, long polling, server-sent events, WebSocket. The feature tables compare them on bidirectionality, which decides nothing. The decision is one curve — compare the staleness you will accept against 1 ÷ (update rate + 1/hold) — and on one side of it there is no trade-off at all. Push does not remove the poll; it moves it and shrinks it.

01

The idle fleet that was not idle

A team replaces 5-second polling with WebSocket. The migration goes well: p99 update latency drops from about 2.5 seconds to under 50 milliseconds, and the request graph on the API tier falls off a cliff. The write-up says they eliminated the polling traffic.

Two months later, with 100,000 concurrent clients and no traffic at all — a quiet Sunday, nobody sending anything — the metrics show:

  • 3,300 frames per second arriving at the WebSocket tier.
  • 6.4 GB of memory across the fleet doing nothing but holding sockets open.
  • A deploy that produces 100,000 simultaneous reconnects, against a steady state of 28 per second.

None of that is a bug. The frames are heartbeats, which exist because a connection that sends nothing gets closed by a NAT table or a proxy idle timeout. The memory is socket buffers. The reconnect storm is what “held state” means when the thing holding it restarts.

The polling did not go away. It changed direction and got smaller.

02

The comparison everyone makes, and the one that decides it

The usual table compares transports on capability: is it bidirectional, does it work through proxies, does the browser reconnect for you. Those are real and they are tie-breakers. None of them tells you which one to use, because they do not mention the two quantities the decision actually turns on:

  • how often the data changes — call it λ
  • how stale you will let a client be

Here is the whole decision. Polling at interval T sends clients ÷ T requests per second. A long poll ends when an update arrives or when the hold times out, so it sends clients × (λ + 1/hold). Set them equal:

T* = 1 ÷ (λ + 1 ÷ hold)

The client count cancels. Above T*, polling sends no more requests than a held connection would and holds nothing — so it wins with no trade-off whatsoever. Below T*, polling sends more requests and still leaves staleness behind, so push wins on both axes and the only remaining question is whether the sockets fit.

That is a curve, so it is a map rather than a table.

Predict Data changes about once a minute. You hold a long poll for 30 seconds. You have 200 clients today and expect 2 million within a year. Below what polling interval does polling start sending more requests than long polling?
20 seconds, and the first option is the trap because it is what everyone says. Polling sends clients/T; long polling sends clients × (λ + 1/hold). Setting them equal cancels the client count: T* = 1/(1/60 + 1/30) = 1/0.05 = 20 s. Neither 60 nor 30 alone is right, because a long poll ends on either event and both terms count — at these values the timeout term is two thirds of long-poll traffic, which is the part a comparison that only counts updates misses entirely. Scale decides whether the winning option is affordable. It does not decide which one wins. Asserted in src/sim/realtime.test.mjs at 10 clients and at 10 million.

03

The intuition: the ward round and the pager

A doctor needs to know when a patient’s condition changes. There are two arrangements.

The ward round. Walk the ward every fifteen minutes and look at everyone. Most visits find nothing changed, and that is fine — a visit is cheap. The cost is fixed and predictable: it does not matter whether it was a quiet night or a terrible one, the round takes the same time. And a patient can deteriorate for up to fifteen minutes before anyone knows.

The pager. Nurses call you when something happens. Notification is immediate and you are not walking corridors for nothing. The cost is that you must carry a device, keep it charged, and stay reachable — a standing arrangement rather than a repeated action.

Now the part of the analogy that matters most, and the reason hospitals do it: the pager gets a test page. Because a pager that has received nothing all night is indistinguishable from a pager with a dead battery. The only way to tell “nothing is happening” from “the channel is broken” is to spend traffic on saying nothing.

That test page is the heartbeat, and it is why the upkeep of a push channel cannot be zero. It is not an implementation detail of WebSocket. It is the price of a standing channel in any domain — the night watchman’s clocking-in station was invented in the nineteenth century for precisely this, so that “nothing to report” and “the watchman is unconscious” would be different observations.

04

The mechanism: four transports, and what each sends when nothing happens

Requests/s per clientHeld socketWhat it sends when idleMean staleness
Polling1/Tnoa full request per intervalT/2
Long pollingλ + 1/holdyesa request per hold timeout~0
Server-sent events1/lifetimeyesa comment frame per heartbeat~0
WebSocket1/lifetimeyesa ping/pong pair per heartbeat~0

Four things in that table are worth more than the rest of this section.

T/2, and where it comes from. Mean polling staleness is half the interval — the wait from a change to the next tick of a periodic process with unknown phase is uniform over the period, so it averages half of it. That is the same residual-time result as the stacked DNS caches in the previous lesson, which is why the model imports meanStaleness from dns.mjs rather than re-deriving it. Two lessons, one law.

λ + 1/hold, and which term dominates. A long poll ends on an update or a timeout. At one update a minute with a 30-second hold, the timeout term is 1/30 against the update term’s 1/60 — so two thirds of long-poll traffic is timeouts, and the hold time is a bigger lever on server load than the update rate is. A comparison that counts only updates gets this backwards.

e^(−λT), the statistic that misleads. The fraction of polls returning nothing is the probability of no arrival in an interval. At one update per hour and a 5-second poll that is 99.86%; at two updates per second it is 0.005%. So “98% of our polls are wasted” is a fact about your update rate and not about polling being wrong — a wasted poll costs one cheap request, and its cost does not grow with how pointless it was. Use this number to notice you should check the boundary, not to decide.

1/lifetime, which hides a thundering herd. Half a million clients reconnecting hourly is 139 requests per second — trivially small, and completely misleading, because a deploy drops every connection at once and returns as 500,000 simultaneous reconnects. Four orders of magnitude above steady state, and the mitigation is the one from the thundering-herd lesson: jittered reconnect backoff, which every serious client library implements and which is easy to disable by accident.

And the heartbeat identity

At equal periods, the ratio of idle traffic between polling and a pushed connection is just the payload ratio, because the periods cancel:

poll bytes ÷ heartbeat bytes = 700 ÷ (2 × 6) ≈ 58×

Which is a large, real win — and it is a win on payload, not on mechanism. The periodic traffic still exists, at the same cadence, in the other direction. Saying “we removed the polling” is how a team under-provisions an idle tier.

The numbers: three workloads, three answers, one formula

The badge row is the one worth sitting with. Half a million clients, and the answer is polling — against the instinct of almost everyone, including the instinct that says “at that scale you obviously need WebSocket”. Scale is the argument for polling here: 500,000 held sockets is 4 to 32 GB of kernel buffers before any application state, and the range is a single kernel tunable rather than a measurement, which is why the model returns it as a range and why every conclusion resting on it says so.

And notice that 98% of the badge’s polls return nothing — e^(−60/3600). That statistic is true, unflattering, and not a reason to change anything.

When does this stop mattering? Below roughly a thousand clients, all four options are fine and you should pick on operational grounds — which one your load balancer understands, which one your client library reconnects properly, which one you can debug with curl. The arithmetic here only starts deciding things in the tens of thousands, and it decides them hardest at the extremes of the update rate rather than at the extremes of scale.

06

The trade-off: a held connection is state, and state is what makes systems hard

Every cost of push comes from the same source — the connection is state — and the costs are more varied than the memory figure suggests.

A deploy becomes a reconnect storm. Restarting a stateless polling tier drops in-flight requests that clients retry anyway. Restarting a push tier disconnects every client simultaneously, and they all come back at once. This is the thundering-herd problem arriving through a door labelled “performance improvement”, and it is why connection draining and jittered reconnect are not optional.

Load balancing gets harder in a specific way. A held connection cannot be rebalanced. Add capacity and the new instances stay empty until connections churn naturally, so a push tier’s load distribution reflects when clients connected rather than current capacity — and scaling out does not relieve a hot instance the way it does for stateless request routing.

Idle timeouts are a distributed configuration problem. A NAT table, a proxy, a load balancer and a CDN each have an idle timeout, and your heartbeat interval must be shorter than the smallest one anywhere in a path you do not fully control. Get it wrong and connections drop silently for a subset of users on a particular network, which is a maddening class of bug to diagnose because it is invisible from your side.

Polling’s cost is predictable and push’s cost is bursty. Polling generates clients/T requests per second, forever, whatever is happening — provisioning is arithmetic. A push tier is nearly free when idle and generates load in bursts correlated with real events, which is exactly when everything else is also loaded. Predictable-and-higher is often the easier engineering problem.

And a genuine cost of polling that the boundary does not capture: synchronised clients. If everyone polls on a wall-clock boundary, clients/T is a mean concealing a spike. Jitter the interval per client — the same fix, again, from the same lesson.

The option missing from the menu. When the update source is willing to make an outbound request, a webhook dominates everything above: push semantics with the connection held by nobody, no heartbeat, and no idle traffic in either direction. That is why git providers, payment processors and every serious API offer them. The four-transport menu is what you are left with when the source will not call you — which is the browser case, and which is why the browser case drives the whole discussion.

07

In the wild

The heartbeat is in the specifications, not bolted on afterwards. WebSocket defines Ping and Pong control frames; server-sent events conventionally keep a stream warm with comment lines. Both exist for the reason slot 3 gives — a silent channel and a dead channel are indistinguishable — and both are the reason an idle push fleet has a non-zero traffic floor. That the mechanism is standardised is the strongest evidence that it is structural rather than incidental.

The 60-second heartbeat is a compromise with middleboxes you cannot see. The interval every library defaults to is not tuned against your servers; it is tuned to be shorter than the idle timeouts of NAT tables, corporate proxies and mobile carrier gateways. Which is a nice illustration of a general property of network engineering: your protocol’s parameters are frequently set by equipment owned by people you will never speak to.

And the reason polling refuses to die. Every few years a piece of infrastructure replaces polling with push and later reintroduces a slow poll alongside it — as a safety net, because a missed push is undetectable and a poll is self-healing. That pattern is worth recognising as a design rather than a failure: the poll is not there for latency, it is there so that a lost update has a bounded lifetime. Push for latency, poll for convergence, and accept that you are paying for both.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The polling/push boundary is acceptable staleness versus 1 ÷ (update rate + 1/hold time), and it contains no client count
  • Above that boundary polling sends no more requests than a held connection and holds no state, so it wins with no trade-off
  • Below it polling sends more requests and still leaves staleness, so push wins on both axes and only memory is in question
  • Mean polling staleness is half the interval — the same residual-time law as stacked DNS caches
  • The fraction of polls returning nothing is e^(−λT), so polling looks absurd at rare updates and is nearly pure signal at fast ones
  • A long poll ends on an update or a timeout, so its request rate is λ + 1/hold and the timeout term often dominates
  • Every push transport heartbeats to survive idle timeouts, so the periodic traffic never reaches zero — it only gets smaller

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    Data changes about once a minute, you hold a long poll for 30 seconds, and you have 200 clients today and expect 2 million next year. At what polling interval does polling stop being the cheaper option, and how does the answer change at 2 million clients?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    20 seconds, and it does not change at all. Polling sends clients ÷ T requests per second; a long poll ends on an update or a timeout, so it sends clients × (λ + 1/hold). Setting those equal, the client count cancels: T* = 1/(λ + 1/hold) = 1/(1/60 + 1/30) = 20 s. So if you can tolerate 20 seconds or more of staleness, polling sends no more requests than a held connection would and holds no state — there is no trade being made. Scale decides whether the winning option is affordable; it never decides which one wins.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A team replaces 5-second polling with WebSocket and reports that they "eliminated the polling traffic". What is still happening, and roughly how much of it?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Heartbeats. A held connection dies to a NAT or proxy idle timeout unless something keeps it warm, so every push transport in production pings periodically — and a heartbeat is a poll in the other direction. At a 30-second heartbeat, 100,000 clients send about 3,300 ping/pong rounds per second while completely idle. What actually changed is the payload: a poll is a full HTTP request at perhaps 700 bytes, a ping/pong pair is about 12, so the idle byte rate fell by roughly 58× — the payload ratio, because the equal periods cancel out of the comparison. That is a large and real win. It is not elimination, and the difference matters when you size the fleet: an idle WebSocket tier is not free, and "we removed the polling" will make you under-provision it.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A notification badge updates about once an hour per user, 60 seconds of staleness is fine, and there are 500,000 users. Almost everyone reaches for WebSocket. What does the arithmetic say?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Poll. The crossover is 1/(1/3600 + 1/30) ≈ 29.8 seconds, and you will accept 60 — so you are above the line, and polling at 60 seconds sends 8,333 requests per second with no held state at all. That is an ordinary stateless tier. The WebSocket alternative holds 500,000 sockets, which is 4 to 32 GB of kernel buffers alone — the spread is one kernel tunable, not a measurement — plus a deploy that drops every connection at once and returns as half a million simultaneous reconnects rather than 139 per second. And yes, 98% of those polls return nothing, which is the statistic that makes people uncomfortable and is not the point: a wasted poll costs one cheap request, and the alternative costs a socket held open for the whole session.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Why is "98% of our polls return nothing" a bad argument for switching to push, and what is the good one?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because it measures the wrong thing. The wasted fraction is e^(−λT), which is high whenever updates are rare — but a poll that returns nothing is cheap, and its cost does not grow with how pointless it was. The good argument is the boundary: if the staleness you need forces you to poll faster than 1/(λ + 1/hold), then polling is sending more requests than a held connection and still leaving staleness on the table, and you are losing on both axes simultaneously. That is a structural argument rather than an aesthetic one. The wasted fraction is a good way to notice you should check the boundary, and a bad way to decide.
    Did you have that?
  5. Near transfer

    A build system polls a git provider every 60 seconds for new commits across 4,000 repositories. Commits arrive a few times a day per repo. The provider starts rate-limiting them and the team's proposal is to poll every 5 minutes. What does this lesson say about both the diagnosis and the fix?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Same shape, same arithmetic, and the proposed fix is on the wrong axis. Updates arrive at roughly 3/86400 per repo per second, so with any sane hold time the crossover is around 30 seconds — they are already well above it, which is why polling was the right call to begin with and why the rate limit is a volume problem rather than a design error. Polling every 5 minutes cuts request volume 5× and multiplies mean staleness by 5, to 2.5 minutes, which may be fine or may defeat the point of CI. The structural fix is the one git providers already ship: a webhook, which is push with the connection held by nobody — the provider makes an outbound request when something changes, so there is no held socket, no heartbeat, and no idle traffic in either direction. That is the option missing from the usual menu, and it is available exactly when the update source is willing to call you. When it is not, you poll, and the interval is a staleness budget rather than a preference.
    Did you have that?
  6. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a consumer needs to know when a rare event happens; it can either check repeatedly at a chosen interval, paying for every check whether or not anything changed, or maintain a standing channel that must be kept alive with periodic signalling — and the standing channel's upkeep never reaches zero. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A hospital pager versus a ward round. A doctor doing rounds checks every patient on a fixed interval and most checks find nothing changed — cheap per check, and a patient can deteriorate for up to one interval before anyone knows. A pager is a standing channel: notification is immediate, and it costs a device that must be carried, charged and tested — and the periodic test page is the heartbeat, sent precisely because a silent channel is indistinguishable from a broken one. That last part is the deep half of the analogy: you cannot tell an idle push channel from a dead one without spending traffic, which is why the upkeep cannot be zero in either domain.

    Other instances: a smoke alarm's weekly chirp test, the same heartbeat for the same reason. Freight logistics, where scheduled milk-run collection is polling and call-for-collection is push — and the standing arrangement costs a retained carrier. And a night watchman's clocking-in stations, which exist so that "nothing to report" and "the watchman is unconscious" are different observations — the invention of the heartbeat, in the nineteenth century, for exactly the reason WebSocket needs one.
    Did you have that?
