Tier 1 · The network Packet loss and retransmit explained well nowhere else

A lost SYN can hang a connection for 63 seconds, doubling its wait every single retry

Fast retransmit needs three duplicate ACKs, which needs three packets to arrive after the lost one — a packet lost near the end of a burst has no followers to generate that evidence, so it waits out the retransmission timer instead. That timer is floored at one full second by RFC 6298 regardless of how fast the network actually is, and a lost SYN before any RTT sample exists starts there and doubles on every retry: six retries is 63 seconds before a connection attempt gives up, on a formula anyone can compute, not a mystery hang.

Read first — network-layersip-and-nat

01

A one-second latency spike that shouldn't be possible on a 2ms network

Two services sit in the same cloud region, same availability zone, round trip under 2 milliseconds. Most requests between them complete in single-digit milliseconds. A small, consistent fraction take over a full second — not variable, clustered almost exactly at 1.0 to 1.1 seconds, disproportionately on the smallest responses.

Nobody can find a 1-second delay anywhere in the application code, because there isn’t one. The delay is a rule from a 1990s-era RFC, applied exactly as written, and it can be computed in advance rather than discovered in production.

02

Assume packet loss recovers uniformly, and find where the recovery mechanism runs out of runway

The instinct is that “TCP handles packet loss” as a single fact — lose a packet, TCP notices, TCP resends, done, roughly as fast either way. The actual mechanism has two completely different recovery paths with wildly different costs, and which one you get depends on nothing app-visible: it depends on where in the flight the lost packet happened to be.

Predict RFC 6298 computes RTO from measured RTT and its variance, then applies a floor. On a same-datacenter connection with a 1ms round trip, what does an unrecoverable ('tail') packet loss cost, in wait time?
RFC 6298 §2.4 requires the computed RTO to round UP to a 1-second minimum whenever the SRTT + 4×RTTVAR formula produces something lower — which it always does on a fast, low-jitter link. The floor isn't a function of the actual RTT at all once it kicks in, which is exactly why a 1ms connection pays 1,000× its own round trip for the identical absolute wait a 150ms connection pays under 7× for.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — an 8-packet window, a 48ms RTT — 37.5% of loss positions can’t fast-retransmit, and when that happens the wait is the floored 1.0 second, against the 48ms fast retransmit would have taken: a 21× penalty for landing in the last three packets instead of anywhere else.

03

The anchor leg with nobody left to notice

A relay team drops the baton. If it happens on the second leg, with two more runners still to go, someone notices almost immediately — there’s a next leg whose confusion is itself a signal. If the anchor runner drops it, right before the line, there is no next leg. Nothing downstream exists to generate an early signal, so the discovery waits on the runner’s own reaction — slower, and with nothing to compare against.

Fast retransmit’s three duplicate ACKs are exactly those downstream runners. They exist only if packets kept arriving after the lost one — evidence generated by continuation, not by the loss itself. A loss with no continuation left has no fast path, structurally, no matter how good your TCP stack is.

04

The mechanism — evidence that only exists after the fact, and a floor that ignores the facts

Fast retransmit needs 3 duplicate ACKs, which needs 3 packets to arrive at the receiver after the lost one, in the same flight. A window of W packets with the loss at position k (from the start) has exactly W − k followers:

fastRetransmitFeasible  =  (W − k) ≥ 3

Exactly the last 3 positions in any window fail this, so:

tailLossProbability(W)  =  3 / W          (W ≥ 3; 1 below that)

When fast retransmit isn’t feasible, recovery waits on the retransmission timer — RTO — and RFC 6298 fixes its floor regardless of network speed:

RTO  =  SRTT + max(G, 4 × RTTVAR)      then:      RTO  =  max(RTO, 1 second)

The floor exists to stop ordinary jitter from triggering spurious retransmits — a real problem the rule solves — but it applies the identical 1-second wait whether the underlying RTT is 200ms or 200 microseconds, which is why the multiple of RTT it represents is where the real story is:

penalty  =  RTO / RTT

A lost SYN starts this exact clock with no RTT sample to work from, so it begins at the 1-second floor and doubles on every retry (RFC 6298 §5.5) — the mechanism behind “why did connecting to a dead host hang for over a minute,” computed rather than guessed at.

06

The trade-off — the floor and the backoff both exist to prevent a worse failure

The 1-second floor isn’t an oversight — it exists because a timer tuned to the typical RTT would fire on ordinary jitter, and a spurious retransmit is its own cost: wasted bandwidth resending data that was going to arrive anyway, and in some stacks, an unnecessary congestion-control backoff triggered by a “loss” that wasn’t real. RFC 6298’s authors picked a floor conservative enough to make that false-positive rate very low on the general internet, at the acknowledged cost of tail loss being expensive when it genuinely happens. Modern mechanisms — Tail Loss Probe (RFC 8985) and TCP RACK — attack this by sending a probe before the full RTO expires rather than by lowering the floor itself, converting most tail losses into roughly one extra RTT instead of the full floored wait; check whether your kernel has them enabled before concluding the floor itself needs changing.

SYN backoff’s total wait is a deliberate trade between patience and abandonment. A short total (low retry count) fails fast against a genuinely dead host, which is good — but it also fails fast against a host that’s merely slow to accept a connection under load, which is bad. Linux’s default of 6 retries (~63-127 seconds depending on kernel specifics) errs toward patience; many application-level HTTP clients override it with their own, much shorter connect timeout (often 1-10 seconds) specifically because waiting a minute for a “maybe it’s just busy” host is rarely the right trade for a user-facing request, even though it might be exactly right for an internal batch job that can afford to wait.

None of this is a reason to avoid short flows or artificially inflate them. Small responses being more exposed to tail loss is a real, computable fact — not a reason to pad every API response with junk bytes to manufacture followers. The actual mitigations (TLP/RACK, sensible connect timeouts, and simply knowing which of your traffic is tail-loss-exposed when you’re debugging a mystery 1-second spike) cost far less than trading real bytes for insurance against a fairly rare event.

07

In the wild

RFC 6298 is the RTO specification this lesson computes directly, floor and all. Paxson, Allman, Chu and Sargent, RFC 6298: Computing TCP’s Retransmission Timer (IETF, 2011), is the current standard, obsoleting an earlier 1988 specification, and its §2.4 is the exact source of the 1-second floor and its stated justification (avoiding spurious retransmission on ordinary variance) that this lesson’s model reproduces without embellishment.

The exponential backoff on retransmission, including for a lost SYN, is specified in the same document. RFC 6298 §5.5 requires doubling the RTO on every retransmission of the same segment — the mechanism behind this lesson’s SYN backoff schedule, and the reason “double the wait every retry” isn’t folklore but a normative requirement most TCP stacks implement close to verbatim.

Tail loss got its own RFC because fast retransmit’s limitation was a known, named, and eventually fixed problem. Dukkipati, Cardwell, Cheng and Mathis (eds.), RFC 8985: The RACK-TLP Loss Detection Algorithm for TCP (IETF, 2021), formalizes Tail Loss Probe — a sender-side probe sent shortly before the full RTO would expire, specifically to give tail losses a fast-path recovery closer to fast retransmit’s cost than to the floored timer’s. Its existence is itself evidence that the problem this lesson describes was significant enough, across the real internet, to justify a new IETF standard rather than a footnote.

Fast retransmit’s 3-duplicate-ACK threshold has been the standard since the original congestion-control work. Jacobson’s Congestion Avoidance and Control (ACM SIGCOMM, 1988) — later formalized across several RFCs including RFC 5681 — established the 3-duplicate-ACK trigger this lesson’s fastRetransmitFeasible() implements directly, a design choice old enough that “why exactly 3” is now treated as settled practice rather than an open question.

  • Vern Paxson, Mark Allman, Jerry Chu and Matt Sargent, RFC 6298: Computing TCP’s Retransmission Timer, IETF, 2011.
  • Neal Cardwell, Yuchung Cheng, C. Stephen Gunn, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh and Van Jacobson, RFC 8985: The RACK-TLP Loss Detection Algorithm for TCP, IETF, 2021.
  • Van Jacobson, Congestion Avoidance and Control, ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 1988.
  • Matt Mathis, Jamshid Mahdavi, Sally Floyd and Allyn Romanow, RFC 2018: TCP Selective Acknowledgment Options, IETF, 1996 — SACK, which lets a receiver report exactly which segments arrived, sharpening the evidence fast retransmit and RACK both act on.
  • Ethan Blanton, Mark Allman, Lisong Wang, Ilpo Järvinen, Michael Kojo and Yoshifumi Nishida, RFC 6675: A Conservative Loss Recovery Algorithm Based on Selective Acknowledgment (SACK) for TCP, IETF, 2012.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Fast retransmit fires on three duplicate ACKs, which requires at least three packets to have arrived AFTER the lost one in the same round trip — a structural requirement, not a tuning knob
  • A packet lost within the last three positions of a flight has no way to generate three duplicate ACKs, so it can only be recovered when the retransmission timer (RTO) expires
  • RFC 6298 sets RTO to SRTT plus four times RTTVAR, then rounds UP to a hard 1-second floor whenever that comes out lower — a rule written to prevent premature retransmits, applied identically on every network regardless of speed
  • The 1-second floor means a 1 ms datacenter round trip pays 1,000× its own RTT waiting out a tail-loss timer, while a 150 ms cross-continent connection pays under 7× for the exact same absolute one-second wait
  • A lost SYN has no RTT sample yet, so its retry timer starts at that same 1-second floor and doubles on every attempt (RFC 6298 §5.5) — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 seconds
  • Six SYN retries — a common default — sums to 63 seconds before a connection attempt is abandoned, purely from doubling; one more retry very nearly doubles that total again, to 127 seconds
  • Tail-loss probability is exactly 3 divided by the window size (for windows of three or more), so it falls fast as flights get longer — 75% at 4 packets, under 5% at 64
  • Short flows — a small API response, a handful of packets — are disproportionately exposed to tail loss simply because "the last three packets" is a much bigger fraction of a short flight than a long one

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A backend engineer says "our p99 latency has a weird spike to exactly 1 second on some requests, no matter how fast the network path is." What's the mechanism?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    That's the RFC 6298 RTO floor, and "no matter how fast the path is" is the tell, not a coincidence. RTO is computed as SRTT + 4×RTTVAR, but the RFC requires rounding UP to 1 second whenever that computed value comes out lower — which it always does on anything faster than a very slow WAN link. A request that loses a packet too close to the end of its response (see the next question) can't fast-retransmit, so it waits out that exact floor: 1.000 second, regardless of whether the underlying RTT is 200ms or 200 microseconds. The fix isn't tunable RTO in most stacks — it's reducing how often a loss lands in the unrecoverable tail, which is a flow-size and window question, not a timer question.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why does a small API response (say, 4 packets) suffer tail loss so much more often than a large file download (say, 200 packets), at the identical underlying packet loss rate?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because tail-loss probability isn't about the loss rate at all — it's about what fraction of the flight is "close enough to the end" to lack three followers. That fraction is exactly 3 / windowPackets: 75% at a 4-packet flight, 9.4% at 32, under 2.5% at 128. The loss rate decides how often something is lost; the window size decides how expensive that loss is once it happens. A small response has almost no room for fast retransmit to work at all — most of its packets ARE the tail.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A team enables TCP keepalives more aggressively to "detect dead connections faster," but a colleague says this doesn't address why connection attempts to genuinely dead hosts still hang for over a minute. Are they right?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Yes — keepalives and SYN retry backoff are solving different problems. Keepalives probe an already-established connection to detect whether the remote end has silently gone away; they say nothing about how long a fresh connect() call waits when its very first SYN is lost or unanswered. That's governed by the SYN retry schedule: starting from the 1-second RFC 6298 floor and doubling each attempt, 6 retries sums to 63 seconds, and Linux's default of 6 retries produces close to that in practice. If "the connection hangs on first attempt to a dead host" is the actual complaint, the fix is a lower `tcp_syn_retries` (or an application-level connect timeout), not a keepalive setting — a completely different knob for a completely different phase of the connection's life.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Given that tail loss is expensive, why not just make every flight artificially longer (pad responses, or hold connections open with dummy data) so there's always "room" for fast retransmit?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the fix has costs on both sides of the trade, and the direct one is real: you'd be sending bytes purely to create three artificial followers, which is bandwidth spent buying insurance against an event that, per-request, is fairly rare (loss rate × tail fraction). The mechanisms that actually solve this — Tail Loss Probe (RFC 8985) and TCP RACK — instead have the sender proactively send a probe segment after a short timeout shorter than the full RTO, so the ACK (or lack of one) resolves the loss in roughly one extra RTT instead of the full floored RTO, without inflating every flight's size to manufacture followers that usually aren't needed.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Someone claims "the RFC 6298 1-second RTO floor is outdated and should just be removed on fast modern networks." What was the floor actually protecting against, and does removing it just work?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The floor exists to prevent a retransmission timer that's too aggressive from firing on ordinary jitter rather than genuine loss — a spurious retransmit sends a duplicate of data that was going to arrive anyway, wasting bandwidth and (worse) potentially triggering the sender's congestion control to back off for no real reason. Lowering the floor trades "tail loss costs 1 second" for "ordinary RTT variance sometimes triggers unnecessary retransmits," and the right trade depends on how variable the actual path's RTT is — which is exactly what RTTVAR already measures. This is a real, actively-debated area (some modern stacks and data-center-specific TCP variants do use lower floors, deliberately, with tighter RTT measurement to compensate) rather than a settled "just remove it," which is why it's still 1 second in RFC 6298 for the general internet case decades after it was written.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    Your service's p99.9 latency has a strange bimodal shape: most slow requests cluster around 200-400ms (a normal tail), but a smaller cluster sits at almost exactly 1.0-1.1 seconds. The affected requests are disproportionately your smallest API responses (single-digit KB), and disproportionately from clients on very low-latency connections (same-region, same-cloud-provider traffic). Diagnose it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    This is tail loss meeting the RFC 6298 floor, and every detail in the report points at the same mechanism rather than three separate ones.

    The exact 1.0-1.1 second cluster is the RTO floor, not a coincidence. RFC 6298 requires RTO to round up to 1 second whenever the computed value (SRTT + 4×RTTVAR) is lower, and on same-region, same-cloud-provider traffic the RTT is likely under 5ms — meaning the computed RTO is a few tens of milliseconds at most, and the floor is doing all the work. The extra 0.0-0.1 second on top of the clean 1.0 is the actual RTT plus processing time riding on top of the floored wait.

    Small responses are disproportionately affected because they have less room for fast retransmit. A single-digit-KB response is a handful of packets — at, say, 4-6 packets, tail-loss probability (3/window) is 50-75%, meaning most loss events on these responses land in the unrecoverable zone by construction, not bad luck. A larger response spreads the same underlying per-packet loss rate over many more packets, most of which have 3+ followers and recover via fast retransmit in about one RTT — invisible in the latency distribution because it's fast.

    Low-latency clients are disproportionately affected because the floor costs them more, not because they lose packets more often. The underlying per-packet loss rate is presumably similar across your traffic; what differs is the multiple the floor represents. A 2ms-RTT connection pays 500× its own RTT sitting out the floor; a 150ms cross-region connection pays under 7×, so the SAME 1-second wait is a much smaller relative event for it, and might not even separate cleanly from that population's normal tail in a histogram.

    What to check: correlate the 1.0-1.1s cluster against response size in packets (not bytes — a mostly-header response and a full-MTU response of the same byte count have different packet counts) and against measured RTT to those specific clients; both should show the pattern above. What to actually do about it: confirm whether your kernel/TCP stack has Tail Loss Probe (RFC 8985) or RACK enabled — most modern Linux kernels do by default, and if this pattern is showing up anyway, that's worth escalating as a configuration gap rather than an inherent TCP limitation, since TLP exists specifically to convert this exact failure mode from "wait the full floored RTO" into "wait about one extra RTT."
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a recovery mechanism requires evidence that only exists if something else happens AFTER the failure, so failures near the very end of a sequence — where there's no "after" left — can't use the fast path and fall back to a much slower, fixed-cost one. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A relay race baton drop. If a runner drops the baton and there are teammates still ahead who haven't finished their leg, the drop is often "recoverable" quickly — a coach or the runner themselves can react almost immediately because there's continuing race action to react to and compare against. But a baton dropped by the anchor (last) leg, right before the finish line, has no "next leg" generating any signal at all — there's nothing after it to notice something went wrong faster than the runner's own reaction time, which is the race equivalent of falling back to a fixed, much slower timeout instead of an immediate fast-path recovery.

    Other instances: a manufacturing defect caught by downstream inspection stations — a flaw at station 3 of 10 gets caught fast because stations 4 through 10 all have a chance to flag it, generating multiple independent "something's wrong" signals quickly (the equivalent of duplicate ACKs); the same flaw introduced at station 9 of 10 has only one downstream station left to catch it, and if that station misses it, the defect isn't caught until a much slower, expensive process — a customer return or a full audit — the equivalent of waiting out the RTO. And a break in a relay of radio operators passing a message down a line: a garbled message near the start gets corrected quickly because many operators down the line will notice the next handoff doesn't make sense, while the same garble in the second-to-last handoff has only one more operator who might notice, and if they don't, the error surfaces only when the final recipient acts on bad information — a fixed, late, and expensive discovery instead of an early, cheap one.
    Did you have that?
