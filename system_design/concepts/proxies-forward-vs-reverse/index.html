Tier 1 · The network Proxies

Concentrating TLS termination at one reverse proxy costs the same CPU as splitting it across the fleet — but a compromise of it can see 20x more traffic

A forward proxy and a reverse proxy are the same box shape pointed in opposite directions — one serves a client population talking to many destinations, the other serves many clients talking to one destination — and that direction, not the protocol, is the entire difference. This lesson computes what concentrating traffic at either one actually costs (CPU, connections, ports) and what it exposes if that one box is compromised.

01

Same box, opposite direction, and the difference is who it can see

A platform team migrates TLS termination from 50 application servers to a single reverse proxy in front of them, expecting the CPU graphs to drop. They don’t move. A separate team runs a forward proxy for outbound employee traffic and treats a compromise of it with the same urgency as a compromise of the public-facing reverse proxy fronting their API. Neither team is wrong to have tried — both are missing the one number that actually distinguishes a forward proxy from a reverse proxy: not the CPU it spends, but who and how much it can see.

02

Assume concentrating a proxy saves compute, and watch two bars refuse to differ

The instinct: a reverse proxy centralizes TLS termination, so surely it does the same work with less total CPU than a fleet of servers each doing their own — economies of scale, one part of the system optimized instead of fifty. That instinct is wrong for the CPU number and right for a completely different number, and this rig can show you both without letting you conflate them.

Predict 20 servers each terminate their own TLS at 1,000 handshakes/second. A team moves termination to one reverse proxy handling all 20,000 handshakes/second instead. What happens to the TOTAL CPU cores spent on TLS termination?
Total TLS termination CPU is (microseconds per handshake) × (handshakes per second), summed. That product is the same whether it's computed once at 20,000/s or 20 times at 1,000/s each — the total handshake RATE the traffic represents doesn't change based on where you terminate it. Concentration changes the number of certificates and private keys involved, not the arithmetic of how much crypto has to happen.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — 20 servers, 1,000 handshakes/second each — terminating TLS costs 0.99 cores whether it’s split across the fleet or concentrated at one reverse proxy: the same number, because the arithmetic is linear. What concentration buys instead is 20x fewer certificates (0.22/day across the fleet versus 0.011/day at one proxy) — fewer private keys that exist to leak. On the exposure side, at a 2-hour detection window a compromised forward proxy could have observed 720,000 requests — one client’s share — while a compromised reverse proxy fronting the same 20 clients could have observed 14,400,000 — a 20x gap that comes entirely from aggregating every client’s traffic, not from anything either proxy does differently.

03

The doorman sees every package; the assistant sees only yours

A resident’s personal assistant handles that one resident’s outgoing mail to however many recipients the resident deals with — wide on destinations, narrow on whose mail it is. A building’s doorman, by contrast, is where every courier serving every resident converges before reaching anyone — narrow on destination (this building), wide on whose deliveries pass through. Nobody routed all those deliveries through the doorman on purpose the way a resident chooses their own assistant — the building’s own layout made the doorman the one point everything necessarily passes. A compromised or gossiping doorman has seen every package addressed to everyone in the building. A compromised assistant has seen one resident’s mail, however far it traveled.

A forward proxy is the assistant: one client, many destinations, exposure bounded by that one client no matter how many other clients also happen to run their own assistants. A reverse proxy is the doorman: many clients, one destination, exposure that grows with the building’s population because that’s what a doorman’s position structurally means.

04

The mechanism — same interception, opposite population

FORWARD PROXY   client configures it (or traffic is intercepted to it)
                sees: ONE client's requests, fanning OUT to MANY destinations
                typical job: egress filtering, client anonymization, caching for a client population
                compromise exposure: bounded by that one client's traffic — flat, doesn't grow with
                how many OTHER clients use other forward proxies

REVERSE PROXY   servers deploy it; clients never know it exists
                sees: MANY clients' requests, fanning IN to ONE backend (or small backend set)
                typical job: TLS termination, load balancing, WAF, caching for a server population
                compromise exposure: grows with EVERY client sending traffic to that backend — by
                construction, not by misconfiguration

Both are the identical mechanical trick — sit in the path, terminate or forward, optionally rewrite — and TIER1.md is explicit that the “which algorithm balances load” question belongs to load-balancing, not here. What belongs here is the one axis that actually flips between them: a forward proxy’s blast radius is bounded by its OWN client; a reverse proxy’s is bounded by its BACKEND’s entire client population.

06

The trade-off — concentration is a security trade, and it trades in both directions

Concentrating TLS termination is a real, worthwhile trade — it is just not the CPU trade it’s often sold as. Fewer certificates means fewer private keys that could leak, fewer expiry dates to track, and one hardened box to patch instead of a fleet — genuine wins. The cost side is exactly this lesson’s asymmetry: that one box’s compromise now exposes what used to require compromising every server individually.

A forward proxy shared across a growing client population trades the same way, in the opposite direction. Centralizing egress buys visibility and policy enforcement over every client’s outbound traffic — but the egress-ceiling numbers above show the shared IP’s port-tuple budget shrinking linearly as more clients join, the same NAT-exhaustion math ephemeral-port-exhaustion already covers, just applied to a box whose whole job is being the one shared address.

Neither trade is free, and neither is “more secure” in general — a reverse proxy concentrates backend-facing risk to reduce server-facing risk; a forward proxy concentrates policy control to gain visibility it pays for in port budget. The number that tells you which trade you’re actually making is the one this lesson computes: whose traffic converges here, and how much of it.

07

In the wild

The forward/reverse distinction is old enough to predate the web — RFC 1919, Classical versus Transparent IP Proxies (IETF, 1996), already draws the line this lesson computes a cost for: a “classical” proxy the client explicitly configures (forward) versus a “transparent” one intercepting traffic without the client’s knowledge, a distinction later reused almost unchanged when reverse proxies became the standard way to front a server fleet.

The NAT-shaped egress-ceiling math this lesson reuses from ephemeral-port-exhaustion is specified, not folklore — RFC 3022, Traditional IP Network Address Translator (Traditional NAT) (IETF, 2001), defines exactly the address-and-port-sharing behavior that turns a forward proxy’s client population into a shared tuple-space problem the moment they share one egress address.

Reverse-proxy TLS termination inherits every certificate-and-key concern certificates-and-revocation.mdx already computed — concentrating termination doesn’t just divide the issuance rate by N, it also concentrates revocation-gap exposure onto whichever mechanism (CRL, OCSP, stapled OCSP) that one proxy relies on, rather than spreading it across a fleet that might not all fail the same way at once.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A forward proxy and a reverse proxy are the same mechanism (intercept, inspect, forward) pointed in opposite directions — a forward proxy serves a KNOWN client talking to MANY destinations it doesn't control; a reverse proxy serves MANY clients it doesn't know, talking to a backend it does control. The direction of "who configured it" is the entire distinction, not anything about the protocol
  • Concentrating TLS termination at one reverse proxy does NOT save CPU — `serverLoad`'s cost is linear in handshake rate, so N servers each terminating their own share costs exactly the same total cores as one proxy terminating all of it (0.99 cores either way at 1,000 handshakes/s × 20 servers). What concentration actually buys is fewer private keys: 20 servers each needing their own certificate becomes 1 certificate, a 20x reduction in what could leak
  • A reverse proxy's connection pool is a real compression ratio, not a metaphor — Little's Law says 2,000 requests/s at a 50ms hold needs 100 concurrent client-facing connections, and a 20-connection backend pool serves all of them: a 5x compression between what clients see and what the backend has to hold open
  • A forward proxy hiding client IPs behind one shared egress address is a NAT gateway, and pays a NAT gateway's tax: the port-tuple ceiling collapses from "ports × clients × destinations" to "ports × destinations" the moment clients share one IP — a 20x shrink at 20 clients, and it gets worse linearly with more clients, not better
  • The asymmetry that matters most for "what can a compromise see": a compromised forward proxy's exposure is bounded by ONE client's traffic and does NOT grow with how many other clients also use that forward proxy. A compromised reverse proxy's exposure grows with EVERY client sending traffic to the backend it fronts — at 20 clients sharing both, the reverse proxy's blast radius is already 20x the forward proxy's, and that gap widens with population, not with anything either proxy does differently

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A platform team is deciding whether to terminate TLS at 50 individual application servers or concentrate it at one reverse proxy in front of all 50. An engineer argues for concentration specifically to "reduce the CPU spent on crypto." Is that the right justification?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it's the wrong justification for a decision that might still be right. Total TLS termination CPU is the sum of `cpu-per-handshake × handshakes`, and that product doesn't care whether it's computed in 50 small increments or 1 large one — the total handshake rate the traffic represents is unchanged by where you terminate it, so the total cores spent are the same either way. The real argument for concentration is that 50 certificates (and 50 private keys that could each independently leak) become 1 — a security-surface argument, not a compute-cost one. Justifying the migration on CPU savings sets an expectation that won't show up in the metrics, which tends to make the actual, real benefit look like it didn't deliver either.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A security review flags that a company's forward proxy (used for employee outbound web access) and reverse proxy (fronting the public API) are both single points of compromise, and recommends treating them with equal urgency. Given what each can see, is that the right call?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not if "equal urgency" means "equally large blast radius" — it usually doesn't. The forward proxy's compromise exposure is bounded by whatever traffic passes through it from the employees who use it in a given window; the reverse proxy's exposure is bounded by traffic from EVERY client of the public API in that same window, which for most companies is many times larger and growing with usage, not headcount. Both deserve monitoring and fast detection — but if forced to prioritize one migration to a hardened, isolated deployment first, the mechanism with the traffic that scales with the whole public client base is the one whose compromise is worse by construction, not by configuration mistake.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `mtls.mdx` computes that mutual TLS's certificate-issuance rate is `serverOnly + mtls = certFleetIssuanceRate(serverCount) vs certFleetIssuanceRate(serverCount + clientCount)` — more certificates because more parties now authenticate. This lesson computes the opposite direction: concentrating termination at ONE reverse proxy shrinks `certFleetIssuanceRate`'s population from `serverCount` to `1`. Are these two lessons in tension?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — they're answering different questions with the same function, which is exactly why `certFleetIssuanceRate` is reused unchanged rather than rewritten. `mtls.mdx` asks "how many parties need to prove identity," and adding mutual authentication adds a party (the client), which can only grow the population. This lesson asks "given that servers need certificates, how many DISTINCT servers hold one," which concentration can shrink independently of whether mTLS is in use at all. A reverse proxy terminating mTLS on behalf of a server fleet gets both effects at once: mTLS grows the population by adding clients, concentration shrinks it back down by collapsing the server side to 1 — the same formula, applied to two independent levers.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: the same interception point can be positioned to serve the party making requests (visible to many destinations, invisible fan-out) or the party receiving them (visible to many sources, invisible fan-in) — and a compromise of the second kind exposes strictly more, because it aggregates traffic nobody chose to route through a single point on purpose. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A building's doorman versus a single resident's personal assistant. A personal assistant filters and forwards ONE resident's outgoing mail and calls to however many recipients that resident deals with — a forward proxy, scoped to one client, fanning out to many destinations, and its exposure if the assistant were compromised is bounded by that one resident's correspondence, however wide. A building's doorman, by contrast, is where deliveries from EVERY courier serving EVERY resident converge before reaching anyone — a reverse proxy, unknown to the couriers, fronting the whole building. A compromised doorman (or a doorman who simply gossips) has seen every package addressed to everyone, not because deliveries were routed through him on purpose by any single resident, but because the building's own layout made him the one point every delivery necessarily passes — exactly why a reverse proxy's blast radius scales with the population behind it and a forward proxy's does not.
    Did you have that?
