Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all Hedged requests explained well nowhere else

Five percent more traffic took slow requests from 63% to 1.6%, and at 92% load the same switch made them worse

A hedge fires only when the first attempt has already crossed the 95th percentile, so it costs 5% more requests rather than double — and once it has fired the leg is the minimum of two draws, so the tail gets squared. Both halves stop being true at the same moment: when the slowness has a shared cause, or when the extra 5% is what saturated the system. The break-even is 1/(1 + 2e), which is 91% utilisation for a p95 hedge.

Read first — tail-at-scale

01

The fix worked, shipped, and six weeks later caused the outage

Same search service as the previous lesson: one query, 100 shards, and the user waits for the slowest. Each shard is slow 1% of the time, so 63.4% of searches are slow. The replica set runs at 70% utilisation on an ordinary day.

The team applies the fix from the paper. If a shard has not answered by the time 95 shards in a hundred normally would, send the same read to a replica and take whichever answer arrives first. It costs 5% more requests. Slow searches go from 63.4% to 1.6%. The dashboard is unrecognisable and the change is thirty lines.

Six weeks later a promotion pushes traffic up and the replica set spends the afternoon at 92% utilisation. What happens next is not that hedging stops helping:

  • At 92% load, before any hedging, a shard is slow 14.1% of the time — because a queue’s tail is set by its headroom, and the headroom went from 30% to 8%.
  • Hedging adds 5% more requests, so offered load is 96.6%, and a shard is now slow 60.6% of the time.
  • Hedging made the tail 4.3× worse than not hedging at all, and the incident channel spent two hours looking for the shard that had broken. None of them had.

Nothing was misconfigured. The deadline was still the p95, the overhead was still 5%, the mechanism still squared whatever it was given — and squaring a much worse number gives a much worse number. The same switch took slow searches from 63.4% to 1.6% in the morning, a 39× improvement, and made the per-leg tail 4.3× worse in the afternoon. The only thing that changed was a utilisation figure that appears nowhere in the decision to turn it on.

02

Two beliefs, and the rig breaks the second one

The first belief is that a hedge is a duplicate request, so hedging doubles your load. That one is straightforwardly wrong and the arithmetic is one line. The hedge is not sent with the request; it is sent only if the first attempt is still outstanding when the deadline passes. If the deadline is the p-th percentile of the leg’s own latency, then by the definition of a percentile exactly (100 − p)% of attempts are still outstanding there. So:

extra requests = 1 − p

Hedge at the p95 and you send 5% more requests. At the p99, 1%. At the p99.9, one in a thousand. And nothing about the shape of the distribution enters, which is why Dean and Barroso state the rule as a percentile rather than in milliseconds — a millisecond figure is right for one service on one day.

The second belief is the one that costs you an outage: that because 5% is cheap, hedging is close to free. The 5% is not the price. The price is 5% more load on the system that was already the reason you have a tail, and the rig below is where that becomes visible — because it draws the thing every other treatment of hedging omits, which is the line for not hedging at all.

Predict You send a second copy of any request that has not answered by its 95th-percentile latency, and take whichever answer comes back first. How much extra traffic does that generate?
The hedge fires only for an attempt that is still outstanding at the deadline, and the deadline is the 95th percentile — so by the definition of a percentile exactly 5% of attempts fire one. Extra requests = 1 − p. The last option is the interesting wrong answer: the cost is distribution-free, which is the entire reason to specify the deadline as a percentile instead of in milliseconds. Measured at 4.96% over 12,000 fan-outs, across four different backend distributions, in fanout.test.mjs.
Show it without answering

Four things to do with it.

Start at 70% utilisation and drag the deadline. From p99.9 to p95 the tail barely moves (0.0101% → 0.0164% per leg) and the bill goes from 0.1% to 5%. From p95 to p75 the bill goes to 25% and the tail gets twenty times worse — 0.332% — because by then the hedging is adding enough load to matter. The good region is wide and it is all on the expensive-deadline side, which is the opposite of the intuition that a more aggressive hedge catches more stragglers.

Walk utilisation up and watch the breaks even at readout. At 70% it says p70.7 — every deadline on the slider helps. At 90% it says exactly p95, so the deadline the paper recommends is sitting precisely on the break-even, which is the M/M/1 result 1/(1 + 2e) = 90.9% showing up in a different model. At 92% it says p96.7 and your p95 setting is now on the wrong side of it: the amber dot is inside the shaded region. Nothing about the deadline changed. The headroom did.

Set slowness shared to 100%. The amber curve now sits above crimson at every deadline, both break-even readouts say never, and the verdict says why. A second copy of a request cannot outrun a bottleneck it shares. Then back it off to 10% — still “mostly independent” by any reasonable reading — and the residual tail is 0.143% instead of 0.0164%, for exactly the same 5%.

Watch the load bar, not the percentage. 5% extra requests is 3.5 points of utilisation at 70% load and 4.6 points at 92%. The same percentage is a rounding error in one case and the last of your headroom in the other, and the readout that matters is the one against the saturation line.

03

The intuition: order a second taxi, but only when the first one is late

You order a taxi. Nineteen times in twenty it arrives within eight minutes; the twentieth time the driver has gone to the wrong street and you are standing there at twenty minutes.

The fix is not to order two taxis every time. It is to wait eight minutes — the time nineteen in twenty arrive by — and then order a second one from a different company, and take whichever pulls up first. Your taxi bill goes up by one twentieth, because you only order the second one in the one-in-twenty case. And your worst case is now the case where both drivers get it wrong, which is much rarer than either one getting it wrong, in the same way that two coins both landing heads is rarer than one.

That is the whole technique, and it also contains its own failure. It works because the first taxi is late for a reason that belongs to that driver — wrong turn, wrong address, stopped for fuel. If instead the reason is that the whole city is gridlocked, then the second taxi is sitting in the same traffic, you have not improved your odds at all, and you have put one more car into the gridlock.

A hedge is a second draw from the same distribution, fired only when the first draw is already in its tail. Firing it late is what makes it cheap; the second draw being independent is what makes it work. The thing that made the first attempt slow is very often the thing that will make the second one slow too — and in that case the only effect left is the extra load.

04

The mechanism: the tail gets squared, and the bill is the tail's own frequency

Why the tail is squared rather than shaved

Once the hedge has fired, the leg does not finish when the primary finishes. It finishes at min(primary, deadline + replica). So for any threshold past the deadline, the leg is only still outstanding if the primary is outstanding and the replica is:

P(leg still slow at x) = S(x) · S(x − d)

S is the survival function, P(latency > x); d is the deadline. Two survival probabilities multiplied where there used to be one. In the two-state model of the previous lesson this is just q → q², and over a 100-way fan-out 1 − (1 − q)¹⁰⁰ falls from 63.4% to 1.0%. Asserted against a Monte Carlo in fanout.test.mjs, and against Dean and Barroso’s published BigTable measurement.

This is why hedging is a factor of 24 in the paper’s benchmark and not 24%. You are not making the tail thinner. You are multiplying it by itself. Cutting each replica’s own p99.9 in half would be an enormous engineering programme and would halve the tail; racing two replicas squares it, and costs 2% of your traffic.

Tied requests: bounding the waste by a message delay

That band is the reason Dean and Barroso’s second variant exists. A tied request goes to both replicas immediately, each copy tagged with the identity of the other, and whichever replica dequeues it first sends its twin a cancellation. Both replicas only execute if the second one starts before that cancellation lands — so the waste window is one cross-server message delay wide rather than one whole request long:

P(duplicated work) ≤ Δmsg / W

Δmsg is the one-way message delay, W the mean queue wait. For exponential waits the difference of two waits is Laplace, whose density never exceeds 1/(2W), and the window is 2·Δmsg wide. 0.5 ms of message delay against a 10 ms mean wait bounds the waste at 5% of one request — the exact figure is 4.53% — against 100% for an uncancelled duplicate. Checked two ways in fanout.test.mjs: the Laplace closed form against a simulation of two queues and a cancellation in flight.

The trade this makes is worth naming, because it is not free either: the servers now have to talk to each other about queue state, and the client has to know which two replicas it tied. Dean and Barroso add a further refinement — introduce a delay of twice the average message delay (1 ms in their cluster) before sending the second copy, which shrinks the window further at the cost of 1 ms on every request.

And the two things that undo all of it

q_hedged = ρq + (1 − ρ)q²

ρ is the fraction of slow episodes whose cause the replica shares. At ρ = 0 this is the textbook q². At ρ = 1 it is q exactly — hedging buys nothing and still costs 1 − p. It is a straight line between the two, so “mostly independent” does not mean “mostly squared”: at ρ = 0.1 the mixture gives 0.109%, eleven times q² and only nine times better than not hedging at all. (Slot 5’s table reads 0.143% for the same ρ because it also counts the load the hedge itself added.)

hedging helps iff u < 1/(1 + 2e), where e = 1 − p

M/M/1, and what cancels is the point. Sojourn time is exponential with mean S/(1−u), so the squared tail beats the original iff 2(1 − u(1+e)) > 1 − u. The threshold and the service time both drop out: the break-even depends on nothing but how much load the hedge adds. 90.9% for a p95 hedge, 50% for a p50 hedge.

That second one is the real reason to hedge late. The traffic bill is a footnote; where this line sits is not.

This is the column that decides whether hedging is worth building, and it is the one nobody measures. The oracle: reproducing the paper’s BigTable measurement

Dean and Barroso publish three numbers for one benchmark: 1,000 keys read from a BigTable spread over 100 servers, hedged after 10 ms, took the 99.9th percentile for retrieving all 1,000 values from 1,800 ms to 74 ms while sending just 2% more requests. Two of those calibrate a model and the third grades it.

Fitting a Pareto read latency so that the unhedged p99.9 of the maximum of 1,000 reads is 1,800 ms gives a tail index of α = 1.84 — which is itself a check, because a tail index near 2 is what a real storage read has. Then, with nothing fitted to the answer:

Two things came out of that check. The first is a consistency result the paper does not state: its chosen 10 ms deadline and its quoted 2% cost agree with each other through 1 − p — the deadline that costs exactly 2% is 8.4 ms, right next to the 10 ms they used. The paper’s two independent measurements pin each other.

The second is a disagreement, and it is the useful one. The model is optimistic by a factor of 1.55, and it is optimistic in exactly the direction its one assumption predicts: it treats the two attempts as perfectly independent draws, while the real benchmark puts 1,000 keys on 100 servers — so ten keys share a server, and a server having a bad moment is slow for several of them at once. Partial correlation un-squares part of the tail. Slot 6’s failure mode is visible as a residual in slot 4’s arithmetic, which is a better argument for taking it seriously than any amount of prose.

Halving the mean wait doubles the fraction of work that gets duplicated, because the two replicas now start closer together in real time relative to how long the cancellation takes to arrive.

Practical thresholds

  • Below about 70% utilisation, hedge at the p99 and stop thinking about it. 1% more traffic, the tail squared, and the break-even is 40 points of load away.
  • Between 70% and 85%, hedge at the p95 and put a budget on it. This is the paper’s recommendation and the region it is right in.
  • Above 90%, do not hedge — fix the load. The break-even for a 5% hedge is 90.9% and every model in this lesson agrees within two points. Above it you are spending traffic to make the tail worse.
  • If you cannot show that slow episodes are independent across replicas, assume they are not. ρ = 0.1 costs eight times the residual tail; ρ = 1 costs you the whole technique.

The trade-off: you bought independence, and you paid in headroom

Two tail models from opposite ends of the tail-weight range — an exponential M/M/1 sojourn and a Pareto tail with the index the BigTable calibration produced — and at the p95 they agree to within a point. That agreement is why about 90% is worth quoting as a number and not merely as a shape. Note also that they bracket each other in different directions at the two ends, so neither is the conservative one; the honest reading is “the break-even for a p95 hedge is around 90% and you should not need it to three digits”.

You gave up headroom, and headroom is what you were short of. Every other cost in this lesson is a footnote next to this one. The extra 5% is not spent on a healthy system; it is spent at the exact moment the system is loaded, because a loaded system is why you have a tail. This is the same closed loop as retry-storms — a load-dependent failure met with a response that adds load — and the fact that this version is usually stable does not make it a different mechanism. It makes it a mechanism whose failure mode is rarer and therefore un-rehearsed.

You gave up idempotency, or the ability to hedge. Two copies of a read are two copies of a read. Two copies of a write, of a counter increment, of a card charge, are an incident. Hedging is a read-path technique until you have made the operation idempotent, and “the client retries anyway, so it must be safe” is not the same statement — a retry is sequential and a hedge is concurrent, so a hedge can also produce two interleaved executions of an operation that is only safe run twice in series.

You gave up a clean answer to “how loaded is this service?” Offered load is now a function of your own tail, which is a function of offered load. A traffic increase raises the tail, which raises the hedge rate, which raises offered load. The gain of that loop is small at 70% and it is not small at 92%, which is why the failure arrives suddenly rather than gradually — and it is why every production implementation of this pattern has a budget on it, not just a deadline. gRPC throttles hedged RPCs from the same token bucket as retries for exactly this reason.

And you gave up nothing at all on the shard that was slow. A hedge does not make replica A faster; it routes around it. The compaction is still running, the GC pause still happened, the hot key is still hot. That matters because a hedged tail is an invisible tail: the shard is still misbehaving, the user no longer notices, and nobody fixes it — until the day the hedge stops working and the underlying problem arrives all at once, at 92% utilisation. This is the same trap as a contained failure behind a bulkhead, and it is the reason hedging belongs next to a measurement of how often it fires rather than on its own.

The honest summary: hedging is the best cost-to-benefit ratio in this curriculum and it is a load-amplifying feedback loop with a break-even you cannot see from inside the service. Both are true, they are true of the same mechanism, and the previous lesson’s other two options — reduce the fan-out, or stop waiting and return partial results — cost parallelism and completeness instead and have no such loop in them.

07

In the wild

The paper, and its two variants. Dean and Barroso’s The Tail at Scale is the origin of both. Hedged requests: the BigTable benchmark above — 1,800 ms to 74 ms at the p99.9 for 2% more requests. Tied requests: the client enqueues on two servers, each tagged with the other, and the paper’s Table 2 measures BigTable reads with a 1 ms tie delay — on a mostly idle cluster p99 goes 67 ms to 42 ms and p999 98 ms to 61 ms, and with a concurrent terasort competing for the same machines p99 goes 108 ms to 67 ms and p999 159 ms to 108 ms. The improvement is larger under contention, which is the whole point and also the tension with this lesson’s slot 6: contention is when stragglers happen, and it is also when the spare capacity is gone.

gRPC ships this as configuration, including the budget. A service config can carry a hedgingPolicy with maxAttempts and hedgingDelay; the first RPC goes immediately and further ones follow at each hedgingDelay interval. The part worth copying is retryThrottling: a token bucket with maxTokens and tokenRatio where every failure decrements by one and every success adds tokenRatio, and when the bucket falls below half, hedged RPCs stop being sent at all. That is this lesson’s break-even implemented as a circuit breaker on the hedge itself, and it is the single most important thing to have before turning hedging on.

Envoy does it as a retry variant. hedge_on_per_try_timeout on a route or virtual host issues the retry when the per-try timeout fires without resetting the original request, so both are in flight and the first response wins. It needs a retry policy with a num_retries and a per_try_timeout alongside it, which means the per-cluster max_retries circuit breaker from the cascading-failure lesson is already the budget.

Cassandra’s speculative_retry is Dean’s rule as a table property — and its history is this lesson’s failure mode. The option takes 99PERCENTILE, ALWAYS, NONE, or a fixed millisecond value, and the driver-side equivalents are the DataStax PercentileSpeculativeExecutionPolicy and ConstantSpeculativeExecutionPolicy. Cassandra 4.0 added a hybrid MIN(99percentile, 50ms) form (CASSANDRA-14293), and the motivation is precisely the trap in slot 6: when one host goes bad it drags the percentiles up, so a p99 deadline computed from the current distribution moves out with the distribution and the speculation stops firing in time. A threshold defined relative to the thing it measures stops being a threshold exactly when that thing degrades — which is why the fix is an absolute cap alongside the percentile.

Batch systems call it speculative execution and it is the same arithmetic. Spark’s spark.speculation relaunches a task when it has run longer than spark.speculation.multiplier (default 1.5) times the median of completed tasks, and only once spark.speculation.quantile (default 0.75) of the stage’s tasks have finished — a quantile-based deadline and a straggler multiplier, which is Dean’s rule with different nouns. MapReduce’s mapreduce.map.speculative is the older version of the same idea.

When the theory of “should I send a redundant copy” was written down properly. Vulimiri et al., Low Latency via Redundancy (CoNEXT 2013) treats the general question — when does a redundant request reduce latency and when does the added load make things worse — and the answer turns on how loaded the system already is. That is this lesson’s slot 6, done as queueing theory rather than as two closed forms and a rig.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Hedging at percentile p sends 1 − p extra requests, because only an attempt that crosses p fires a hedge — and that is true whatever the latency distribution is
  • Once the hedge has fired the leg finishes at the minimum of two attempts, so past the deadline P(leg still slow) = S(x)·S(x − d): the tail gets squared, not shaved
  • A tied request bounds the duplicated work by one cross-server message delay rather than by a whole duplicate — waste is at most Δmsg / mean queue wait, 5% at 0.5 ms against 10 ms
  • Correlated slowness turns q² back into q linearly: q_hedged = ρq + (1 − ρ)q², so 10% shared causation costs an order of magnitude of the residual tail
  • Hedging adds 1 − p to offered load, so on a saturated system it deepens the tail it was sent to fix; the M/M/1 break-even is u = 1/(1 + 2e), independent of the threshold
  • A deadline expressed as a percentile of the current distribution stops firing usefully when everything is slow, because the distribution moved and took the deadline with it

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A colleague objects that hedging every request doubles your load. What is the real number, and why does it not depend on what the latency distribution looks like?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    1 − p. The hedge is not sent with the request; it is sent only if the first attempt is still outstanding at the deadline, and if the deadline is the p-th percentile then by the definition of a percentile exactly (100 − p)% of attempts are still outstanding there. Hedge at the p95 and you send 5% more requests; at the p99, 1%. Nothing about the shape of the distribution enters — which is precisely why Dean and Barroso state the rule as "after the 95th-percentile expected latency" rather than "after 20 milliseconds". A millisecond figure is right for one service on one day; the percentile fixes the bill forever.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Hedging takes a 1% per-leg slow rate to 0.01%. Where does the square come from, and why is that a bigger deal than a 2× improvement would be?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Once the hedge has fired the leg finishes at min(primary, deadline + replica), so for a threshold past the deadline both attempts have to be slow: P(leg > x) = S(x)·S(x − d), a product of two survival probabilities where there used to be one. That is why the effect is a factor of 24 and not 24% — you are not making the tail thinner, you are multiplying it by itself. Over a 100-way fan-out, 1 − (1 − q)¹⁰⁰ goes from 63.4% to 1.0%.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    A tied request goes to two replicas at once. Why is the duplicated work a few percent rather than 100%?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because each copy is tagged with the identity of the other, so whichever replica dequeues it first cancels its twin. The only way both execute is for the second to start before that cancellation arrives, which makes the waste window one cross-server message delay wide rather than one request long. For exponential queue waits the difference of two waits is Laplace, whose density never exceeds 1/(2W), so waste ≤ Δmsg / W — half a millisecond of message delay against a 10 ms mean wait is 4.5% of one request, against 100% for an uncancelled duplicate.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Your service runs at 92% utilisation and you switch on p95 hedging. Predict what happens, and give the condition that decides it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It gets worse. The extra 5% takes offered load to 96.6%, and a queue's tail is set by its headroom, so the per-leg slow rate rises from 14.1% to 60.6% — quadratic suppression of a much worse number is a much worse number. The condition, in M/M/1, is u < 1/(1 + 2e) where e is the extra load: sojourn time is exponential with mean S/(1−u), so the squared tail beats the original iff 2(1 − u(1+e)) > 1 − u. The threshold and the service time both cancel, which means the break-even depends on nothing but how much load the hedge adds — 90.9% at p95, 50% at p50.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Cassandra 4.0 added a MIN(99percentile, 50ms) form of its speculative-retry setting, having previously offered a bare percentile. What failure was that fixing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A percentile deadline is computed from the current latency distribution, so when a node goes bad the distribution shifts and drags the deadline out with it. The hedge still fires for 1% of reads, but 1% of a distribution that has moved — it now fires after the damage rather than before it, which is the one regime in which hedging does nothing at all. Capping the deadline with an absolute floor keeps it inside the healthy latency range. The general lesson: a threshold defined relative to the thing it is measuring stops being a threshold exactly when that thing degrades.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A mobile client fetches its config blob from an edge endpoint. p50 is 60 ms, p99 is 400 ms, p999 is 4 seconds, and the app is unusable until the blob lands. You propose firing a second request to a different edge POP after 400 ms. Price it, then say what single measurement would tell you whether to ship it.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Cost: 1% more requests, because 400 ms is the p99 and only the 1% of fetches still outstanding there fire a hedge. Benefit: the fetch now finishes at min of two draws, so the 1-in-1,000 four-second case becomes roughly 1-in-100,000 — the tail is squared, not shaved. The measurement that decides it is whether the two POPs' slow episodes are correlated, because the whole benefit is the independence: if the p999 is caused by the client's own radio, its DNS, or a congested transit link that both POPs sit behind, then ρ ≈ 1, q² collapses back to q, and you have bought 1% more traffic and nothing else. Check it by looking at whether slow fetches from the same client are simultaneous across POPs, not by looking at either POP's own percentiles — each of them is telling the truth about itself, which is the tail-at-scale problem one level up.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: when completion time is a random draw and you can obtain a second draw that is independent of the first, racing the two and taking whichever finishes first replaces the tail with its own square — and the bill is only the frequency of the tail itself, not the cost of a whole second attempt. Independence is the entire product, and shared causation is what destroys it. Where else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Applying to several jobs or submitting to several journals at once — the win is real because rejections are largely independent, and it evaporates in a recession because then they are not. Booking two flights on different airlines through different hubs when weather is the risk, which fails when the weather is the shared cause. Sending an ambulance to the two nearest hospitals and standing one down on arrival — Dean's tied request exactly, cancellation included. Two independent suppliers for one component, which is worth nothing if both are downstream of the same fab. RAID reading from either mirror, and speculative execution in MapReduce and Spark. In every case the same three questions decide it: is the second draw actually independent, does the loser get stood down, and is the extra capacity available at the moment I need it — because that is exactly the moment the system is busy.
    Did you have that?
