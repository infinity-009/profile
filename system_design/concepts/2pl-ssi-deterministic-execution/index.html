Tier 3 · Storage and state 2PL, SSI, deterministic execution

SSI wins below 24% contention, 2PL wins next, and past 67.5% a fixed up-front toll beats both

Serializable execution has three real, shipping mechanisms, not one — a database can enforce "runs as if transactions ran one at a time" by making transactions wait (two-phase locking), by letting them run free and aborting one at commit time if it turns out unsafe (serializable snapshot isolation), or by refusing to let it be ambiguous in the first place (deciding the execution order before anything runs). This lesson prices all three the same way — cost per transaction against one shared variable, how often transactions overlap — and finds the contention level where the optimistic bet stops paying off.

Read first — lock-taxonomy-and-granularitymvcc

01

Same guarantee, three prices, and a crossing point

Ask three different database engineers how their system guarantees serializable transactions and you can get three genuinely different answers, none of them wrong. One system makes transactions wait. Another lets them run free and occasionally throws one away. A third makes sure the question of “who goes first” is settled before anyone starts running. All three produce exactly the same correctness guarantee — the database behaves as if every transaction ran alone, one after another — and this lesson prices all three against the one variable that actually decides which is cheapest: how often transactions overlap in time at all.

02

Assume the fastest-sounding one is just better

The instinct, on hearing “SSI never blocks anyone,” is to treat it as a strictly superior upgrade over locking — no waiting, what’s not to like? The gap in that instinct is that SSI isn’t free of cost, it’s cost of a different shape: instead of paying steadily as contention rises, it pays almost nothing right up until it pays for an entire wasted transaction, and how often that happens scales with the same contention that 2PL pays for more gradually.

Predict A transaction touches 150 of 1,000,000 rows, taking 20ms of real work either way. Under 2PL it holds row-level locks for that whole 20ms; under SSI it runs lock-free and gets aborted-and-retried if a dangerous conflict is detected. As contention (how often another transaction overlaps this one in time) rises from 5% toward 90%, what happens to which mechanism is cheaper?
SSI's cost is baseExecMs/(1 − abortRate) — near the base cost when aborts are rare, but growing WITHOUT BOUND as the abort rate climbs, because every abort re-runs the full transaction and can be aborted again. 2PL's cost is baseExecMs plus an expected wait capped at one lock's hold duration — it grows too, but it can never exceed baseExecMs + one full hold. At low contention SSI's near-zero overhead wins; past a workload-specific crossover (roughly 24% contention for this row-level example) 2PL's bounded cost wins instead, because SSI's unbounded tail has caught up and kept climbing.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — 20% contention, row-level 2PL locking on a transaction touching 150 of 1,000,000 rows — 2PL costs 25.5ms and SSI costs 25ms, nearly tied, and SSI is (barely) the cheapest of the three. Pull contention down to 5% and SSI’s edge widens: 21.1ms against 2PL’s 22.5ms. Push contention up to 90% and SSI has fallen far behind — 200ms against 2PL’s 39.5ms, a 5.1× gap, because its abort rate has climbed enough that most transactions pay for a full wasted redo — but 2PL hasn’t won either: deterministic execution’s flat 35ms has quietly become the cheapest option of all three, because its fixed 15ms ordering toll never moves while both of the other two climb past it.

03

A one-lane bridge, three ways to run it

Picture a single-lane bridge two directions of traffic both need. 2PL is a traffic light: one direction gets a green light and holds the lane until it’s done crossing; the other direction waits, patiently, for exactly as long as the crossing takes. The wait is real and grows with traffic, but it’s bounded — nobody waits longer than one crossing’s worth of time, and if the light logic is sound, nobody ever collides. (If two lights somehow both went green at once, that’s the bridge’s own deadlock — the subject of the very next lesson, not this one.)

SSI is an unsignaled bridge where drivers just go when it looks clear. Almost always, nobody’s coming the other way, and crossing costs nothing beyond the crossing itself. Rarely, two drivers meet in the middle — and when they do, one of them has to reverse the entire way back off the bridge and try again from scratch, which costs far more than the traffic light’s wait ever did, for exactly the crossings unlucky enough to collide.

Deterministic execution is a posted timetable, agreed before any car sets off: vehicle 1 crosses at 9:00, vehicle 2 at 9:01, and so on. Building that timetable takes a coordination meeting up front — nobody moves until it’s settled — but once it exists, nobody waits or reverses at the bridge itself, ever, regardless of how much traffic the timetable is scheduling. The catch: the schedule needs to know how long each vehicle’s crossing will take before it’s built — a vehicle that won’t know its own crossing time until it’s halfway across doesn’t fit a timetable at all.

04

The mechanism: one shared input, three cost shapes

Every mechanism here answers the same question — “how likely is it that two transactions active at the same time actually fight over the same data?” — call that contention. What differs is what each mechanism does once contention produces a real conflict.

2PL       Take locks as you go; hold every one until commit (that's "two-phase": a growing
          phase that only acquires, then a shrinking phase that only releases — never a new
          acquire after the first release). A conflict makes the loser WAIT for the winner's
          entire remaining hold duration. Cost: steady, bounded by one hold duration.

SSI       Take no locks; run against a free MVCC snapshot. At commit, check for a "dangerous"
          read-write pattern between concurrent transactions (a cycle of rw-dependencies).
          If found, ABORT one side; it must redo its FULL execution. Cost: near-zero when
          conflicts are rare, unbounded as they approach certainty.

Calvin    Agree on the total execution order BEFORE any transaction runs (a consensus/ordering
          step across the transactions about to execute). Every replica then executes that
          exact order deterministically — no locks, no commit-time checks, because there is
          nothing left to negotiate. Cost: a fixed up-front toll, then near-zero, flat with
          contention. Requires each transaction's read/write set to be knowable in advance.

SSI leads below ~24% contention; 2PL takes over from there; but 2PL’s own cost keeps climbing too, and at 67.5% contention its steady, bounded bill reaches 35ms — exactly deterministic execution’s flat cost — so past that point deterministic execution is cheapest of all three, not because it got any cheaper, but because everything else got more expensive around its fixed price.

06

The trade-off — none of these three is free, and none is free the same way

2PL’s bill is steady and you always pay something, even at zero contention. Every lock taken is a lock-manager object with its own acquire/release bookkeeping cost, and every lock held blocks whoever else wants it for the lock’s entire remaining life — bounded, predictable, and paid whether or not a conflict was ever likely. lock-taxonomy-and-granularity.mdx already priced the bookkeeping-versus-collateral-blocking trade inside this bill; this lesson adds the missing half — what that bill costs in wait time as contention actually rises, and it never resolves what happens when two of 2PL’s own waits meet in a cycle: that’s a real, structural vulnerability (deadlock) this lesson only names, and deadlocks prices next.

SSI’s bill is nearly free right up until it isn’t. Reusing an MVCC snapshot instead of taking locks means near-zero overhead exactly while conflicts stay rare — which, in most real workloads, they do, and that’s why SSI is the default serializable mechanism in systems like PostgreSQL. But the model’s own numbers show the trade inverts past a workload-specific contention level, because an abort throws away the FULL transaction, not a partial cost, and that waste compounds with every retry.

Deterministic execution’s bill is a toll gate, not a meter. Pay the ordering/coordination cost once, up front, and the marginal cost of running — regardless of how contended the workload turns out to be — is close to zero, because there’s no negotiation left to do during execution. The real cost isn’t latency at high contention; it’s that a transaction whose next read depends on a result it hasn’t seen yet can’t be pre-ordered this way at all without extra machinery (buffering, speculative re-execution, or restructuring it into a stored procedure whose logic is fully known up front) — an expressiveness constraint, not a performance one.

07

In the wild

Two-phase locking’s own definition — a lock acquired grows the transaction’s hold set, and nothing is released until commit or abort — is Kleppmann’s, not this lesson’s invention: Martin Kleppmann and Chris Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., ch. 8, pp. 314–315 (“Two-Phase Locking (2PL)”), with index-range/predicate locking (the mechanism lock-taxonomy-and-granularity.mdx already priced in full) on p. 317.

Serializable snapshot isolation is Cahill, Röhm, and Fekete’s algorithm, not a generic optimistic-concurrency idea — Michael J. Cahill, Uwe Röhm, and Alan D. Fekete, “Serializable Isolation for Snapshot Databases,” ACM SIGMOD, 2008 (also ACM Transactions on Database Systems, 2009), the paper DDIA cites as SSI’s original description (ch. 8, p. 318). It is what made “full serializability at close to snapshot-isolation’s performance” a shipped result rather than a theoretical one — PostgreSQL’s SERIALIZABLE isolation level implements it.

Deterministic execution at distributed scale is Calvin, not a hypothetical — Alexander Thomson, Thaddeus Diamond, Shu-Chun Weng, Kun Ren, Philip Shao, and Daniel J. Abadi, “Calvin: Fast Distributed Transactions for Partitioned Database Systems,” ACM SIGMOD, 2012. Calvin’s sequencing layer is exactly the “agree on the order before executing” step this lesson prices as orderingLatencyMs — a real, shipped design, not this lesson’s simplification of one.

The MVCC snapshot SSI runs against, for free, is mvcc.mdx’s mechanism, reused unchanged — the visibility rule that lets a reader see a consistent snapshot without taking a lock is priced there in full; this lesson only uses the fact that it exists and costs SSI nothing extra to rely on it.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • Three different, real mechanisms all produce the SAME guarantee (the database behaves as if every transaction ran alone, one after another) by three structurally different means: 2PL makes transactions WAIT for each other; SSI lets them run free and ABORTS one at commit if it turns out unsafe; deterministic execution removes the ambiguity before execution starts by fixing the order in advance.
  • 2PL is pessimistic and its cost is steady: a lock, once taken, is held until commit (never released early, never taking a new lock after releasing one — that's the two phases), so the cost of contention is bounded by one transaction's own hold duration, no matter how contended the workload gets.
  • SSI is optimistic and its cost is bimodal: near-zero overhead when the dangerous read-write pattern it watches for is rare, because it never takes a lock at all — it reuses the same MVCC snapshot mechanism `mvcc.mjs` already prices — but the aborted transaction's ENTIRE execution cost is wasted and must be redone, and that wasted-work cost grows without bound as conflicts get more common, unlike 2PL's bounded wait.
  • Quantified crossover: at 20% contention on a 150-of-1,000,000-row transaction, row-level 2PL costs 25.5ms and SSI costs 25ms — nearly tied. Below roughly 24% contention SSI is cheaper; above it, 2PL is, because SSI's cost keeps climbing (200ms at 90% contention) while 2PL's stays bounded by one lock's hold duration — but 2PL's own bounded cost still climbs with contention, and past 67.5% it exceeds deterministic execution's flat 35ms, so at very high contention neither locking scheme wins at all; the fixed up-front toll does.
  • Deterministic execution (Calvin-style) pays a fixed, up-front ordering/coordination cost to agree on execution order BEFORE any transaction runs, then pays near-zero marginal cost during execution — no locks to wait for, no conflicts to detect, because every replica deterministically produces the same result from the same pre-agreed order. This cost is flat with contention — a genuinely different shape from either of the other two, not just a cheaper version of one of them.
  • Deterministic execution's real constraint is not performance, it's expressiveness: the transaction's read/write set has to be knowable or boundable BEFORE execution starts, so it can be placed in the global order. An interactive transaction that decides what to read next based on a result it just read inside the SAME transaction doesn't fit this model without extra machinery — this is a genuine limitation, not a tuning knob.
  • 2PL's vulnerability to deadlock (two transactions each holding a lock the other one needs, neither able to proceed) is a fact this lesson names but does not resolve — detecting and breaking a deadlock, and the lock-ordering discipline that avoids it, is `deadlocks`'s territory, immediately next in this tier.

  1. Cued recall

    A teammate says "SSI is strictly better than 2PL — it never blocks anyone, so why would you ever choose locking?" What does this lesson's crossover number say is wrong with that claim?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    "Never blocks" is true but incomplete — SSI trades blocking for aborting, and an abort throws away the transaction's entire execution cost, which then has to be redone. At low contention that trade is nearly free (few conflicts, few aborts), but the model's own numbers show it inverts: at 20% contention on the worked example, 2PL and SSI cost about the same (25.5ms vs 25ms); at 90% contention, SSI costs 200ms against 2PL's 39.5ms — 2PL stays bounded because a lock's wait is capped at one hold duration, while SSI's wasted-retry cost has no such cap. SSI isn't strictly better; it's better exactly when conflicts are rare, which is a contention-dependent claim, not an unconditional one.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A Calvin-style deterministic system needs a client to submit a transaction as "transfer $50 from account A to account B" rather than "read A's balance, then decide how much to transfer based on what you see." Why does the mechanism itself force that restriction, rather than it being an arbitrary API rule?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because the whole cost saving comes from deciding the GLOBAL EXECUTION ORDER before any transaction runs, and every replica then executing that exact order deterministically — which requires knowing (or bounding) what each transaction will read and write before it executes. A transaction whose second read depends on the result of its first read inside the same transaction can't have its read/write set pinned down in advance; the ordering step would have nothing concrete to schedule. This isn't a policy choice bolted onto the mechanism — it's the mechanism's own precondition for working at all.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `lock-taxonomy-and-granularity.mdx` shows that a table-level lock blocks nearly the whole table as collateral damage, versus a row-level lock blocking nothing beyond what's touched. If a team migrates a hot transaction from table-level to row-level locking under 2PL, does this lesson's model say that always lowers the transaction's total cost?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Not always, and the model shows why: `twoPhaseLockingCost` has two terms that move in OPPOSITE directions with granularity. Coarser (table) locking inflates the conflict probability via collateral blocking — more of the table is blocked, so more overlapping transactions actually collide — but it needs only 1 lock object, near-zero bookkeeping. Finer (row) locking has zero collateral blocking, but the bookkeeping cost scales with the row count: for a transaction touching many rows, thousands of lock objects' worth of per-lock overhead can outweigh the blocking savings, which is exactly the pressure that makes `lock-taxonomy-and-granularity`'s escalation mechanism exist in the first place. Whether row-level wins depends on the workload's row count and contention level together, not on granularity alone — which is also why this lesson's crossover point itself SHIFTS when granularity changes (roughly 24% contention at row-level vs roughly 50% at table-level, in the worked example).
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: the same correctness guarantee can be delivered by making conflicting actors wait for each other, by letting them proceed and undoing the rare loser, or by removing the possibility of conflict by fixing everyone's order in advance — and each option trades a different kind of cost (steady blocking, occasional wasted work, up-front coordination) for the same outcome. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Managing a single-lane road bridge. Option one: a traffic light lets one direction cross at a time and the other direction WAITS — steady, predictable delay, worse as traffic (contention) rises, and if both lights ever malfunctioned "green" simultaneously you'd get the bridge's version of deadlock. Option two: an unsignaled bridge where drivers cross whenever they judge it clear, and on the rare occasion two meet in the middle, ONE reverses back off the bridge and waits — cheap when traffic is light, increasingly costly (someone reverses a full crossing's worth of distance) as traffic grows, exactly SSI's wasted-work shape. Option three: a scheduled timetable, agreed before anyone sets off, that assigns each vehicle a specific crossing slot — a coordination meeting has to happen up front, but once the schedule exists nobody waits or reverses at the bridge itself, provided every vehicle's crossing time was knowable when the schedule was made (a vehicle that doesn't know until the last second how long it needs to cross doesn't fit the timetable model at all).
    Did you have that?
