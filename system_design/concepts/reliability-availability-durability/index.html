Tier 0 · Why anything is hard at all The three guarantees explained well nowhere else

Two services both hold 99.99%, and one of them will have no incident all year while the other has 315

Availability, reliability and durability are not three grades of the same thing — they are measured in three different units, so a number in one says nothing about the other two. Availability has no time argument at all, which is why one SLO figure covers systems whose chance of an incident-free year differs by 136 orders of magnitude. And durability has no repair term, which is why eleven nines of it sits beside four nines of availability in the same published spec sheet, honestly.

Read first — availability-mathmtbf-mttr

01

Two SLOs that read identically, and the review that cannot tell the systems apart

Two teams present for the same review, and each brings one number.

  • Team A’s service fails about once every four and a half years. When it fails, a human is paged and it takes four hours.
  • Team B’s service fails 315 times a year — most weeks have several. Each one clears itself in ten seconds.

Both slides say 99.99%. Not approximately: both lose exactly 52.6 minutes a year, and the availability figures agree to every digit the slide has room for.

Here is what the slide does not say. Team A will probably have no incident at all this year — the probability is 80.3%. For Team B the probability of a year with no incident is 1.1 × 10⁻¹³⁷, a number with no physical interpretation other than never. The two systems differ by 136 orders of magnitude on a question the review never asked, and the number they both brought cannot express it, because availability has no time argument in it.

Then a third team presents. Their service holds 99.99% too, and in the last quarter it accepted 80 writes it later could not return. The client was told 200 OK. The data is not somewhere else; it is gone. Nothing in a percentage-of-uptime figure has anything to say about that either, and unlike the other two failures, no amount of waiting fixes it.

Three teams, one number, three different guarantees — and the number describes exactly one of them.

02

Add nines to the SLO, and watch the thing you were worried about not move

The instinct is that these are grades of one quantity: that four nines is pretty good and five nines is better, and a system with enough of them is reliable and durable as a consequence. So the fix for all three teams is one fix — raise the target.

Hold the availability target wherever you like in the rig below and drag repair time. The availability readout does not move a digit. The annual failure count multiplies by 1,440× across the slider’s range, and the incident-free-year probability crosses 136 orders of magnitude. Every point on that curve satisfies the same SLO, and there is no repair time at which the SLO complains.

Predict Two services both hold exactly 99.99% availability. One repairs in four hours, the other in ten seconds. Which is more likely to get through a whole year with no incident at all?
Availability fixes only the ratio, so at a fixed target MTBF = MTTR·A/(1−A) — the failure rate is proportional to 1/MTTR. A service that recovers in ten seconds has bought the right to fail 1,440× more often than one that takes four hours, and to hold the same percentage it must. Substituting into R(t) = exp(−t/MTBF) gives R = exp(−t(1−A)/(A·MTTR)): 80.3% against 1.1 × 10⁻¹³⁷. Faster repair at the same SLO is worse reliability, which is the opposite of what the SLO's single number suggests.
Show it without answering

Now the second half, for the third team. Their problem is data, so the instinct is again a single knob: add copies. Drag copies in the bottom band and it looks like it works — one copy is 2.0 nines, three is 11.25, six is 25.5. Then drag shared fate to one event per ten thousand years and every bar past the third is drawn with its tail cut off. At c = 10⁻⁴ the durability of three copies and the durability of six copies are both 4.0 nines, because the floor is −log₁₀(c) and no count reaches below it.

And the third team’s actual failure is not on the copy axis at all. Set copies before ack (W) to 1 and read the last readout: 80 acknowledged writes lost a year. Now raise copies — the number does not change. Raise W to 2 and it falls by nine orders of magnitude. The knob they needed was never the one the proposal was about.

03

Open, uninterrupted, and still holding your parcel

Think about a shop that also keeps things in a safe for customers.

Availability is whether the shop is open right now. It is a fraction of the year, and it says nothing about the pattern: a shop that closes for one long afternoon and a shop that closes for ninety seconds twice a day can be open the same fraction of the time.

Reliability is whether the shop will get through the year without ever unexpectedly closing. It is a question about an interval, and it has an obvious property availability does not: the longer the interval, the worse the answer, for every shop. Ask about a day and almost every shop passes. Ask about thirty years and none do. Nothing about the shop changed between those two questions.

Durability is whether the parcel you left in the safe is still there. And this is the one that is different in kind rather than in degree, because of what happens next after a violation. A closed shop opens again — that is what “closed” means. A lost parcel does not come back. So the shop’s whole arithmetic for opening hours has a term for how long until it reopens, and its arithmetic for the safe has no such term at all. That single missing term is why the two guarantees are priced, engineered and audited completely differently, and why a shop can honestly advertise both “open 99.99% of the time” and “nothing has ever been lost from the safe” without the two numbers being on the same scale.

04

Three formulas, and the one substitution that makes the SLO look silent

Three quantities, three units. The units are the fastest way to see they cannot be compared.

question it answersformulaunithas a time argument
availabilityright now, does it answer?A = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR)fraction of timeno
reliabilitywill it survive the next interval?R(t) = exp(−t/MTBF)probability over tyes
durabilityis the acknowledged data still there?1 − exp(−t/MTTDL)loss per object per yearyes

The duty cycle belongs to mtbf-mttr and the point taken from it here is one consequence: A depends on MTBF and MTTR only through their ratio. So fixing a target fixes the ratio and nothing else:

MBTF = MTTR · A/(1−A)

which means at a fixed SLO the failure rate is proportional to 1/MTTR. Substitute that into the reliability formula and the silence becomes an equation:

R(t) = exp( −t(1−A) / (A · MTTR) )

At a fixed availability target, reliability rises with repair time. Not “is unrelated to” — rises. A system that recovers in ten seconds must fail 1,440 times as often as one that takes four hours in order to hold the same percentage, and it does. That is the whole of slot 1 in one line, and it is derivable from two formulas neither of which mentions the other.

Durability’s formula has the same shape and one crucial absence. MTTDL for a shard on n copies is the mean first-passage time to all n gone at once, which is the k=1-of-n repairable chain that mtbf-mttr already solves — a copy under rebuild is “failed”, and the rebuild window is the exposure. Two things fall out:

  • Each extra independent copy adds a near-constant number of nines — 4.7 at a 1% AFR and a 12-hour window. Constant-per-copy is why eleven nines needs three copies and not thirty.
  • The rebuild window enters with an exponent of (n−1). Cutting it from 12 hours to 1 is worth 12× at two copies, 144× at three, 1,728× at four — and exactly 1× at one copy, where there is no window because there is nothing to be exposed to.

What durability’s formula does not have is a repair term for the loss itself. Availability’s MTTR says “and then it comes back”. There is no such factor, so the arithmetic spends its structure on independence and exposure instead — and that is not a modelling convenience, it is the difference between the two guarantees.

Two things worth pausing on. A single disk is two nines — calling one copy “durable storage” is a factor-of-a-hundred-per-year claim, and a fleet of ten thousand of them loses a shard every 3.65 days. And the mean time to data loss is a terrible unit: 1.78 × 10¹¹ years sounds like a guarantee about the heat death of the universe and it is eleven nines, which is a number a large fleet fails against constantly. Convert to loss-per-year before believing anything.

The published pair, reconciled. Three copies give 11.25 nines of durability. Put five serving hops in front of them at 2 × 10⁻⁵ unavailability each and the serving path is 4.0 nines, because a serial chain adds unavailability per hop however good the disks are. That is the spec sheet: eleven nines and four nines, seven orders apart, both honest, describing different layers of one system. The storage layer contributes 7.7 × 10⁻¹¹ of the total unavailability — so adding a fourth copy moves durability by 4.7 nines and moves availability by nothing you could measure in a century of operation.

The acknowledgement window, at 20,000 writes a second and 400 ms of propagation:

Two copies acking at W=2 beat three copies acking at W=1 by nine orders of magnitude, with less hardware. N does not appear in the per-write probability — the model asserts that a 30-copy store at W=1 has the identical figure to a 3-copy store — because the write only ever lived on W. And these are underestimates: they price the window at the disk failure rate, while the event that loses an unpropagated acknowledgement is a kernel panic or a rack losing power. At a once-a-year-per-node crash rate the 80 becomes 8,000.

And the floor. At a shared-fate probability of 10⁻⁴ per year, three copies deliver 4.0 nines instead of 11.25 — 7.25 nines bought and thrown away — and six copies deliver 4.0 as well. Below the floor, another copy is hardware that changes no guarantee. The rebuild window still works, because it does not need independence.

06

The trade-off — W is durability, N is availability, and you cannot raise both for free

Everything above makes durability sound like it is simply under-bought. It is not: the knob that moves it is the same knob that moves availability, in the opposite direction.

Raising W buys durability and spends availability. A write acknowledged at W=2 of 2 requires both nodes up, so the write path fails whenever either is down — strictly worse availability than W=1 of 3, which needs any one. This is the trade in one line: the acknowledgement rule is where durability and availability are in direct competition, and the replication factor is not. It is also why the standard answer is a majority rather than either extreme — W=2 of 3 has the durability of two copies and still tolerates one node down, which is what quorums is for.

Raising W also spends latency, on every single write. The ack now waits for the slowest of W nodes rather than the fastest of one, which is the fan-out tail from tail-at-scale applied to the write path. Durability is not bought with hardware; it is bought with latency on the common case to insure against an event that has not happened yet, which is the least popular kind of purchase in any planning meeting.

Improving reliability spends availability, at a fixed budget. The systems in the table with the best chance of a quiet year are the ones that fail rarely and take four hours to recover. If you want both — few failures and short ones — you are not trading, you are asking for a better ratio, and that costs the full amount. The trade only appears when the budget is fixed, and organisational budgets are always fixed.

And there is a real case for choosing low reliability. Team B’s 315 self-clearing ten-second failures are what a well-designed automated recovery looks like: it fails constantly and no human ever hears about it. Optimising Team B’s reliability figure would mean making failures rarer, which is capital, when the thing that actually protects users is already in place. So “improve reliability” is sometimes exactly the wrong instruction — and you cannot tell which case you are in from the availability number, which is where this lesson started.

The honest summary of the three is a table of what a violation costs, not what it measures:

violationrestored bybought with
availabilitya request failswaiting — the definition of MTTRredundancy and fast failover
reliabilitya failure occurs at allnothing — the interval already contains itfewer failures: testing, change control, fewer dependencies
durabilityan ack cannot be read back, evernothing at allindependent copies, a short rebuild window, a strict W

The middle column is the one that does the work. Two of the three have a repair term and one does not, and that is why durability is not availability with more nines.

07

In the wild

The seven-order gap in one spec sheet. Amazon publishes S3 Standard as designed for 99.999999999% durability and 99.99% availability — eleven nines beside four, in the same table, for the same product. Read as two grades of one thing that pairing is incoherent. Read as two layers it is exactly the arithmetic above: the copies at the bottom set the first number, the serving fleet in front sets the second, and the storage layer’s contribution to unavailability is around 10⁻¹¹. The reason the gap is stable across storage vendors is that it is structural, not competitive.

Correlation is the term that decides it, and it was measured. Ford, Labelle, Popovici, Stokely, Truong, Barroso, Grimes and Quinlan, Availability in Globally Distributed Storage Systems (OSDI 2010), instrumented Google’s storage fleet and found that failure bursts — correlated events taking many nodes at once — dominate the loss probability, to the point where the paper’s conclusion is that replication within a datacentre stops paying and multi-cell replication is what buys nines. That is the amber rule in the rig, found in production data rather than assumed: once c dominates, more copies of the same fate are not more copies.

A durability incident that looked like an availability incident. GitHub’s October 2018 outage began with a 43-second network partition and produced roughly 24 hours of degraded service. The availability arithmetic cannot explain that ratio at all — 43 seconds of partition should cost 43 seconds. What made it a day was that writes had been accepted on both sides of the partition, so the recovery was not “bring the service back” but “work out which acknowledged writes are authoritative”, and that is a durability-and-consistency problem wearing an availability problem’s clothes. The 43-second and 24-hour figures are the two units of this lesson, in one postmortem.

The disk numbers are public and worth reading against your own assumptions. Backblaze has published per-model annualised failure rates for its fleet quarterly for over a decade; the fleet-wide figure has generally sat in the low single-digit percent. Everything in the durability tables above uses 1% AFR, which is optimistic for some models and pessimistic for others — and the structure of the result (constant nines per copy, exponent n−1 on the window, a floor at c) does not depend on the value.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The three guarantees have three different units — a fraction of time, a probability over an interval, and a probability of loss per object per year — so a number in one cannot be compared to a number in another
  • Availability has no time argument, so it cannot express "will this get through the quarter", and reliability is the only one of the three that can

  • Each additional independent copy buys a near-constant 4.7 nines of durability, which is why eleven nines needs three copies rather than thirty

  1. Cued recall

    Two services both hold exactly 99.99% availability. One repairs in four hours, the other in ten seconds. Which is more likely to get through a year with no incident at all?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The four-hour one, and not narrowly — 80.3% against 1.1 × 10⁻¹³⁷. Availability fixes only the ratio MTTR/MTBF, so at a fixed target MTBF = MTTR·A/(1−A) and the failure rate is proportional to 1/MTTR. Substituting gives R(t) = exp(−t(1−A)/(A·MTTR)): at the same SLO, faster repair buys the right to fail more often, and it spends that right. The four-hour service fails 0.22 times a year, the ten-second service 315 times, and both lose exactly 52.6 minutes. The SLO is not wrong about either — it is silent, and what it is silent about is the thing an on-call rota, a change-freeze policy and a press office all actually care about.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Your object store reports 99.999999999% durability and 99.99% availability. Is one of those numbers marketing?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — they are set by different layers, which is why they can sit seven orders of magnitude apart in one honest spec sheet. Durability is set by the copies at the bottom: three independent copies of a 1%-AFR disk with a 12-hour rebuild window give a mean time to data loss of 1.8 × 10¹¹ years, which converts to 11.25 nines of annual loss probability. Availability is set by the serial chain in front — load balancer, front end, auth, metadata, router — and a serial chain adds unavailability per hop however good the disks are: five hops at 2 × 10⁻⁵ each gives 4.0 nines and the storage layer contributes 7.7 × 10⁻¹¹ of it. The consequence that matters in a design review: adding a fourth copy moves durability by 4.7 nines and moves availability by nothing measurable, so "add a replica" is an answer to only one of the two questions.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    You have three replicas and you acknowledge a write as soon as one of them has it. How durable is that write?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    As durable as one copy for the length of the propagation window, because N does not appear in the probability at all. The write lives on W copies until it propagates, and loss over that window is p_copy^W — so at W=1 with a 400 ms window and 20,000 writes a second you lose about 80 acknowledged writes a year, and the chance of a year with none is 1.8 × 10⁻³⁵. Two replicas acking at W=2 lose 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ — nine orders better with less hardware. And the default here is charitable: it prices the window at the disk failure rate, when the event that actually loses an unpropagated ack is a kernel panic or a rack losing power, one to two orders more frequent. N buys availability, W buys durability, and quoting the replication factor answers the wrong one.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Durability is short of target. You can add copies or make rebuilds faster. Which, and why is one of them not a choice at all?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Both work, they are priced very differently, and one of them stops working. Each extra independent copy buys a near-constant 4.7 nines, which is the reason eleven nines is reachable with three copies. But the rebuild window enters with an exponent of (n−1), so cutting it from 12 hours to 1 hour is worth 144× at three copies — 2.16 nines — and it is the lever that does not need independence, which is the one that survives contact with reality. Copies stop working the moment a shared-fate term appears: at c = 10⁻⁴ per year the floor is 4.0 nines whatever the count, so three copies give 11.25 nines on paper and 4.0 in fact, and six copies give 25.5 on paper and still 4.0. Twelve copies of one deploy, one config push, one bad bucket policy is one copy.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    Why can availability not answer "will this survive the quarter", however many nines it has?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because it has no time argument. Availability is MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR), a dimensionless ratio describing an instant — "right now, does it answer?" — and no amount of it says anything about an interval. Reliability is the one with the argument: R(t) = exp(−t/MTBF), which falls monotonically towards zero as the horizon grows no matter how good the system is. At an MTBF of one year, reliability is 90% over 923 hours, 50% over 8.3 months, and 10% over 2.3 years. That is not pessimism, it is the definition, and it is why "five nines" is not an answer to "can we freeze changes for the quarter" or "will this survive its five-year service life".
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A team is asked to make their message queue "more reliable" after an incident where the queue was up the whole time but 40,000 published messages were never delivered. They propose moving from three brokers to five and adding a second availability zone. Evaluate the proposal with this lesson.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The proposal buys availability, and the incident was a durability failure. Those are different units and this fix does not touch the one that broke.

    Start from what was lost. A message accepted and never delivered is an acknowledged write that did not survive — the exact quantity ackWindowLoss describes. Ask one question: how many brokers had the message when the publisher was told it succeeded? If the answer is one, then N was never the variable. Loss over the propagation window is p^W and N does not appear in it; going three brokers to five leaves the per-message probability identical to the last digit, and the model asserts that directly. Raising the publish acknowledgement from W=1 to W=2 is what moves it, by nine orders of magnitude, and it can be done without buying anything.

    Then price what W costs, because it is not free. A write that needs two of two brokers up fails whenever either is down, so W=2 of 2 is strictly worse availability than W=1 of 3 — the trade this lesson is about, in one line. The shape that gets both is a majority: W=2 of 3 has the durability of two copies and still tolerates one broker being down. That is quorums, and the reason it is the standard answer is precisely this trade rather than anything about consistency.

    Then check the incident against the correlation floor. Forty thousand messages at once is not forty thousand independent events — it is one event. If the cause reached every broker (a bad deploy, a full disk on the shared volume, an expired credential, a poison message), then c is what the incident measured, and the durability ceiling is −log₁₀(c) nines regardless of broker count. Five brokers of one deploy is one broker. The incident history answers this in an afternoon and it is the highest-value thing on the list.

    Finally, notice what the second AZ genuinely buys. It is the one part of the proposal with real value, and its value is *independence* rather than count — it lowers c, which is the only term that was capping them. So: keep the AZ, drop the two extra brokers, change the acknowledgement rule to a majority, and re-derive the number afterwards. Cheaper than the proposal and it fixes the thing that broke.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: three different promises about one system — that it responds now, that it will not stop during an interval, and that what it accepted is permanently kept — which are measured in different units, so a number in one is not evidence about another, and the third is different in kind because it has no repair term. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A bank. The branch being open is availability; getting through the quarter without an unscheduled closure is reliability; your balance still being your balance is durability. They are quoted in different units and everyone knows not to confuse them: nobody accepts "we are open 99.99% of the time" as an answer to "where is my deposit". And the asymmetry this lesson turns on is the reason banking looks the way it does — a closed branch reopens, so closures are managed with schedules and staffing, while a lost ledger entry has no repair term, which is why the ledger gets double-entry bookkeeping, an append-only journal, physical off-site copies, and an audit function that exists for nothing else. The controls are not stronger versions of the opening-hours controls. They are a different kind of control, because the failure is permanent.

    Other instances: a hospital. The A&E department being open is availability; a year with no unplanned closure is reliability; your medical record surviving thirty years and a change of provider is durability — and note that record retention is legislated while opening hours are merely managed, which is society pricing the permanent failure differently. A library. Lending hours, uninterrupted service, and the archive — and the archive is the only one with fire suppression, acid-free paper and a second building, which is the correlation floor being paid for explicitly. A postal service, where the counter's hours, the absence of strikes, and whether a registered letter can be lost are three separately-priced promises, and only the third one carries insurance — because insurance is what you buy when the thing cannot be repaired.

    The instance that fails the analogy is instructive: a restaurant kitchen has availability and reliability and no durability at all, because it accepts nothing it must keep. Systems with no durability requirement are genuinely simpler, and part of the design skill this lesson is for is noticing when you are in one — a stateless request path has two guarantees to argue about, not three.

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
