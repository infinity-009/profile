Tier 4 · Distributed systems mechanics Two generals & FLP start here

No number of acknowledgements ever proves the other side agrees with you

Two generals coordinating an attack over messengers who might be captured, and a formal 1985 proof about consensus algorithms, turn out to be the same fact told twice. Neither is a bug waiting for a cleverer protocol — both are proofs that certainty is unreachable over an unreliable, unbounded-delay channel. What's computable is how close you can get: confidence climbs toward 100% with every extra confirmation and never touches it, and a consensus attempt under real delay can only ever be safely stuck, never safely wrong.

01

Two generals, one hill each, and a messenger who might not make it

General A and General B camp on two hills overlooking a city. Together their armies can win; alone, either one loses. A sends a messenger: “attack at dawn.” The messenger might be captured — the message might never arrive. So A waits for B to send an acknowledgement back. But that acknowledgement travels over the exact same unreliable route. If it doesn’t arrive, does that mean B never got the message, or that B got it and their reply was lost? A cannot tell — so A must send another messenger to make sure. And now B faces the identical problem in reverse: did A ever receive that confirmation?

There is no message in this exchange, however many are sent, after which both generals can be certain the other one knows what they know. This isn’t a limitation of these particular generals or this particular postal service. It is a mathematical fact about any protocol running over a channel that can silently drop a message — and it is the exact same fact, restated with a formal proof in 1985, that tells you why a distributed system can never simply “try harder” to guarantee consensus either.

02

Assume enough retries eventually gets you certainty

The instinct: keep resending confirmations, and eventually the odds of failure become so small they might as well be zero. That instinct is not wrong about the odds — it is wrong about “might as well be.” The gap between “very likely” and “certain” never closes, and this lesson can show you exactly how much of it is left after any given number of round trips, and why a real consensus protocol handles that leftover gap by refusing to guess rather than by pretending it isn’t there.

Predict Each message (and each acknowledgement) between the generals has a 10% chance of being lost, independently. After 4 full confirmation round trips (message + ack, message + ack, ...), what is the chance both generals are confident the attack is agreed?
Each round trip succeeds only if both its message and its ack arrive: (1-0.1)^2 = 0.81 per round trip. The chance at least one of 4 independent round trips fully succeeds is 1 - (1-0.81)^4 = 0.99869679 - about 99.87%, not 100%. The uncertainty shrinks fast (from 19% to 0.13% in four round trips) but a finite protocol can only ever shrink it, never zero it out.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — 10% loss per message, 4 confirmation round trips — confidence reaches 99.869679%, and one more round trip beyond that would add only 0.105560 percentage points more (the gap shrinks from 0.130321% to 0.024761%). It never reaches exactly 100% for any finite round-trip count. On the consensus side, at the default 5 processes (a 3-of-5 quorum), a 20% chance any one process has actually crashed, and a 50ms timeout against 50ms mean network delay: the protocol decides 50.87% of the time and sits safely undecided 49.13% of the time — but it is wrong exactly 0% of the time, always, no matter how the sliders move.

03

FLP: the same impossibility, minus the story about hills

Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson’s 1985 result formalizes the two generals’ problem for a network of any number of processes trying to agree on a value. Strip away the narrative and the claim is precise: in an asynchronous system — no upper bound on how long a message can take, or how long a process can pause before responding — a deterministic consensus algorithm cannot be guaranteed to terminate if even one process might crash. The proof’s engine is the same indistinguishability the generals ran into: a process that is merely very slow and a process that has actually crashed send the network the exact same signal, which is nothing, and a deterministic algorithm’s next action is completely determined by what it has observed — so an adversary who controls message delays can always find a delay pattern that keeps the algorithm from ever being sure enough to decide.

FLP does not say consensus is impossible. It says consensus cannot be BOTH always-safe and always-live under those exact assumptions. That split is the whole practical payoff:

  • Safety — nothing bad ever happens. No two nodes ever decide two different values. This can be kept unconditionally, in every network condition, forever — a protocol that keeps safety just refuses to move when it isn’t sure.
  • Liveness — something good eventually happens. The protocol terminates with a decision. This is what FLP proves cannot always be guaranteed — an adversarial delay pattern can keep a correct, safe protocol permanently undecided.

Real systems don’t out-clever this proof; they change one of its assumptions, on purpose, and pay for it honestly:

(i) Partial synchrony. Assume message delays ARE bounded almost all of the time in a real data center network — not provably always, just usually. This is exactly what Raft and Paxos lean on: during normal operation, timeouts behave like real deadlines and the system makes progress constantly; during a genuine partition or an extreme GC pause, the same system does precisely what FLP predicts a correct protocol must do — it stops committing rather than risk two leaders. Raft and Paxos are the subject of a later lesson in this tier; the point here is only that they are FLP’s escape hatch in the wild, not a contradiction of it.

(ii) Randomization. FLP’s proof is specifically about deterministic algorithms — its adversary wins by predicting exactly what the algorithm will do next. Let the algorithm flip coins instead, and that prediction stops working; randomized consensus protocols guarantee termination with probability 1 (as the number of rounds grows), which is a different, weaker, and achievable promise than “guaranteed to terminate in every single run.”

(iii) Weaker guarantees. Stop asking for linearizable agreement at all, and the impossibility doesn’t apply because the question changed — eventual consistency promises replicas converge eventually, with no bound on how long, which sidesteps FLP by no longer requiring a single synchronization point to succeed. And even inside strongly-consistent systems, a timeout is this escape hatch’s everyday form: not a proof that a peer is dead, just a bet — “probably crashed” — that a system acts on because refusing to ever guess means refusing to ever fail over.

04

One regress, two literatures, three ways out

TWO GENERALS    A confirmation is itself a message that can be lost, so certainty about the other
                side's state regresses forever. No Nth message ends it.

FLP             The same regress, formalized: a slow process and a crashed process are
                indistinguishable, so a deterministic protocol cannot always tell "wait longer"
                from "give up," which is exactly what blocks guaranteed termination.

SAFETY/LIVENESS FLP's impossibility is about LIVENESS only. A protocol can keep SAFETY (never
                decide wrong) unconditionally, forever, by choosing to stay undecided instead.

ESCAPE HATCHES  Partial synchrony (bet delays are usually bounded), randomization (make the
                adversary unable to predict you), or weaker guarantees (stop requiring one
                synchronized answer at all — or just bet with a timeout).

06

None of the three escape hatches is free

Partial synchrony costs you the rare-but-real day it’s wrong. Betting that delays are usually bounded means your system makes progress essentially all the time — and on the day a data center network partitions badly enough to violate that bet, your system does the one thing it’s actually allowed to do: stop, rather than split-brain. That stall is not a bug report; it’s the safety guarantee cashing out exactly as designed, at the one moment it’s expensive to watch happen.

Randomization costs you a probabilistic, not absolute, termination guarantee, plus the engineering overhead of a real source of randomness every participant can independently draw from without an adversary being able to bias it — a coin flip that isn’t actually unpredictable buys you nothing over the deterministic case FLP already ruled out.

Weaker guarantees cost you the guarantee itself. A timeout used as “probably crashed” is a bet, and every bet has a stake: too short, and it convicts live-but-slow processes constantly (the failure-detection.mdx false-positive cost, computed there in full); too long, and a genuinely dead process keeps its work assigned to it for that whole window. Eventual consistency costs you the ability to answer “is this read up to date?” with anything better than “eventually, yes” — which is fine for a lot of systems and disqualifying for others.

07

In the wild

Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson’s own paper is the source of the proof this lesson quantifies the consequences of — Michael J. Fischer, Nancy Lynch, and Michael S. Paterson, “Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process,” Journal of the ACM, 1985. Kleppmann and Riccomini’s own summary of it — including the exact framing used in slot 3, that FLP rules out guaranteed termination but not consensus itself, and that timeouts or randomization restore solvability — is Martin Kleppmann and Chris Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., ch. 10, “The Impossibility of Consensus,” pp. 425–426. The two generals problem itself, as the direct ancestor of the Byzantine Generals Problem, is described on p. 378 of the same book.

Partial synchrony is not an informal hand-wave — it has its own 1988 formalization: Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch, and Larry Stockmeyer, “Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony,” Journal of the ACM, 1988 — the paper that makes precise exactly what “usually bounded delay” has to mean for a protocol to provably regain liveness.

Randomized consensus predates even FLP’s proof — Michael Ben-Or, “Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols,” PODC, 1983, is the earliest of this escape hatch’s protocols, guaranteeing termination with probability 1 against exactly the adversarial scheduling FLP’s proof depends on being able to predict.

failure-detection.mdx is the practical continuation of escape hatch (iii) — it works out, in full, what a timeout actually costs as a bet: the false-positive rate as a function of the delay distribution’s tail, and why Raft’s ~200ms elections and Cassandra’s ~13s convictions are both correct despite the ten-thousand-fold difference, because the two systems price a wrong guess at wildly different costs. Raft and Paxos themselves — escape hatch (i), worked out in full — are a later lesson in this tier.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The two generals problem is not solved by adding more acknowledgements — each ack is itself a message that can be lost, so "did you get my ack?" regresses forever; there is no Nth message that finally supplies certainty, only an (N+1)th question about it
  • FLP (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985) is the same fact made formal: in an ASYNCHRONOUS system (no bound on message delay or processing time) with even one process that might crash, no DETERMINISTIC algorithm can guarantee it will always terminate with agreement — because a slow process and a crashed process produce an identical observation, and a deterministic protocol's next move is fully determined by what it has observed so far
  • This forces a split between two properties: SAFETY (nothing bad ever happens — no two nodes ever decide differently) and LIVENESS (something good eventually happens — the protocol terminates). FLP's impossibility is about liveness; a well-built protocol keeps safety unconditionally and only ever sacrifices liveness, by refusing to decide rather than guessing
  • Real systems escape the impossibility three ways, none of which contradicts the proof: (i) partial synchrony — assume delays ARE bounded most of the time, even though not provably always, which is what lets a protocol make progress in the common case while staying safe in the rare async case; (ii) randomization — let the algorithm flip coins, which sidesteps the specifically DETERMINISTIC-algorithm impossibility and guarantees termination with probability 1; (iii) weaker guarantees — accept eventual consistency, and use a timeout as a practical (fallible) guess at "probably crashed" rather than a proof of it
  • Confidence after N confirmation round trips has a clean closed form — 1 minus the probability every round trip so far has failed — and it is monotonically increasing and asymptotic: each additional round trip buys a strictly smaller increment of confidence, approaching 100% and never arriving
  • A protocol that only decides once it has a quorum of responses can be forced to sit undecided indefinitely by an adversarial pattern of delays (a liveness failure), but it can never be forced to commit without that quorum (a safety failure) — the two properties fail independently, and only one of them is what FLP actually rules out

  1. Cued recall

    A junior engineer proposes fixing the two generals problem by having each general send three acknowledgements instead of one, reasoning that "the chance all three get lost is tiny." Does this solve the problem?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it shrinks the failure probability but does not change its kind. Three acks lost independently at loss probability p has probability p³, much smaller than p, so the practical risk drops. But the general who sent those three acks still does not know whether ANY of them arrived unless something comes back confirming it — and that confirmation is itself a message that can be lost. The regress does not stop at three, or three hundred; it stops nowhere. This is exactly what bothConfidentProbability computes: a number that climbs toward 1 as round trips increase, never equal to it for any finite count.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    FLP says no deterministic algorithm can guarantee termination in an asynchronous system with one faulty process. Raft elects leaders and commits log entries in production constantly. Is Raft a counterexample to FLP?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — Raft is a real-world escape hatch, not a refutation. FLP's proof is about the asynchronous model specifically: no bound on message delay, ever. Raft assumes partial synchrony — that message delays are bounded almost all of the time in a real data center network, even though a proof can't promise it always. Raft stays SAFE unconditionally (a term number and its election rule mean two different logs are never both accepted as leader for the same term), and it makes progress — LIVENESS — whenever the network actually behaves synchronously enough for its timeouts to work, which is nearly always. During a genuine partition or extreme delay, Raft does exactly what FLP predicts it must: it stops making progress rather than risk being wrong.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    A team debugging a distributed lock service says: "our nodes agree on who holds the lock 99.98% of the time; the other 0.02% they just... don't decide, and a client's request times out." Someone argues this must be a bug, because "a proper consensus system should always give you a definite answer." Is that argument right?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — it has the guarantee backwards. FLP says a deterministic consensus protocol in an asynchronous model literally cannot ALWAYS terminate with an answer while staying safe; a lock service that returns "undecided, please retry" during a genuinely bad network moment is doing the only thing a correct protocol is allowed to do in that moment — the alternative, guessing an answer under uncertainty, is what actually produces a bug (two clients both believing they hold the lock). 0.02% "no answer" is the observable cost of keeping safety. The bug report to actually investigate is whether that 0.02% ever contains a WRONG answer instead of no answer — that would be the real defect.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: proving mutual agreement over a channel that can silently fail requires an infinite chain of confirmations, so no finite protocol can ever supply proof — only a probability that approaches certainty without reaching it. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    "I love you" — "I love you too" — "...did you mean it?" Any relationship reassurance loop has the identical shape: person A wants to be certain person B's affection is genuine and current, so A asks; B answers; but the answer is itself just another signal that could be insincere or outdated, so it does not supply the certainty it was meant to. Asking a second time, or a tenth, drives the confidence up — most people's trust does asymptotically approach near-certainty after enough consistent evidence — but there is no Nth reassurance that converts "very likely true" into logically provable fact, which is exactly why trust is described as something you build over time rather than something you can prove once, the same way this lesson's confidence curve climbs and never touches 100%.
    Did you have that?
