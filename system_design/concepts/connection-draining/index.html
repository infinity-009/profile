Tier 2 · Scaling the stateless tier Connection draining

A 3-second drain window has only 1 second left after the load balancer's 2-second lag, cutting 136 requests across a 40-backend rollout — reaching 99% completion takes 5.5 seconds and makes the deploy 1.85× slower

A backend told to shut down is still carrying whatever it was carrying a moment before — that population is fixed by Little's Law, not by how politely anyone asks it to leave. The load balancer needs its own time to notice and stop sending new work, which eats into the drain window before any draining happens at all. This lesson computes what's left once that lag is subtracted, what fraction of the in-flight population is still running when the process is finally killed, and what buying a higher completion rate actually costs every rolling deploy that ever takes a backend down.

Read first — connection-poolingload-balancingtail-at-scale

01

A 1.5-second grace period that saved nothing at all

A team adds a graceful-shutdown handler: on SIGTERM, stop accepting new work, wait 1.5 seconds, then exit. It ships as an obvious improvement over killing the process outright. Weeks later, a routine deploy still shows a handful of connection-reset errors on every rollout — not fewer than before the fix, the same number.

The fix changed nothing, because the load balancer’s own health check took 2 seconds to notice the backend was going away — longer than the 1.5-second window the backend gave itself. The process was still fielding new connections for the entire “grace period,” and then it died with exactly the same population in flight a hard kill would have caught. Nothing about the handler was wrong. The number it waited was wrong, and it was wrong because it was sized against only one of the two clocks running.

02

Assume a drain window just needs to be longer than zero

The instinct: any wait before killing the process is strictly better than none, so a drain window is a dial with one direction — longer is safer, and “some grace period” already buys something over “no grace period.” The gap is that a drain window races a clock it doesn’t control, and until it wins that race it buys nothing measurable at all.

Predict A backend is carrying 75 in-flight requests when it's told to shut down. Its load balancer takes 2 seconds to notice and stop routing new connections to it. If the backend's drain window is 1.5 seconds — shorter than that 2-second lag — how many of the 75 in-flight requests does the drain window save, compared to killing the process with no drain window at all?
The time actually available to drain is max(0, drainWindow − deregistrationLag). At 1.5 seconds against a 2-second lag that is max(0, −0.5) = 0 — the window expires before the load balancer has even stopped sending new traffic, so every request in flight when the process dies is exactly the same population a zero-second window would have caught. The window only starts saving anything once it exceeds the lag it is racing against.
Show it without answering

At the rig’s default — 300 requests/s, a 250 ms mean duration, a 2,000 ms load-balancer lag, and a 3,000 ms drain window — this backend is already carrying 75 requests the instant the shutdown signal fires (Little’s Law, not a guess), plus 600 more admitted while the load balancer catches up. That leaves only 1,000 ms of the configured window to actually drain anything. 4.5% of the in-flight population is still running when the process is killed — about 3.4 requests on this one backend, 136 across a 40-backend rolling deploy running in 4-backend batches, finishing in 30 seconds. Reaching 99% completion instead needs at least a 5,548 ms window — cutting the fleet-wide loss to 30 requests, at the cost of a 55.5-second rolling deploy: 1.85× slower than today’s.

03

The guard still has to walk to the door

A store’s closing announcement doesn’t lock the door — a person has to walk over and turn the key, and that walk takes real time. Every customer who steps in while the guard is mid-walk isn’t trespassing; the door was, in every observable sense, still open. The store still owes everyone already inside, plus everyone who slipped in during the walk, a chance to finish and check out before the lights actually go off. A grace period sized only against “how long does checkout take” and not against “how long does the walk take” will find people still at the register when the power drops — not because checkout was slow, but because the walk ate into the time the store thought it was giving them.

A drain window and a load balancer’s deregistration lag are exactly that pair. The backend’s own handler is the store; the load balancer is the guard. Sizing the window off the request-duration distribution alone is sizing the grace period off checkout time alone — correct as far as it goes, and silently wrong the moment the walk to the door takes longer than the grace period itself.

04

Three numbers, one shared shape: a clock you don't control eating a budget you do

IN-FLIGHT POPULATION   Fixed by Little's Law the instant the shutdown signal fires — L = λW,
                        unaffected by how the shutdown is handled. Nothing about telling a
                        backend to leave shrinks what it's already carrying.

LOAD-BALANCER LAG       The window you configure and the window you actually get are not the
                        same number. Every millisecond before the LB notices and stops routing
                        is subtracted from the drain window before any draining happens — and a
                        window at or below the lag drains exactly nothing.

DEPLOY VELOCITY         Whatever window survives that subtraction is paid again, in full, for
                        every batch of backends a rolling deploy or scale-down cycles through.
                        A wider safety margin is a fleet-wide tax, not a one-time cost.

Each row is the same shape: a number that looks like it belongs entirely to the backend actually depends on a clock the backend cannot see or set, and the honest fix has to account for the gap rather than assume it away.

06

Neither side of this trade-off is free

A short drain window costs correctness, and it costs it silently. A connection reset doesn’t raise an alarm the way a 500 does in most stacks — it often surfaces as a client-side retry that quietly succeeds, so the actual count of cut requests is invisible unless someone goes looking for it the way this lesson’s model does. A team can run for months with a window shorter than its own load balancer’s lag and see nothing but a faint, unexplained baseline of connection errors that “has always been there.”

A long drain window costs deploy velocity, and it costs it every single time. The 55.5-second deploy at a 99%-target window isn’t a one-off tax — every future rollout, every scale-down, every autoscaler decision to remove a backend pays that same window, once per batch. Chasing a completion target without also raising the batch size trades a correctness problem you might never notice for a deploy-speed problem everyone will notice on every release.

The lag itself is the one lever neither number can compensate for. A drain window that ignores the load balancer’s deregistration lag is sized against the wrong clock no matter how generous it is — 5,548 ms only reaches 99% completion because 2,000 ms of it is spent waiting for the load balancer to catch up, not draining anything. Shrinking that lag — a faster health-check interval, a readiness probe the load balancer polls instead of waits to be told — buys back window on both sides of this trade-off at once, which neither a shorter nor a longer drain window alone can do.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • The in-flight population at the instant a shutdown signal fires is fixed by Little's Law, L = λW — the same object this course has used for connection pools and CRL populations — and it does not shrink just because the backend has been told to leave
  • The load balancer does not learn a backend is draining the instant the signal fires; a health check has to fail, or an endpoint list has to propagate, first — and every millisecond of that lag is subtracted from the configured drain window before any draining happens
  • A drain window at or below the load balancer's own lag drains exactly nothing — not "less," zero — because the effective time left for in-flight requests to finish is max(0, window − lag), and that floors at zero the same way whether the window is 1.5 seconds or 0
  • What fraction of the in-flight population is still running after a given amount of time is a survival-function question, reused unchanged from the same DELAYS this course already uses for a heartbeat's delay and a retry's delay — a request's duration and a network delay are the same mathematical object: a positive random variable with a tail
  • Widening the drain window to save more requests is not free: a rolling deploy pays the window once per batch of backends it cycles through, so the same number that protects requests also sets a floor on how fast every future deploy or scale-down can run
  • New connections do not stop arriving the instant the shutdown signal fires either — they keep arriving for exactly as long as the load balancer's own lag lasts, and those late-admitted requests are not exempt from needing to finish before the process dies

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A team configures a 1.5-second drain window, reasoning "some grace period is better than none — even half a second of draining should save a few requests." Their load balancer's health-check interval means it takes 2 seconds to notice a backend has left rotation and stop routing to it. At 300 requests/s and a 250 ms mean request duration, how many of the 75 requests in flight at the moment of shutdown does that 1.5-second window actually save, compared to killing the process immediately with no drain window at all?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Zero. The effective drain time is max(0, drainWindow − lag) = max(0, 1500 − 2000) = 0 — the load balancer hasn't even stopped routing yet when the window expires, so every one of the 75 in-flight requests, and every request admitted during the 2 seconds the load balancer was still sending traffic, is still running when the process is killed. A 1.5-second "graceful" window and a 0-second window produce the exact same outcome here: 75 of 75 cut. The window only starts doing anything once it exceeds the lag it's racing.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    A service raises its drain window from 3 seconds to the 5.5 seconds needed for 99% of in-flight requests to finish cleanly. A teammate objects: "that's barely 2.5 seconds more — why is the rollout so much slower?" Where does the slowdown actually come from?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The window isn't paid once — it's paid once per batch of backends the rolling deploy cycles through. At 40 backends in batches of 4, that's 10 batches, so 2.5 extra seconds per batch becomes 25 extra seconds on the whole deploy: 30 seconds becomes about 55.5, a 1.85× slowdown from what looks like a small per-backend change. The drain window is a fleet-wide multiplier, not a one-time cost, which is exactly why widening it to chase a completion target has to be weighed against every future deploy that pays it again.
    Did you have that?
  3. Near transfer

    `connection-pooling.mdx` computes a pool's acquire timeout as a purely local number — the caller's own clock, checked against the pool's own state, with nothing external in the loop. A reader concludes that a drain window should be sized the same way: pick a number the backend's own request-duration distribution justifies, and be done with it. Is that the right way to size a drain window?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    No — a drain window has an actor a pool acquire timeout does not: a separate system (the load balancer) that has to learn the backend is leaving before the window can do any good. A pool's client and the pool live in the same process, so the acquire timeout's clock and the pool's clock are the same clock. A drain window's clock and the load balancer's clock are not — they're two independent systems, and the backend's own duration distribution only tells you the SECOND half of the sizing problem. Sizing a drain window purely off the duration tail, the way this reader wants to size it purely off the pool's own numbers, silently assumes the load balancer's lag is zero — and a window at or below that lag drains nothing at all, regardless of how carefully the duration tail was measured.
    Did you have that?
  4. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a system is told to stop accepting new work, but the mechanism that routes work to it takes its own, separate amount of time to notice — so work keeps arriving for a while after the "stop" signal, and none of that work is exempt from finishing before the system is actually allowed to go away. Name an instance outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A store's closing announcement and the security guard who has to walk to the door to lock it. At closing time, a cashier flips a sign and makes an announcement — the "shutdown signal" — but the guard posted at the entrance, thirty metres away amid the aisles, takes a genuine amount of time to walk over and actually lock the door. Every customer who steps in during that walk is not turned away; they came in while the door was still, in fact, open, exactly as a connection arriving during a load balancer's deregistration lag is not rejected. The store still has to let everyone already inside — and everyone who slipped in during the walk — finish shopping and check out before the lights go off, and a store that budgets its "everyone out" grace period without accounting for how long the guard's walk takes will find people still at the register when the power drops, for the same reason a drain window sized only off request duration and not off deregistration lag cuts requests it thought it had time for.
    Did you have that?
