Tier 5 · Moving data around CDNs explained well nowhere else

A hundred and twenty-eight edge locations shortened every round trip and multiplied origin traffic 87 times

A CDN cannot make a packet travel faster — the floor is distance over the speed of light in glass. What it removes is round trips, and a cold HTTPS request is four of them. Then the bill arrives: the same request stream divided over more locations gives each location a thinner slice, and thin slices do not cache. There is an interior optimum, and it moves with your traffic.

01

The CDN made it four times faster, and nothing on the network changed

A site served from London. Users in Sydney complain, and they are right to: a page that renders in 90 ms in London takes most of a second there.

Sydney is 16,994 km from London by great circle. Light in a fibre core travels at about 204,000 km/s, so one round trip has a floor of 166.7 ms. That is not a measurement of anybody’s network — it is the distance divided by a physical constant, and it is the same number for every vendor at every price.

Now count the round trips in one cold HTTPS request. TCP’s handshake is one. The TLS 1.2 handshake is two more. Then the request goes out and the response comes back, which is a fourth. Four round trips, three of which carry no application data at all. Four times 166.7 ms is 667 ms of pure propagation, before the origin has read a byte.

Somebody puts a CDN in front of it. The Sydney user’s page now arrives in about 170 ms. The retro concludes that the CDN is four times faster.

The CDN is not faster. Every packet still crosses the same fibre at the same speed. What changed is that the four round trips now happen inside Sydney — the edge terminates TLS, so the handshakes are local, and only the content has to come from far away, and on a cache hit it does not come from far away at all. The 4× is a ratio of counts: four round trips became one long one plus three short ones, and then the long one became zero.

Six months later the same team ships a price change and it goes live everywhere except Brazil, where the old price is served for nineteen more minutes to a small number of very annoyed customers. That incident and the 4× have the same root, and it is not a network property.

02

Add locations until it stops helping

The belief this has to break
More edge locations means lower latency, so more is better.

More locations does mean lower latency, and the second half does not follow. A CDN’s edge caches on a TTL, and a TTL cache’s hit ratio for one object under Poisson arrivals is a formula with the arrival rate in it:

hit ratio = λT / (1 + λT)

where λ is how often that PoP is asked for that object and T is the TTL. One miss fetches the object, it is served for T seconds, and then it expires — so a cycle contains one miss and λT hits.

Now divide the same global request stream over N locations. Every object’s λ at every location is divided by N. Because x/(1+x) is concave, splitting a stream and re-averaging always loses, and it loses most on exactly the objects that were only just worth caching. So the two things you are trading move in opposite directions: distance falls as 1/√N, and the hit ratio falls too. That is what makes the answer an interior number rather than “as many as they will sell you”.

Predict One object is requested once per second worldwide, and the TTL is 60 seconds. On a single edge location its hit ratio is λT/(1+λT) = 60/61 = 98.4%. Serve the identical traffic from 200 locations instead. What is that object's hit ratio now?
λ at any one location is now 1/200 per second, so λT = 60/200 = 0.3 and 0.3/1.3 = 23.1%. The traffic and the TTL are unchanged; what changed is the rate per cache, and that is the only variable the formula has. Note the shape of the loss: hit ratio fell by a factor of four while the traffic was divided by two hundred, because x/(1+x) is concave — which is also why the loss is not simply proportional and cannot be reasoned about without the formula.
Show it without answering

Four things to take from it, all computed by the model that drives it.

The minimum is real and it is not at the right-hand end. On the default settings — 50,000 req/s over a 200,000-object catalogue, one-hour TTL, cold TLS 1.3 — the floor is 12.9 ms at 38 locations. At 2,048 locations it is 46.5 ms, which is almost exactly what a single location gives you (51.1 ms) for entirely the opposite reason. One PoP is slow because it is far away; two thousand are slow because none of them can cache anything. Note also that the minimum is flat: 32 locations give 13.0 ms and 45 give 12.9 ms, so nobody needs to hit this number. What matters is not being an order of magnitude past it.

Origin load is the term that moves fastest. 1 location serves 99.9% from cache and sends the origin 55 req/s. 128 locations serve 90.4% and send the origin 4,796 req/s — an 87× increase in the number a bill is computed from, at identical user traffic. A 9-point drop in hit ratio is a 90× rise in origin traffic, because you are billed on 1 − hit ratio and 1 − 0.999 is a very small number to multiply.

Traffic decides the answer. Drag traffic and read the best PoP count readout: at 100 req/s the optimum is 4 locations; at 50,000 it is 38; at a million it is 256. A small site on a very large edge network is paying the multiplexing loss to buy latency it could have had from four locations.

The protocol changes it too. On a warm connection the optimum drops to 16 locations, because proximity is only worth one round trip instead of three. Fix your round-trip count and you need less of a network.

03

The intuition: one city bakery, or two hundred corner shops

Forget servers. A city with one central bakery.

Getting bread is not one trip. Before the bakery will sell to you, you have to walk over to check they are open, walk back, walk over to agree what you are buying, walk back, and then walk over to collect it. Five walks. Nobody designed this on purpose; it accumulated. When the bakery finally lets you do the whole thing in one visit, bread appears to have got five times faster, and your walking speed has not changed at all. That is the handshake, and it is the entire 4× in slot 1. It is also why a nearby shop is worth five times what you thought: you were not making one trip.

Now open two hundred corner shops. Every walk is two minutes instead of forty. And each shop sells three rye loaves a day instead of six hundred, so rye is either sitting there going stale or not stocked at all, and when you ask for it the shop has to phone the central bakery and wait. The central bakery, which used to receive one bulk order a day, now takes two hundred phone calls for rye.

Nothing about the bread changed. What changed is that demand was pooled and now it is not.

And notice which loaf is stale. In the shop that sells rye constantly, there is always rye on the shelf and it is on average half a sell-by old. In the shop that never sells rye, you wait for the phone call — and you get it straight out of the oven. The popular thing is the stale thing; the rare thing is fresh and slow. That inversion is exact, and it comes back in slot 4 as an identity.

A CDN buys you two things and sells you two things. It buys shorter round trips and fewer of them. It sells you the pooling of demand that made your cache work, and a copy of the truth in every location that can disagree with the original. The first is geometry, the second is arithmetic, and neither is on the vendor’s page.

04

The mechanism: a count, a concave function, and one identity

One: the floor is per round trip, and the count is the variable

propagation floor = distance ÷ (c / n), per round trip

c is 299,792 km/s, n is silica’s refractive index of about 1.47, so light in glass travels at roughly 204,000 km/s. London to Sydney is 16,994 km, so one round trip has a floor of 166.7 ms. This is the number latency numbers establishes, and nothing in this lesson improves it.

Since the per-round-trip cost is fixed, the only movable quantity is how many round trips you take. Here is a cold cross-continent request decomposed, with the immovable parts hatched.

Two consequences worth stating separately. A round trip you remove is worth its full propagation cost, so TLS 1.3’s single saved round trip is worth 166.7 ms to a user in Sydney and 3.4 ms to a user in Paris — the same change, a fiftyfold difference in value. And sequential round trips add while parallel ones do not, which is why a page needing ten serialised requests cannot be fixed by a CDN alone.

Two: hit ratio, and why more locations lowers it

hit ratio for one object = λT ÷ (1 + λT)  ·  λ = (total rate × object share) ÷ PoPs

A renewal cycle is one miss plus λT hits, so the hit ratio is the hit fraction of the cycle. Rate and TTL appear only as their product, which means halving the TTL and doubling the traffic are the same intervention. Asserted in cache.test.mjs, including the hand case λ = 1/s, T = 60 s giving exactly 60/61.

Because x/(1+x) is concave, dividing λ across PoPs and re-averaging strictly loses. This is the statistical multiplexing loss in the same form as bulkheads — there it is Erlang-B under a partitioned pool, here it is a TTL cache under a partitioned request stream, and in both cases the thing you give up was never itemised because it was just the pool working.

The second mechanism is capacity, and it is the eviction lesson’s. Under Zipf popularity the mass of the top C objects of N is (C^(1−s) − 1) ÷ (N^(1−s) − 1), and at s = 1 that limit is exactly ln C ÷ ln N. So hit ratio is logarithmic in cache size: equal gains cost equal multiples, not equal amounts. The model, on a 200,000-object catalogue:

At s = 1.0 the logarithm is exact and visible: every tenfold increase in the cache buys 18 percentage points, forever, which is why “we will just make the cache bigger” runs out.

Which mechanism binds is the diagnosis, and it flips with PoP count. At one PoP the hour-long TTL implies holding 199,160 of the 200,000 objects, so a disk smaller than that is the constraint. At 2,048 PoPs the implied footprint is 31,608, so the same disk is idle and time is the constraint. edgeCache reports both, because buying disk when you needed a longer TTL is the expensive version of this mistake.

Three: what a TTL actually promises, which is not what people think

mean age of the copy served = (T ÷ 2) × hit ratio

Derived, not asserted, and exact. Ages served in one cycle sum to ∫λt dt = λT²/2 over 1 + λT requests, which factorises into (T/2) × the hit ratio — because every miss is served fresh and every hit is served a copy uniformly aged over the TTL. Conditional on a hit it is exactly T/2 for every object at every rate. Checked to 1e-12 in cache.test.mjs.

This is the identity the bakery predicted, and it is uncomfortable in a specific way: freshness and offload are the same number. You cannot raise the hit ratio without raising the mean staleness in exact proportion, because the only way to hit more is to serve older copies. A one-hour TTL at a 90.4% hit ratio serves a mean age of 27.1 minutes — not 60, not 30, and the gap between 30 and 27.1 is your miss ratio.

The same renewal cycle bounds the origin’s side. One object at one PoP causes at most 1 ÷ T origin fetches per second no matter how hard it is hammered, which is what “offload” means: an unbounded user rate becomes a bounded origin rate. But the bound is per PoP, so the fleet multiplies it — a single hot object on a 60-second TTL across 200 PoPs generates up to 3.3 origin fetches per second, and the measured rise from 1 PoP to 200 for one object requested once a second is 47×. That is cache stampede with 200 members instead of one, and it is worse at the edge for a structural reason: the PoPs share an expiry deadline and nothing coordinates them. A per-key lock at one PoP does not know the other 199 exist.

Four: invalidation, and the three ways to pay for it

A TTL is a promise about staleness that costs nothing to keep and cannot be broken early. A purge breaks it early, and it is a fan-out.

The third row is the one worth internalising, because it is the only one that does not trade anything. app.a83f21.js exists so that the answer to “how do I invalidate this?” can be you do not. Content-addressed URLs are cached for a year with immutable, a new build is a new URL that was never in any cache, and the invalidation problem moves to the one small document that references them — which you then serve with a short TTL, or no cache at all. The expensive-to-move bytes become immutable and the invalidation lands on a 2 kB file. That is the whole trick, and it is why the arithmetic above never bites for static assets and always bites for prices, stock levels and permissions.

The mean age column is the one nobody plots. It falls as you add locations — 30.0 to 19.2 minutes — because more of the traffic is now misses, and misses are fresh. Adding PoPs makes the site fresher and more expensive, simultaneously, from the same cause.

When does none of this matter? When your objects are immutable and your traffic is large. A versioned static asset at a million requests a second has a hit ratio in the high nineties at any PoP count, a TTL of a year, and a staleness of zero by construction. Every hard case in this lesson is a mutable object with modest traffic, which is exactly the case people put behind a CDN last and reason about least.

06

The trade-off: proximity against pooling, offload against freshness

Proximity costs pooling, and the exchange rate is your traffic volume. Every location you add shortens the trip as 1/√N and thins the slice each cache sees. At a million requests per second you can afford 256 locations; at a hundred you can afford four, and the arithmetic does not care that the network has more. This is the same trade as bulkheads with distance in place of blast radius, and it has the same structure — a concave benefit against a convex cost, therefore an interior optimum, therefore no answer without a number.

Offload costs freshness, exactly. mean age = (T/2) × hit ratio is an identity, not a tendency. There is no configuration that raises the hit ratio and lowers the mean staleness, because they are the same quantity. The only escape is to stop caching mutable things: version the immutable ones and let the mutable ones miss.

A purge costs a window, and the window scales with the fleet. TTL expiry is free to operate and slow to correct. Purge is fast to correct and is a fan-out whose tail is the disagreement window, so the same 2,048 locations that made the site fast made the inconsistency longer. If your correctness argument depends on all PoPs agreeing, you do not have one.

And the one that is not a trade: a CDN is a copy, not a fix. It bounds the origin’s request rate and does nothing about the origin being slow. A 900 ms page behind a CDN is a 900 ms page for every miss, and the misses are the requests you cannot predict — a new product page, a crawler, a long-tail search result. The multiplexing arithmetic above says that as you add locations the fraction of users who get the uncached experience goes up, so a CDN makes the mean better and quietly enlarges the population that sees the tail. That population is the one that files tickets, and it is why the honest order of work is: fix the round-trip count, fix the origin, then buy proximity.

07

In the wild

The multiplexing loss has a name in the industry, and it is a product. Every large CDN sells a mid-tier between the edge and the origin, precisely because a flat edge tier’s origin load scales with PoP count: Cloudflare calls it Tiered Cache and routes an edge miss to the nearest “Tier 1” datacentre first; Fastly calls it shielding; AWS calls it CloudFront Origin Shield and describes it as collapsing requests so that as few as one reaches the origin per object. AWS’s announcement reports customers seeing up to a 57% reduction in origin load — a vendor figure, quoted as one. The mechanism is exactly the fix the model implies: put the pooling back by making one cache see all the misses.

Breslau, Cao, Fan, Phillips and Shenker got there in 1999. Web Caching and Zipf-like Distributions: Evidence and Implications (INFOCOM ‘99) modelled web accesses as independent draws from a Zipf-like distribution and found that a proxy’s hit ratio grows log-like in the cache size and in the number of requests the proxy sees. That second half is the statistical multiplexing result stated as a published finding twenty-seven years ago: hit ratio depends on the client population per cache, so dividing the population across caches lowers it. The model in this lesson derives it from a different starting point — TTL renewal rather than capacity — and agrees on the shape, which is the useful kind of corroboration.

Netflix Open Connect moves the servers instead of the packets, and it is the case where every trade in this lesson is cheap. Video segments are immutable and versioned, so the invalidation column is empty; a popular title’s per-PoP request rate is enormous, so the multiplexing loss barely registers; and the catalogue is small enough per region to fit on the appliance. It is the best possible workload for a CDN, which is why it is a bad model for yours.

TLS 1.3 and QUIC are round-trip-count changes, and that is why they were worth doing. RFC 8446 folded the TLS handshake into one round trip by putting the key share in the ClientHello; RFC 9000 folded the transport and crypto handshakes together so a first-ever QUIC connection costs one setup round trip. Neither made anything faster in the sense of bits per second. They removed 166.7 ms each from a Sydney user and 3.4 ms each from a Parisian, and the 0-RTT resumption mode in both removes the last one at the price of replay exposure for non-idempotent requests — the same “buy latency with a correctness risk” shape as stale-while-revalidate.

The versioned-asset pattern is universal and undersold. Webpack, Vite, Rails’ asset pipeline and every static site generator emit content-hashed filenames, and the HTTP header that makes them pay is Cache-Control: max-age=31536000, immutable (RFC 8246). The reason to know why rather than just to copy the config: it tells you what to do with the parts that cannot be hashed. Everything you can address by content, you cache forever and never invalidate. Everything you cannot, you serve with a TTL and accept (T/2) × hit ratio of staleness. There is no third option, and most invalidation architectures are an attempt to build one.

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • A CDN removes round trips rather than making them faster, and a cold TLS 1.2 request is four round trips of which three carry no application data
  • A TTL cache's per-object hit ratio is λT/(1+λT), so the arrival rate and the TTL only ever appear as their product
  • Dividing one request stream over N locations divides every object's λ by N, which lowers the aggregate hit ratio — statistical multiplexing loss, as in bulkheads
  • Mean age of the copy served is exactly (TTL ÷ 2) × hit ratio, so offload and staleness are the same number
  • Under Zipf popularity, hit ratio grows with the logarithm of cache size, so the last point of hit ratio costs hundreds of times more objects than the first
  • Origin fetches for one object at one PoP are capped at 1/TTL, so the origin's load scales with the number of PoPs rather than with user traffic
  • TTL expiry, purge and a versioned URL are three different invalidation costs: bounded staleness, a fan-out with a disagreement window, and no invalidation at all

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    A colleague says the CDN made the site four times faster. Nothing about the network changed. What did actually change, and why is four a suspicious number?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The count of round trips, not their duration. A cold TLS 1.2 request is four round trips to the server — TCP's handshake (1), the TLS 1.2 handshake (2), then the request and its response (1) — and three of those four carry no application data. An edge that terminates TLS makes all four short instead of long. Four is suspicious because it is exactly 4 ÷ 1: the ratio of a cold connection's round trips to a warm one's. London to Sydney the floor is 166.7 ms per round trip, so four of them is 667 ms of pure physics before a byte of content moves.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Same traffic, same TTL, same total cache. You go from 1 edge location to 128. Which way does the hit ratio move, and why?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Down, and steeply — measured 99.9% → 90.4%, which multiplies origin traffic by 87×. A TTL cache's hit ratio for one object is λT/(1+λT), and dividing the stream over 128 locations divides every object's λ by 128. Because x/(1+x) is concave, splitting a stream and re-averaging always loses. It is the same statistical multiplexing loss as splitting one connection pool into four (bulkheads) — the containment is easy to picture and the pooling gain was never itemised.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    Your TTL is one hour. How stale is the copy a user actually gets, on average?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Half the TTL times the hit ratio, exactly — because every miss is served fresh and every hit is served a copy uniformly aged over the TTL. At a 90.4% hit ratio that is 27.1 minutes, not 60 and not 30. The consequence is the uncomfortable one: you cannot raise the hit ratio without raising the mean staleness in exact proportion. They are the same number scaled by T/2.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Your hit ratio is 90% and you want 99%. Roughly what does that cost, and why is the answer not "10% more disk"?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    On the measured catalogue it is 94,555 → 186,025 objects: nine points of hit ratio for 91,470 more objects, when the first fifty points cost 2,274. Under Zipf popularity the hit ratio is logarithmic in cache size — at exponent 1.0 every tenfold increase in the cache buys the same 18 percentage points — so equal gains need equal multiples, and the marginal cost per point rose from 45 objects to about 11,900.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    You issue a purge and the graph shows the old value still being served. What is the mechanism, and what design avoids the question entirely?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    A purge is a fan-out to every PoP, and they do not all apply it at the same instant. Until the slowest one does, two users on two continents get different answers from the same URL — a window whose width is the fan-out's tail, not its mean. The design that removes the question is a versioned URL: put the content hash in the path, cache it for a year, and never invalidate anything, because a new version is a new URL that was never cached. That is what app.a83f21.js is for.
    Did you have that?
  6. Near transfer

    A service has one shared Redis cache with a 60-second TTL and a 96% hit ratio. Someone replaces it with an in-process cache in each of the 50 application instances — same total memory, same TTL, no network hop. What happens, and what would you actually do?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The same division. Every key's arrival rate at any one cache drops by 50×, so λT/(1+λT) collapses for everything outside the very hottest keys, and the database sees the difference. A key requested once a second globally went from 60/61 = 98.4% to λT = 60/50 = 1.2, so 55%. The lookup got faster and the backing store got hit several times harder. What you would actually do is both tiers: a small in-process cache for the handful of keys whose per-instance rate is still high, backed by the shared Redis so a local miss does not become a database query. That is tiered caching, and it is the same fix a CDN's mid-tier shield is.
    Did you have that?
  7. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: dividing one demand stream across more local stockpiles shortens every trip and destroys the pooling of demand that made each stockpile efficient, and the exchange rate is set by how concave the pooling gain is. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Retail and logistics is the canonical one: consolidating warehouses reduces total inventory roughly as the square root of the number of locations (the "square root law of inventory", Maister, 1976), which is the same arithmetic read backwards — every new depot shortens delivery and raises total stock. Blood banks, where a rare type held locally expires unused while a central bank turns it over. Spare parts depots for aircraft. Ambulance stations, where more stations cut response time and cut each station's utilisation. Library branch collections. In every case the same two questions decide it: what does proximity buy, and how much demand pooling am I giving up — and the second is the one nobody asks, because pooling was never a line item.
    Did you have that?
