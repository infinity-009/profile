Tier 4 · Distributed systems mechanics Logical clocks explained well nowhere else

A Lamport clock orders every pair of events and tells you nothing about any of them

Replacing wall clocks with logical ones fixes the reply-before-the-message bug completely and exactly. It does not fix conflict detection, and almost every explanation implies it does — a Lamport timestamp assigns an order to pairs that have none, and those invented orderings are indistinguishable from real ones. Half of them, typically. Detecting concurrency needs a vector, a vector costs one counter per participant, and that cost is why the modern answer is a hybrid.

01

The lost writes stopped, and then a comment appeared above the post it replied to

The previous lesson ends with an observation: you never wanted the time, you wanted the order. So this team did the right thing. They removed wall-clock timestamps from their conflict resolution and replaced them with Lamport timestamps — a counter per node, L = max(L, received) + 1 — which is the textbook fix and is genuinely correct for the problem it solves.

The silent write loss stopped. Completely, not statistically. A Lamport clock guarantees that if a happened before b, then L(a) < L(b), and that guarantee holds at any clock skew, on any network, forever. The class of bug where a reply is timestamped before its own message became impossible, which is a much stronger result than “rare”.

Then, a month later: a comment rendered above the post it was replying to. And an edit that reverted someone else’s paragraph. And a delete that un-deleted.

Because the guarantee runs one way, and only one way.

a → b implies L(a) < L(b)  ·  L(a) < L(b) implies nothing

The converse is false, and that is not a technicality — it is the entire content of the construction. A Lamport clock cannot produce an ordering that contradicts causality, and it cannot tell you which of its orderings causality actually required.

So every pair of events gets a number comparison, and some of those pairs were genuinely concurrent — neither caused the other, no order exists to report — and the clock returned an order anyway. Nothing marks those. In a four-process system where about a quarter of events send a message, roughly 56% of event pairs are concurrent, and a Lamport clock assigns an order to about 49% of all pairs that do not have one.

Half of what it tells you is invented, and it is mixed in with the half that is real.

02

Same graph, four clocks, four different stories

The belief worth naming, because it is what every introduction leaves you with: that a logical clock is the fixed version of a wall clock. It is presented as an upgrade — same job, no clock skew — and it is not the same job. A wall clock and a Lamport clock answer different questions, and neither answers the one an application usually needs.

There are three questions hiding in “which came first”:

  1. Did a happen before b? Three possible answers, including “neither”.
  2. Give me some total order. Always answerable, never informative.
  3. Did a and b conflict? The one you actually need, and the only one that requires question 1’s third answer.

A wall clock answers (2) and gets (1) wrong. A Lamport clock answers (2) and answers (1) in one direction only, so it cannot reach (3) even in principle. Below is one event graph judged by each.

Predict Four processes, a fixed event graph, one event selected. Judged by a vector clock, 14 of the other 35 events are concurrent with it. You switch the selector to a Lamport clock — same graph, same events, nothing else changed. How many does the Lamport clock report as concurrent?
A Lamport timestamp is one number, and two distinct numbers are always ordered — there is no value a comparison can return that means incomparable. So all 14 concurrent events get placed on one side or the other, and the only ones reported as concurrent are exact ties. Causality is still intact: no real relationship is contradicted. It is the absences that cannot survive the round trip through a scalar, and absence of order is exactly what a conflict is.
Show it without answering

Four things to take from it.

The hollow dots are the whole lesson. They are an absence — no order exists between those events and the selected one — which is why they are drawn hollow rather than in a third colour. A conflict is exactly this absence, so a clock that cannot render it cannot detect one.

Switching to Lamport fills every hollow dot and breaks nothing. Watch causality broken stay at 0 while orderings invented goes from 0 to the entire concurrent set. That combination — perfectly safe and completely uninformative — is the state most systems are in, and it does not look like a problem from inside.

Only the wall clock produces crosses. Raise the skew and real causal edges start being reported backwards. No logical clock can do this at any skew; it is the one failure mode that is exclusive to physical time.

Turn the communication rate down and the concurrent set grows. Causality is created by messages and nothing else, so a system whose parts rarely talk has almost no causal structure to recover — at zero communication about 77% of pairs are concurrent. The quieter your system, the more of any total order over it is fiction.

03

The intuition: letters, not clocks

Forget clocks. You and three colleagues work in different cities and communicate only by letter. Each of you keeps a numbered diary of what you did.

When can you be certain that your colleague’s action happened before yours? Only in one situation: you read about it in a letter before you acted. That is it. Information reached you, and you acted afterwards. If no letter carried it, then as far as the world is concerned the two things happened independently — and asking which came first is not a hard question, it is a question with no answer. Nobody could have been influenced by the other.

Now notice what a Lamport clock is. Each letter carries “my diary is up to entry 40”. You bump your own counter past anything you receive. That construction guarantees the thing you want: anything you learned about has a lower number than what you did next. Causality is preserved exactly.

And notice what it cannot do. Your diary says entry 41, your colleague’s says entry 47. Did theirs happen after yours? You cannot tell. Maybe they read your letter and continued — or maybe they had simply been busier that month and no letter ever passed between you. One number cannot distinguish “later because of you” from “just further along”.

The fix is the obvious one once stated: each letter carries what you know about everyone. “I’m at 41, I’ve heard up to your 38, up to Bob’s 12, up to Dana’s 5.” Now compare two of those lists. If every entry in mine is less than or equal to yours, I definitely came first. If some of mine are higher and some lower, neither of us came first — we were both doing things the other had not heard about. That is a vector clock, and the incomparable case is not a failure of the comparison, it is the correct answer.

The price is visible in the analogy too: the letter now has a line per colleague. With three colleagues that is nothing. With a thousand correspondents the postscript is longer than the letter.

Causality travels only along messages. Two events with no chain of messages between them are concurrent, and “which came first” has no answer — so any mechanism that always returns an answer is manufacturing one. A scalar always returns an answer.

04

The mechanism: one counter, n counters, and the hybrid

Lamport clock (1978). One counter per process. Increment on every local event; on receiving a message, L = max(L, L_message) + 1.

a → b ⟹ L(a) < L(b)  ·  and the converse does not hold

Ties are broken by process id to get a total order, which is where “arbitrary but consistent” comes from. Asserted in causality.test.mjs across every communication rate: zero causality violations, always.

Vector clock (Fidge 1988, Mattern 1989). One counter per participant. Increment your own entry; on receive, take the pointwise maximum and then increment your own. Comparison:

V(a) ≤ V(b) elementwise ⟹ a → b  ·  some higher and some lower ⟹ concurrent

This is exact: the comparison agrees with transitive reachability over the event graph, which is the definition of happens-before. That equivalence is the theorem, and the test checks it over 49,728 event pairs across a sweep of graph shapes — because every other number in this lesson is graded against vector-clock comparison, so it has to be right first.

How much of a total order is invented. Four processes, nine events each, averaged over eight seeds:

Even at the absurd extreme where every single event sends a message, a quarter of pairs are concurrent and nearly a fifth of all orderings are fiction. There is no communication rate at which a Lamport total order becomes trustworthy, because concurrency is not noise to be reduced — it is what a distributed system is.

The size problem, which is why vectors are not simply the answer.

The decision that sets this cost is not “vector or scalar”, it is what counts as a participant. Keyed by replica — properly called a version vector — the length is the replication factor, and it never grows. Keyed by client or actor, it is bounded by nothing, and a popular object accumulates an entry for every writer that has ever touched it.

And truncating to fit is not a graceful degradation. A pruned entry can make two concurrent versions compare as ordered, which is silent loss — the exact failure you adopted vector clocks to escape, reintroduced by the size fix.

Hybrid logical clock (Kulkarni et al. 2014). A physical timestamp that is forbidden to go backwards, with a counter for ties:

l ← max(lprev, lmessage, physical now)  ·  c increments only on a tie

Causally safe like Lamport, totally ordered, 64 bits, and never more than the clock skew ahead of physical time — so the value is still human-readable and still comparable against wall-clock timestamps elsewhere in the system. The test asserts zero causality violations at skews up to ten seconds.

That combination is why every database written after 2014 uses one, and the honest way to state what it is: an HLC is the best scalar available, and it is still a scalar. It cannot report concurrency, for the same reason nothing else with one number can.

That is not a coincidence of this table; it is the trade, and no fifth column fixes it. A total order is a function to a totally ordered set, and such a function cannot have incomparable outputs.

Every logical clock in the table above has a hard zero in every cell of this one.

What to use.

  • Never wall-clock timestamps for ordering. The previous lesson’s conclusion, and this table is the reason: the failure is not rare, it is one in seven at ordinary datacentre numbers.
  • Default to a hybrid logical clock for anything that needs a timestamp. It is a strict improvement on both a wall clock and a Lamport clock at the same 8 bytes, and it stays readable.
  • Add a vector only where you need to detect a conflict, and then key it by replica, not by client. If you find yourself keying by client, the real problem is usually that the unit of concurrency is too coarse.
  • Never truncate a vector clock to save space. If it is too big, the participant definition is wrong. Truncation converts a bounded cost into an unbounded correctness risk.
  • If you have a vector, you have a merge. Detecting concurrency does not resolve it. Decide who resolves it — the application, a CRDT, or the user — before you deploy the detection, or the sibling values will pile up unhandled.
  • Consider not needing an order at all. Making the unit of concurrency finer — per field, per paragraph, per item — removes conflicts rather than resolving them, and is usually cheaper than any clock.
06

The trade-off: what you pay to know that you do not know

Every clock here buys something by giving up something, and the giving-up is uniformly less discussed than the buying.

A Lamport clock buys causal safety for free and pays in false confidence. Eight bytes, no coordination, no synchronised clocks, and it can never contradict causality. What it costs is that its output looks like information and is half fiction, with no marker distinguishing the halves. That is a worse position than a wall clock in one specific respect: a wall clock’s failures are at least in principle detectable — a reply before its message is visibly absurd — whereas an invented Lamport ordering is indistinguishable from a real one by construction. You cannot audit it, even in retrospect, from the timestamps alone.

A vector clock buys exactness and pays in size and in a decision you now have to make. The size is the famous cost and the second one is the real one: once you can see that two versions are concurrent, you must do something about it, and there is no default. Both versions get kept, and now the application, or the user, has to merge. Systems adopt vector clocks to be correct and then discover they have imported a product question — “what should happen when two people edit the same thing?” — which nobody had to answer while last-write-wins was silently answering it.

A hybrid logical clock buys nearly everything and pays by not solving the problem you may actually have. It is the right default and it is not a conflict detector, and the danger is that it feels like enough. It has a timestamp you can read, a total order, causal safety, and 64 bits — so a design reviewed against a wall-clock baseline looks entirely fixed, and the concurrent writes are still being resolved arbitrarily.

And what none of them can buy: a total order that is also honest. This is not an engineering limitation to be designed around, it is arithmetic. A total order is a map into a totally ordered set; happens-before is a partial order with genuinely incomparable elements; and no such map can preserve incomparability, because the target has none to map onto. Any single value you attach to an event has thrown the concurrency away before you compare anything.

Which suggests the move worth more than any of them, and it is the one the near-transfer question turns on: stop needing the order. Two edits to different paragraphs, two increments of a counter, two additions to a set — these do not conflict, and the only reason they appear to is that the unit of concurrency was drawn around the whole document, the whole row, the whole object. Make the unit smaller and the concurrency stops being a conflict rather than being resolved. That is what CRDTs are for, and it is the next lesson.

07

In the wild

Lamport, “Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System” (CACM 21(7), 1978) is the origin, and it is worth reading for how carefully it states the one-directional guarantee that later summaries drop. The paper is explicit that the total order it constructs is arbitrary among concurrent events, and it introduces the space-time diagram this lesson’s rig draws. It is also the most-cited paper in distributed systems, which makes the widespread misreading of its central asymmetry all the more striking.

Fidge (1988) and Mattern (1989) independently gave the vector construction and the theorem that its comparison coincides exactly with happens-before. That equivalence is what makes vector-clock comparison usable as ground truth, including in this lesson’s own tests.

Amazon Dynamo (SOSP 2007) is the production case study for the size problem. It used version vectors for conflict detection, hit vector growth in practice, and truncated with a timestamp-based limit — and the paper states plainly that this can lose causality. Riak carried the design forward and later moved to dotted version vectors specifically to bound the growth without the truncation hazard. The distinction between per-client and per-replica keying is the whole difference, and Dynamo’s is the documented case of getting it wrong at scale.

Kulkarni, Demirbas, Madappa, Avva & Leone, “Logical Physical Clocks” (OPODIS 2014) introduced HLCs, and the adoption is the argument: CockroachDB, MongoDB (as its cluster time) and YugabyteDB all use one for transaction ordering. What they get is Lamport’s safety in a value that fits an existing 64-bit timestamp and still means something to a human — and none of them claims it detects conflicts.

Spanner is the interesting contrast, because it went the other way: rather than abandon physical time, it bounded the error and waited it out. HLC and TrueTime are two answers to the same question, and the difference is whether you can afford GPS receivers in every datacentre. Git is the everyday example nobody labels as one — a commit’s parent pointers are a causal graph, the merge base is a lowest common ancestor in it, and a merge conflict is literally the concurrent case being handed to the user because the system correctly refused to invent an order.

DDIA ch. 5, “Detecting Concurrent Writes”, and ch. 9 on ordering guarantees, are the reference treatment. Ch. 5’s version-vector walkthrough is the clearest prose derivation of why the comparison needs more than one number.

Free recall — do this before you scroll back

Write down everything you remember from this lesson. Don't look. Unstructured, no cues, nothing to recognise — this is the hardest and highest-yield thing here, and any hint spends it.

  • "Which came first" has three answers, not two, and the third one — concurrent — is the one applications need in order to detect a conflict
  • A Lamport clock guarantees a → b implies L(a) < L(b), and the converse is false, so L(a) < L(b) carries no information about causality
  • A Lamport clock therefore orders every pair including the concurrent ones, and nothing marks which orderings were invented
  • A vector clock's comparison is exact — before, after, or concurrent — because it agrees with transitive reachability over the event graph
  • A vector clock costs one counter per participant, so keying it by replica is cheap and keying it by client is unbounded
  • Truncating a vector clock to bound its size can make two concurrent versions compare as ordered, which is silent loss again
  • A vector clock provides no total order, so concurrent versions must both be kept and the merge decision moves to the application
  • A hybrid logical clock is causally safe, totally ordered, comparable with wall time and 64 bits, and still cannot report concurrency, because one number cannot
  • The less processes communicate, the more pairs are concurrent, so a total order over a quiet system is almost entirely fiction

Score yourself on the list, not on how much you wrote. A missing item is a located gap, which is worth more than a page of recall that skirted it.

  1. Cued recall

    You replaced wall-clock timestamps with Lamport timestamps and the lost writes stopped. A month later a comment appears above the post it replies to. What did the change fix, and what did it not?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It fixed causality violations — completely, and by construction. If a → b then L(a) < L(b), so no Lamport clock can ever place a reply before its own message, at any skew. What it did not fix is conflict detection, because the guarantee runs one way only: L(a) < L(b) does not imply a → b. So the clock still assigns an order to every pair, including pairs that were genuinely concurrent, and nothing distinguishes an invented ordering from a real one. The comment and the post were concurrent; something picked one, consistently and arbitrarily.
    Did you have that?
  2. Cued recall

    Why can a single number never report that two events were concurrent, no matter how it is computed?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Because concurrency is the absence of a relation and numbers are totally ordered. Given two distinct integers, one is smaller — there is no third outcome available to express. Reporting "neither preceded the other" requires a comparison that can return incomparable, and that requires at least one dimension per independent participant, which is precisely what a vector clock is. This applies to hybrid logical clocks too, which are causally safe and still cannot detect a conflict.
    Did you have that?
  3. Cued recall

    In a four-process system where about a quarter of events send a message, roughly what fraction of event pairs are concurrent — and what does that make of a Lamport total order?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    About 56%, and a Lamport clock assigns an order to roughly 49% of all pairs that do not have one. So more than half of the orderings it reports are fiction, mixed indistinguishably with the real ones. And it gets worse as the system gets quieter: with no communication at all, about 77% of pairs are concurrent, because causality only travels along messages. A total order over a system that barely talks to itself is almost entirely invented.
    Did you have that?
  4. Cued recall

    Vector clocks are exact. Why does anyone use anything else?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    Two costs. Size: one counter per participant, so 24 bytes for three replicas and 8 kB for a thousand clients — at which point the version metadata is forty times the 200-byte value it describes. And truncating to fit is not a graceful degradation: a pruned entry can make two concurrent versions compare as ordered, which is the silent loss you adopted vector clocks to escape. No total order: concurrent versions are both kept, so the application must merge them, and "the application must merge them" is a product decision disguised as a storage one.
    Did you have that?
  5. Cued recall

    What is the difference between keying a vector by replica and keying it by client, and why did it matter for Dynamo?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The key determines whether the size is bounded. Keyed by replica — a version vector — the length is the replication factor, which is 3 or 5 and never grows. Keyed by client or actor, it is bounded by nothing, and a popular object accumulates an entry per writer that ever touched it. Dynamo hit this and had to truncate with a timestamp-based limit, which the paper acknowledges can lose causality. The lesson generalises: decide what a participant is before deciding to use a vector, because that choice, not the data structure, is what sets the cost.
    Did you have that?
  6. Cued recall

    A hybrid logical clock is 64 bits, never contradicts causality, gives a total order, and is comparable with wall-clock time. What is the catch?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    It still cannot report concurrency — it is one number, so the previous answer applies. What it buys is everything else a wall clock had: it stays within the clock skew of physical time, so timestamps remain human-readable and comparable across the system, and it fits an existing 64-bit timestamp column. That combination is why CockroachDB, MongoDB and YugabyteDB all use one. The design admits the trade honestly: if you need conflict detection you need a vector somewhere, and an HLC is what you use for everything else.
    Did you have that?
  7. Near transfer

    A team builds collaborative document editing. Each client stamps its edits with a Lamport timestamp taken from a counter it shares with the server, and the server applies edits in Lamport order. Testing looks good. In production, two people editing different paragraphs simultaneously sometimes see one person's paragraph revert. What is happening, and what is the fix?

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The two edits were concurrent, and the Lamport clock ordered them anyway. Applying them in that order is fine when they touch different paragraphs and last-write-wins is applied per document — the later-ordered edit's document state simply does not contain the other person's paragraph, so it reverts. The Lamport clock did nothing wrong; it correctly reported an order, and the order was invented. Two clues confirm it: it only happens with simultaneous edits, and which paragraph reverts depends on who the server heard from second. The fix is not a better clock, it is to stop needing a total order: make the unit of concurrency the paragraph rather than the document, so concurrent edits to different paragraphs do not conflict at all — which is what operational transformation and CRDTs do. If the document must stay the unit, you need a vector clock per document to detect the concurrency, and then a merge, and then a decision about what to show the user. Note that the merge is unavoidable once concurrency is detected; the only thing you get to choose is whether the user or the clock makes it silently.
    Did you have that?
  8. Far transfer

    The abstraction is: a measurement forced to return a totally ordered answer must invent one when the truth is "incomparable", and the invention is indistinguishable from a real result. Name instances outside computing.

    How sure are you, before you look?
    The abstraction is a forced ranking over a partial order — the underlying reality has genuine incomparabilities, the instrument has no way to say so, and its output looks the same either way. Instances: ranking universities on one number when they are better at different things, so the ordering between two near-equal institutions is manufactured by the weighting; a job interview scorecard that produces "candidate A: 4.2, candidate B: 4.1" from incommensurable strengths; single-winner voting over a Condorcet cycle, where a majority genuinely prefers each candidate to another in a loop and the system must still name a winner; a pain scale from one to ten; performance review stack-ranking a team whose members do unrelated work. The giveaway is always the same: nobody can reproduce the ordering of the middle of the list, and the ties that were reported as small differences were the honest answer being suppressed.
    Did you have that?

Graded items get a due date on an expanding ladder — 1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 90, 180 days — and land in review mixed with every other lesson's items. Mixed, not grouped: blocking one topic teaches you what its problems have in common, while interleaving forces you to tell them apart, which is the skill that actually fails in an interview and in production. Nothing is marked mastered today — same-day correctness measures how accessible a thing is right now, not how well you learned it.
