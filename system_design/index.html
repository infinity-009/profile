TIER 0 Why anything is hard at all 28/30

The tier that does not exist anywhere else. Every other resource opens at "add a load balancer", which answers before the question has been asked.

Operational vs analytical orphan 1 rig One database can accept orders and answer reports, until the report becomes the workload. The first architectural fork is not which database to buy; it is which data is authoritative, which data is derived, and where each kind of work is allowed to run. Latency numbers 2 rigs Jeff Dean's table of latency numbers is on every system-design cheat sheet, and almost nobody can use it — because nine orders of magnitude do not fit in a human head. The ratios do, and they are the part that decides your design. One request end to end orphan 1 rig A request passes through two kernel queues before any line of your code runs, and both of them drop connections without producing a single application-visible event. At 20,000 new connections a second the modern default accept queue absorbs 204.8 ms of silence — the historical default absorbed 6.4 ms. This is the vocabulary the rest of the tier assumes. Back-of-envelope orphan 1 rig Estimation is not about being right. It is about being able to reject a claim in two minutes with arithmetic anyone can check — and about discovering, before you build, that one unstated input decides the entire design. Byte budgets orphan 1 rig Everyone estimates storage from the payload and is wrong by an order of magnitude, systematically and in the same direction. The multipliers — row overhead, indexes, compression that does not apply, rendition ladders, replication — are each individually unsurprising and they compound. Worse, the smallest rows have the worst overhead ratio, so the table you thought was cheap is the expensive one. Little's Law 1 rig L = λW is the only law in this curriculum that assumes nothing — no arrival pattern, no service-time distribution, no queue discipline. It turns a latency into a headcount, runs backwards to catch a latency metric that is lying to you, and hands you a confidently wrong answer in exactly two situations. Queueing & utilisation orphan 3 rigs Nothing broke, no bug shipped, the CPU is not at 100% — and your service is five times slower than it was yesterday. This is the first thing to understand about systems at scale, and it happens on a single machine. Throughput vs latency orphan 1 rig Bandwidth is a capacity, throughput is an achieved rate, latency is a duration for one request, and the three get used as synonyms. They are not reciprocals — `throughput = concurrency / latency`, so a third variable breaks the link — and they routinely move in opposite directions, which is why every batch size is a defensible answer. The number worth carrying: a 5 ms latency budget lets you use 87.7% of a machine, and the last twelve points cost a tenfold budget. Write path / read path orphan 1 rig An index, a cache, a materialised view, a denormalised column, a precomputed timeline — these are taught as six techniques and they are one decision made six times: how much of the derivation happens eagerly at write time versus lazily at read time. The work is moved, not removed. And the optimum is a corner: across six decades of plausible read/write ratios, the middle is best in under one per cent of them. One machine's ceiling 1 rig CPU, memory, disk and network all run out eventually — but only one of them runs out first, and the other three are money you have already spent and cannot reach. Knowing which one gives out is the whole skill, and the CPU graph will not tell you. Event loops & C10K orphan 1 rig The two reasons everyone gives for choosing an event loop — threads eat memory, context switches eat CPU — are both off by two orders of magnitude on a modern machine. The reason that is real never gets stated: a loop's whole advantage is that it never blocks, and one synchronous call in one handler takes 11× the throughput off eight cores. Blocking vs async orphan 1 rig A blocked thread costs memory and zero CPU. A retry loop costs CPU and zero memory. An async completion costs a queue entry, a share of one wakeup, and a per-operation fee the other two never pay — so below a computable concurrency, and above a computable ready fraction, "make it async" is a rewrite that ends with more CPU per operation than it started with. Amdahl & the USL orphan 1 rig Tripling a fleet from 32 machines to 96 can leave you with less throughput than you started with — and 20 machines would have matched all three of those numbers. Amdahl's Law explains the flattening; Gunther's Universal Scalability Law explains the decline. Vertical vs horizontal orphan 1 rig Vertical and horizontal scaling are not two settings of one dial — they raise N against different terms of the same equation. One machine of 50 units and 50 machines of one unit do exactly the same amount of work, and nine machines of 5.6 units do 2.7 times more from identical hardware. Performance vs scalability orphan 1 rig Performance is how long one unit of work takes for one user. Scalability is whether that number survives more load or more data. They are separate axes, all four quadrants are populated, and the interesting designs are the ones where buying one sells the other. The number worth carrying: put 1.2 ms of an 8 ms request inside a lock and you have set a ceiling of 833 requests a second that no amount of hardware moves. Why averages lie orphan 1 rig Your dashboard says 26 milliseconds and it is not lying. Four requests in five are faster than that number, one in a thousand takes three hundred, and the figure on the dashboard is not a percentile of anything. Then somebody averages the p99s across ten machines and the last honest number on the page is gone too. Availability math 1 rig Series multiplies availability down and parallel multiplies failure down, and the two feel like they belong to different universes. They do not: adding a required dependency and adding a second replica move a downtime budget by exactly the same number of minutes, always, at every component availability. The third replica moves it by a thousandth of that, and the fourth buys thirty-one milliseconds of uptime a year. Seven three-nines services in a row are two nines, not three. MTBF and MTTR orphan 1 rig Availability is a duty cycle — MTBF over MTBF plus MTTR — which means it depends on the two means only through their ratio. So one four-hour outage a year and fifty-two five-minute outages a year are the same percentage and nothing like the same system, and a thing that fails every single day but recovers in twenty seconds beats both. Halving repair time and doubling reliability buy exactly the same availability; they do not cost remotely the same. The three guarantees orphan 1 rig Availability, reliability and durability are not three grades of the same thing — they are measured in three different units, so a number in one says nothing about the other two. Availability has no time argument at all, which is why one SLO figure covers systems whose chance of an incident-free year differs by 136 orders of magnitude. And durability has no repair term, which is why eleven nines of it sits beside four nines of availability in the same published spec sheet, honestly. The tail at scale orphan 1 rig One hundred shards, each answering in 10 milliseconds, each slow only 1% of the time. Every shard dashboard is green. And 63% of user requests take more than a second, because the user does not wait for the average backend — the user waits for the worst one. This is multiplication, not a bug. Hedged requests orphan 1 rig A hedge fires only when the first attempt has already crossed the 95th percentile, so it costs 5% more requests rather than double — and once it has fired the leg is the minimum of two draws, so the tail gets squared. Both halves stop being true at the same moment: when the slowness has a shared cause, or when the extra 5% is what saturated the system. The break-even is 1/(1 + 2e), which is 91% utilisation for a p95 hedge. Stop-the-world pauses orphan 1 rig A garbage collector, a hypervisor stealing a vCPU, a cgroup CPU quota running out mid-slice and a writeback storm all do the same thing: the process stops for a while and does not know it stopped. That produces a latency bug with a cliff in it — cross 1% of requests and your p99 is the pause length, whatever your code does — and a correctness bug that is worse, because a paused process is indistinguishable from a crashed one and its peers will give its lease away while it still believes it holds it. Failure is normal orphan 1 rig Every reliability figure you are given is per component and per year. Every question you have is about the whole fleet and a much shorter interval. The conversion is one multiplication and it changes what a failure *is*: at Backblaze's published 1.4% annualised rate, a rack of 480 drives loses one every 54 days and a hundred thousand drives lose one every 6.3 hours. Then the harder finding — the failure rate is not constant with age, and a constant-hazard model calibrated on your own first year understates the next five by 3.02×. Single point of failure orphan 1 rig The phrase is easy to define and almost impossible to apply to your own system, for a structural reason: the redundancy you build is in the data plane, where the boxes on the diagram are, and the single points that survive are in the control plane, which is not on the diagram at all. A config service, a service registry, a secret store, one DNS zone, one certificate job, one identity provider. Each is required by everything and drawn by nobody — and giving them a second instance makes availability *worse*. Correlated failure orphan 1 rig Redundancy multiplies only if the failures are independent. Put one per cent of shared cause into a pair of three-nines replicas and they are eleven times more likely to fail together than the arithmetic promised — and past the third replica, adding more buys literally nothing, because the shared term does not shrink. Worse, six of the ten shared causes are immune to placement diversity, which is the only fix the industry has seriously engineered. Maintainability orphan 1 rig Maintainability loses every planning meeting for a structural reason rather than a cultural one: the feature is a benefit now and every maintainability property is a cost avoided later. The fix is arithmetic. All three properties the word covers are separately computable, two of them have dates on them — a team at 20% toil on a fleet growing 40% a year is a pure operations function in 4.8 years — and the module boundary everyone reaches for is worth 15× when it seals and 1.1× when a fifth of it leaks. The microsecond regime orphan 1 rig Below about a hundred microseconds, latency stops being about how fast your work runs and becomes about how many layers your work passes through. Every technique here removes a layer rather than speeding one up — and most systems should not pay what that costs. Client-perceived latency orphan 1 rig Every percentile on your dashboard is computed over requests that arrived. The user's percentile is computed over intents — and the two diverge twice over. Once because time-to-usable is a chain of round trips your server contributes 2.6% of, and again because the users who gave up never sent the beacon that would have put them in your data.

TIER 1 The network 32/34

TCP, TLS, HTTP/3, and the real-time menu — only the parts that change a decision.

DNS and TTLs orphan 1 rig DNS is a naming system with caching, and almost everyone uses it as a control plane. Caches in series add their TTLs rather than sharing one, negative answers get a TTL sixty times longer than the record you were publishing, and an RFC-compliant resolver may serve an expired answer for a week — precisely when your nameservers are the thing that broke. Record types and the apex orphan 1 rig A CNAME says "this name is somewhere else", and RFC 1034 forbids anything else from sharing that name. A zone's apex must carry its SOA and its NS records. So of the 512 combinations of the nine standard record types, 64 are legal at an apex and none of them contains a CNAME. Every provider's ALIAS is a workaround with a bill: an extra resolution, a TTL they choose, and the CDN's geo-routing answered from their network instead of yours. Routing policies orphan 1 rig Weighted, latency-based, geo and health-checked DNS are sold as global load balancing. They divide resolutions, not requests, and nothing in the loop ever observes a queue. At 8,000 requests per second behind a 300-second TTL, one routing decision commits 80,001 requests — and the realised split's standard deviation depends on the resolver population, not on your weight. You would need 2,401 equally-sized resolvers to land within two points. Anycast orphan 1 rig One IP, announced from every PoP, and each router picks the shortest AS-path to it — a count of peering hops that reflects who sells transit to whom, not distance. On a network with realistic peering density that sends a majority of vantage points to a PoP that is not their nearest, by a detour bigger than the trip itself. And when the catchment changes, nothing tells the TCP connection riding it: a withdrawal reaches half the network in about two minutes at the classic 30-second MRAI, and a session that long survives that change only as often as `exp(−t/MTBF)` says it will. Connection setup orphan 1 rig A request is not one round trip. A cold HTTPS request is four, three of which carry no application data at all — so for a small response the answer arrives when distance says it can, and a wider link changes it by a rounding error. The number worth knowing is how big a response has to get before the bytes cost more than the introductions. The layers, briefly orphan 1 rig Seven layers exist so each one can be reasoned about, and changed, without touching the others — but almost every real decision only touches two or three of them. This lesson puts a number on the two places that separation actually bites: header overhead, which is 73% of a 20-byte VoIP sample and under 1% of an 8 kB file, and path MTU, where adding a tunnel silently eats bytes at layer 2 or 3 while every symptom shows up as a mysterious timeout at layer 7 — three layers and, often, a different team away from the change that caused it. IP, subnets, NAT orphan 1 rig Splitting a block into equal subnets is not free — every new subnet needs its own reserved network and broadcast address, so a /24 split four ways loses 6 usable hosts to bookkeeping it didn't need before. One layer up, a carrier-grade NAT shares one public IP's 64,512 usable ports across every subscriber behind it: at a realistic subscriber count, that block can be smaller than the concurrent connections Little's Law says one person's browsing needs — a hard exhaustion nobody's application code will ever see coming. And the reason both exist at all: 86.6% of a 32-bit address space was never going to cover billions of devices, while a single standard IPv6 subnet holds 4.3 billion IPv4 internets' worth of addresses. Packet loss and retransmit orphan 1 rig Fast retransmit needs three duplicate ACKs, which needs three packets to arrive after the lost one — a packet lost near the end of a burst has no followers to generate that evidence, so it waits out the retransmission timer instead. That timer is floored at one full second by RFC 6298 regardless of how fast the network actually is, and a lost SYN before any RTT sample exists starts there and doubles on every retry: six retries is 63 seconds before a connection attempt gives up, on a formula anyone can compute, not a mystery hang. Congestion control orphan 1 rig A sender is not told its share of the network; it has to guess, and every algorithm guesses from a different measurement. Guess from dropped packets and you need a full buffer to learn anything, so you build the delay you then report. Guess from delivered rate and you keep 93% of a link that 1% loss reduces the other sender to under 1% of. And a short transfer never gets far enough into any of this to find out, because it spends its whole life doubling. Bandwidth-delay product orphan 1 rig One connection's throughput is a window divided by a round-trip time. That makes distance a divisor and bandwidth only a ceiling — so an intercontinental 10 Gbps link carries a single stream at a thousandth of its rate, and the fix is never a faster link. The 40 ms mystery orphan 1 rig Nagle will not send a small segment while anything is unacknowledged. Delayed ACK will not acknowledge until it has data to piggyback on or a second full segment to cover. Put them on opposite ends of one connection and each waits for the other, so the latency is decided by a timer — 40 ms on Linux, 200 on Windows, 500 by the letter of the RFC. Both algorithms are correct. Neither is broken. And the escape is not a big first write, it is a remainder of zero. UDP, and when you want it orphan 1 rig This isn't "TCP is sometimes too slow for real-time media" — it's categorical. RFC 6298 floors TCP's retransmission timer at a full second no matter how fast the network is, and no real-time jitter buffer can afford to hold a second of audio or video waiting to find out if a packet is coming. UDP drops the late packet and moves on; the gap gets concealed, not fixed. And the same protocol that can't wait for anyone has a second unrelated superpower: multicast delivers the identical stream to any number of receivers for the cost of one send, where unicast — TCP's only option — costs exactly proportional to how many are watching. Port exhaustion orphan 1 rig A connection is identified by five things, four of which are fixed when you talk to one backend, so the only free variable is the source port — and there are 28,232 of them. A closed socket holds its port for another 60 seconds, which turns a count into a rate: about 470 new connections a second to any one destination, forever, no matter how fast the requests are. A port pool has no waiting room, so the failure is Erlang-B, and the first failures appear 0.8% below the ceiling. There is no degraded region to alert on. Connection pooling orphan 1 rig A pool is a queue with a server count you chose by hand, so its size is set by Little's Law and the wait in front of it is Erlang-C shaped. That makes both mistakes look identical from the outside — too few connections and callers wait while the backend idles, too many and the queue moves inside the backend where you cannot time it out — and it makes the number the database actually sees a product nobody configured. Head-of-line blocking orphan 1 rig HTTP/2 put forty requests on one connection and deleted the blocking that HTTP/1.1's six-connection workaround existed to hide. It did not delete the other one: TCP hands the application a single byte order, so one lost segment stalls every stream behind it — including the thirty-two that lost nothing. HTTP/3 moves the blocking below loss recovery. It cannot move the coupling nobody can remove, because one connection is still one congestion window. TLS on the wire orphan 1 rig The previous lesson counted the round trips. This one opens them. A server's first TLS flight is overwhelmingly one message — the certificate chain — and its size is set by your certificate authority's hierarchy and your key algorithm, not by your protocol version. On QUIC that size decides whether a cold handshake takes one round trip or two. TLS 1.3, 0-RTT orphan 1 rig Turning on session resumption without 0-RTT is a common, well-intentioned change that saves nothing: a resumed handshake still runs a full round trip of TLS messages before the server will accept ordinary application data, identically to a fresh one. Only the `early_data` extension — 0-RTT — removes that round trip, by letting the client attach a request to its very first flight. And that's exactly why it's the one mode with no freshness guarantee: a captured first flight can be replayed by anyone holding a copy of it, up to 1.8 million times inside one day's ticket lifetime at a typical round trip time, unless the server pays for a dedup cache or restricts 0-RTT to requests a repeat can't damage. mTLS orphan 1 rig Asking the client to prove its identity too is not "TLS, but twice." The added CPU is small and landed almost entirely on the server, because a chain-verify is expensive and a signature is cheap. The real bill is operational: every client now needs a certificate before it can connect at all, and a client fleet is usually orders of magnitude bigger than a server fleet. Revocation orphan 1 rig Revoking a certificate and a client actually refusing it are two different moments, and the gap between them is a deliberate configuration choice, not a network glitch. A CRL is stale until its next scheduled update; a stapled OCSP response is stale until the server refreshes it; and a live OCSP query — the only one of the three with no built-in staleness — is also the only one that silently stops checking anything at all the moment its responder is unreachable. HTTP semantics orphan 1 rig Safe, idempotent, and cacheable are not three descriptions of the same good behavior — they are three independent, RFC-fixed properties, and a method or a status code either has one or it doesn't regardless of what any specific API author intends. What that buys is concrete: retrying an idempotent request after a timeout is provably harmless, while retrying a non-idempotent one is a measurable, nonzero risk that doesn't depend on which non-idempotent method you picked. And a cache is allowed to guess freshness for a response with zero caching headers — but only for a short, fixed list of status codes, error pages included. HTTP caching semantics 1 rig A 304 Not Modified is a request. It costs a whole round trip, plus the entire connection handshake if the connection is cold, and it removes only the body — so on a small resource revalidation saves half the bytes and 0.2% of the time. The strategy that wins does not answer the freshness question faster; it renames the file so there is nothing to ask. HTTP/2 multiplexing orphan 1 rig HTTP/2 added its own flow control on top of TCP's, and its default window of 65,535 bytes is smaller than the bandwidth-delay product of essentially every path you care about — a gigabit connection at 100 ms is short by 191×, and a 1 ms LAN still caps gigabit at half. There is a second window shared across streams, where one reader that stops reading leaves exactly zero bytes for the other thirty-nine. And HPACK makes the streams order-dependent above the transport, which is why QUIC cannot remove that coupling either. QUIC and HTTP/3 orphan 1 rig A TCP connection *is* its 5-tuple, so any event that changes an address ends it. A QUIC connection is identified by a connection ID it carries, so it survives a Wi-Fi-to-cellular handover that would kill TCP outright. That is real, it is the one QUIC feature with no TCP equivalent — and it removes about two-fifths of what a network change costs, because both transports still have to climb out of slow start. Then a load balancer takes even that back. Transport compression orphan 1 rig Compressing a response costs time to save time, so there is a bandwidth above which it is a straight loss — and it is `(1 − 1/ratio)` over the harmonic sum of compress and decompress throughput. That puts brotli-11 at 5.6 Mbps, gzip-6 at 180, and zstd-1 at 1.8 Gbps. Compressibility barely enters it: the saved fraction varies by 1.2× across every codec here while their throughput varies by 522×. And the level you should pick is not a fact about your content — it is a fact about whether you can cache the result. Pagination orphan 1 rig `OFFSET k LIMIT s` cannot jump. The engine walks k rows and throws them away, so page depth is a cost and a full walk is quadratic — which is why a table growing 3.3× turned a 37-minute export into a 7-hour one. The second problem is worse and almost never named: an offset is a count into a list that is still being edited, so a reader scrolling a live feed is shown rows they have already seen and never shown rows that were there when they started. Past one threshold they make no progress at all, and every request returns 200 with a full page. REST & versioning orphan 1 rig "Versioning" sounds like one decision — v1, v2, a header, a URL — but the number that actually matters is a policy choice hiding behind it: does a new version have to stay compatible with the ONE before it, with EVERY version ever shipped, or with nothing because nothing was ever removed. Those three policies cost 0, N, and N² compatibility checks respectively, the same pairwise- agreement shape this course already found in team coordination. And a deprecated version doesn't vanish when you announce end-of-life — it decays on a curve, and halving the time it takes to reach 99% migrated needs the migration rate to more than double, not just double. GraphQL N+1 orphan 1 rig GraphQL's most infamous performance bug is the identical tree-fanout arithmetic this course already derived for authorization graph traversal, wearing a different name. A naive resolver issues one query per node in the response tree — the same f-ary sum as walking a group-membership graph — and the fix is the same fix: batch every request at one LEVEL into a single round trip, so total queries becomes a function of depth alone, never fanout. Two further findings ride on top: batching across concurrent requests, not just within one, is Little's Law; and sending a hash instead of a query body is a bandwidth trade with a protocol-defined, one-round-trip tax on a miss. gRPC and protobuf orphan 1 rig "Protobuf is 3–10× smaller than JSON" is a true sentence about a comparison nobody ships. Almost all of the win is field names going away, a compressor already removes repeated field names, and on a 50-row page the 3.13× you measure uncompressed is 1.18× on the wire. The parts of protobuf worth having are elsewhere: a field number that makes a rolling upgrade safe, and unknown fields that survive a hop — until a proxy re-serialises them away. Webhooks 1 rig A webhook sender retries on a schedule it controls, against a receiver it does not — the receiver's dedup window, its signature-verification code, and its own downtime are all opaque to the sender. This lesson computes what that opacity actually costs: how many retries can outlive a dedup window the sender can only guess at, how much cheaper a naive signature compare is to defeat than the signature's own length promises, and how deep a sender's own retry queue grows while it waits for a receiver that isn't answering. The real-time menu orphan 1 rig Polling, long polling, server-sent events, WebSocket. The feature tables compare them on bidirectionality, which decides nothing. The decision is one curve — compare the staleness you will accept against 1 ÷ (update rate + 1/hold) — and on one side of it there is no trade-off at all. Push does not remove the poll; it moves it and shrinks it. Proxies 1 rig A forward proxy and a reverse proxy are the same box shape pointed in opposite directions — one serves a client population talking to many destinations, the other serves many clients talking to one destination — and that direction, not the protocol, is the entire difference. This lesson computes what concentrating traffic at either one actually costs (CPU, connections, ports) and what it exposes if that one box is compromised. Service mesh 1 rig A service mesh's sidecar is nothing new — it's the forward-proxy/reverse-proxy box this course already costed, doing the mTLS this course already costed. What's actually new is what happens once there are N services instead of one proxy in front of one backend: an all-pairs topology to maintain, two extra hops that compound on every single call, and a control plane whose pushed configuration can be stale by a number this lesson computes exactly the way a DNS TTL already is.

TIER 2 Scaling the stateless tier 20/25

The easy half. Load balancing, health checks, autoscaling, and the traps in each.

Stateless discipline 1 rig Stateless doesn't mean "no state anywhere" — it means no state that only one process remembers: no session held only in an instance's memory, no local file a later request depends on, no routing rule that requires request N+1 to land back on the same box as request N. This lesson computes what breaking that discipline actually costs: how many sessions a single restart erases when they're pinned to memory, why scaling the fleet out doesn't change how many forced logouts happen a year — only their size — what moving that state to an external store costs on every request whether or not an instance ever fails, and why sticky routing quietly disables the load balancer sitting right underneath it. Load balancing orphan 2 rigs One backend in your fleet of eight gets four times slower. It does not crash, and its health check still passes. Round robin keeps feeding it and you start rejecting 9% of all traffic. Two of the five standard routing policies survive this untouched, and the cheaper of the two needs to look at exactly two servers. Health Checks 1 rig A shallow health check (does the process accept a connection?) and a deep one (does the real dependency work?) answer different questions, and of five catalogued failure modes a shallow check catches only one. That gap is the first half of this lesson. The second half is the trap a correct deep check sets: pulling the backends it correctly fails concentrates the same total load onto fewer survivors, and if that pushes them past their own queueing limit, they fail the identical check — not from the original bug, but from the fleet's own remediation. Connection draining 1 rig A backend told to shut down is still carrying whatever it was carrying a moment before — that population is fixed by Little's Law, not by how politely anyone asks it to leave. The load balancer needs its own time to notice and stop sending new work, which eats into the drain window before any draining happens at all. This lesson computes what's left once that lag is subtracted, what fraction of the in-flight population is still running when the process is finally killed, and what buying a higher completion rate actually costs every rolling deploy that ever takes a backend down. Thundering herd 1 rig Nobody sent more work than the service can handle. They just sent all of it in the same millisecond, and the requests that suffer most are the ones that had nothing to do with it. Sticky sessions 1 rig `stateless-discipline.mdx` already computes what a hash-pinned, Zipf-skewed session population does to a fleet. This lesson asks what's left once that skew is removed entirely: sessions handed out by a plain round robin, none of them any busier than another. Even then, the busiest instance runs measurably over the fleet's own mean, purely because sessions arrive and leave on independent clocks — and once an instance IS overloaded, sticky pinning has already taken away the load balancer's one real tool for it. This lesson computes both: how far pure arrival/departure timing pushes one instance above the pack, and how long even the fastest possible response takes to bring it back, compared with the zero seconds a stateless backend needs for the identical overload. Autoscaling 1 rig Reactive, predictive, and scheduled autoscaling are not three different mechanisms — they are one mechanism (detect load, then add capacity) with one shared, additive lag, and the three names describe how much of that lag a strategy avoids by acting ahead of a live metric instead of waiting for one. This lesson computes the lag itself, what the existing — undersized — fleet does to its own queue for the whole time that lag is running, and the backlog and drain time left behind once new capacity finally shows up. Warm-up & cold start 1 rig A health check tells you an instance is UP. It says nothing about whether the instance is WARM — whether its JIT has compiled the hot paths, its connection pools are populated, and its caches hold anything. This lesson puts a number on the gap: how much slower a fresh instance actually is, how long that lasts, and what handing it 100% of traffic immediately costs in aggregate queueing time compared to ramping it in. Serverless model 1 rig A provisioned instance costs the same per hour whether it serves one request or ten thousand. A serverless function costs (near) nothing at zero traffic and rises linearly with request rate — two cost curves, one flat and one rising, that cross exactly once. This lesson computes that crossing point, the "cost cliff" the roster names, and a second, unrelated ceiling every serverless platform enforces that a provisioned fleet never has to: a hard limit on concurrent executions, past which requests are throttled outright rather than merely queued. Containers vs VMs 1 rig A container and a VM both promise "your process, isolated from everyone else's." They deliver that promise through completely different mechanisms — one shares the host kernel, the other virtualizes a second one — and that mechanism difference is the entire reason one packs a host far denser and starts far faster than the other. This lesson computes the density a fixed memory budget actually buys under each, and prices the isolation the denser option gives up to get it. K8s reconciliation 1 rig A Kubernetes scheduler places a pod exactly once, against its REQUESTS — a reservation made now. A pod's LIMITS are a separate, later promise, and the scheduler never checks whether limits summed across a node even fit, which is why a node can be "overcommitted" and still be doing nothing wrong. This lesson computes the moment that stops being true: when actual usage, not requests and not limits, crosses physical capacity — and why the two resources fail in completely different ways when it does, and why the controller that fixes it runs as a loop with its own measurable delay rather than a callback that fires the instant something breaks. Deployment Strategies 1 rig Rolling, blue-green, canary, and shadow traffic are not four unrelated techniques — they are four schedules for the same variable: what share of a fleet, and of real traffic, is running a new version that might be bad. This lesson shows that detecting a bad version needs a fixed number of real exposures no matter which strategy is running — a one-sample statistical test that doesn't care about your deployment tooling — and that the strategies differ only in how fast wall-clock time gathers that number and what fraction of live users is exposed while it does. It computes that number, the wall-clock cost of reaching it four different ways, and the capacity bill blue-green and shadow traffic pay to change the trade. Feature flags 1 rig A feature flag's whole promise is that shipping code and exposing it become two separate, independently reversible decisions. This lesson computes what that promise actually costs. A percentage rollout is a hashing problem — get the key wrong (a coin flip instead of a stable user id) and the SAME user flickers between old and new on almost every request. Get the key right and a second problem appears anyway: the rollout percentage itself lives in a snapshot that refreshes on a timer, and two evaluations of one user's session — one at the start of a request path, one further along it — can read that snapshot at two different ages and disagree. Add flags that outlive their purpose and a flag service that can itself be slow or down, and a deploy primitive sold as pure upside turns out to have three measurable costs of its own. Multi-AZ vs multi-region 1 rig Multi-AZ and multi-region are sold as the same feature at a bigger radius: replicate outside one failure domain. They are not the same trade. An AZ boundary and a region boundary stop genuinely different failures, at genuinely different odds — and the distance between two AZs is small enough that synchronous replication barely costs anything, while the distance between two regions is large enough that the same synchronous replication costs a write's entire latency budget. This lesson computes both gaps: what each boundary actually protects against, and the exact point — much closer than either concrete region pair it uses — where "just replicate synchronously, but further" stops being an option at all. Active-active vs passive 1 rig Cold, warm, hot and active-active are usually taught as one ladder of "how much readiness do you want," as if climbing it is strictly good. It isn't a single trade — it's two independent ones, stacked. Climbing the ladder collapses recovery time (RTO), which this lesson computes stage by stage. It does NOT automatically reduce what a standby wastes: warm and hot standby pay close to a full site's bill for a site that serves nothing, and active-active only escapes that waste if its capacity can actually be used — writes partitioned by key, or conflicts resolved, across both sites. Get that part wrong and active-active isn't the free upgrade it looks like: it's a hot standby that costs the same and happens to answer some reads. (Replication lag and RPO are `multi-az-multi-region.mdx`'s model, reused unchanged here — this lesson's own contribution is RTO and the idle-capacity bill.) Cell-based architecture 1 rig Multi-AZ and multi-region replicate the same system outside a bigger failure domain. Cell-based architecture cuts a different way: it PARTITIONS one region into N independent, fully self-contained slices — each with its own compute, its own data store, its own queues — and routes every tenant to exactly one slice, forever. The payoff is a blast radius that is structurally 1/N instead of 100%, and this lesson computes exactly when that payoff shows up: for a plain cell failure, always; for a bad deploy, only if the rollout is staged cell-by-cell rather than pushed everywhere at once. It also computes the two things that arithmetic doesn't hand you for free — a router that fronts every cell and is therefore the one part of the design that isn't divided by N, and an operational bill (and a sizing risk) that grows right along with it. Shuffle sharding 1 rig Isolating tenants by giving each one a fully separate cell works, and it means running N times the infrastructure. Shuffle sharding gets most of the same isolation from ONE shared pool: instead of pinning a tenant to a fixed resource, give it a random, almost-unique COMBINATION of resources drawn from the pool. This lesson computes exactly how much isolation that buys — as a binomial coefficient, not a slogan — and why it gets better, not worse, as the pool grows. Multi-tenancy & noisy neighbours 1 rig Every tenant sharing a pool — CPU, disk I/O, a database, a queue — is exposed to every other tenant's usage, whether or not either one did anything wrong. `cell-based-architecture` and `shuffle-sharding` answer this by dividing the pool up so tenants stop sharing it. This lesson states the problem precisely and computes the cheaper answers that don't require dividing anything: a hard per-tenant quota, and fair-share scheduling — which turns out to buy the same protection as the quota, plus flexibility the quota structurally cannot offer. Gray Failure 1 rig A gray-failed node is invisible to any health check — shallow or deep — that exercises a cheaper, unaffected code path than the one real traffic actually takes. `health-checks.mdx` already showed a deep check misses whatever it never asks about; this lesson shows a check can ask exactly the right question and still miss the fault, because "deep" (calls the real dependency) is not the same claim as "as slow as the real request path." The only signal a gray-failed node cannot fake is what real requests actually experience — a slower, noisier signal than a binary probe, and this lesson computes exactly how much slower, how much noisier, and how much worse it is to have no such signal running at all. Edge isolates & WASM 1 rig An edge platform (Cloudflare Workers, Fastly Compute@Edge) runs a request handler behind neither a container nor a VM but a third, cheaper primitive: a V8 isolate or a WASM sandbox sharing not just the host's kernel but its own already-running process. This lesson extends `containers-vs-vms.mdx`'s density-and-startup ladder down one more rung, computes what that rung actually buys and what it costs, and separates the isolate's real advantage — startup, overwhelmingly, and density only at the small app sizes an edge function actually has — from what it gives up to get it: an OS-enforced boundary, traded for a runtime's own bookkeeping.

TIER 3 Storage and state 71/82

The hard half, and the biggest tier. Storage engines, indexes, isolation, replication, sharding, consistency.

B-tree vs LSM orphan 1 rig Every database picks one of two ways to keep data on disk, and the choice determines whether your workload is fast or hopeless. The usual summary — "B-trees for reads, LSM for writes" — is not wrong so much as missing the number that decides it, which is a property of your hardware and not of either data structure. Consistent hashing orphan 1 rig `hash(key) % N` is correct, obvious, and one line long. It also relocates almost your entire dataset the first time N changes — and the bigger your cluster, the worse that gets. The fix is a circle, and the circle has a price nobody mentions. Hot partitions orphan 1 rig Your shard map is perfectly balanced. One node is at 300% of a fair share and falling over. Both of those sentences are true, because a partitioner balances keys and your users request traffic — and a key is the smallest thing you are allowed to split. Quorums orphan 1 rig The rule everyone can recite is a constraint, not an answer. It guarantees exactly one thing — that your read set and write set share a node — and leaves latency, availability and fault tolerance completely undetermined. Four configurations satisfy it and behave nothing alike, and one of them is less available than no replication at all. Replication lag orphan 1 rig Lag is not a delay, it is a queue, so it obeys 1/(1−ρ) and not intuition. A replica with 5% headroom takes 200 seconds to clear a 10-second stall; with 1% headroom it takes 1,000. And the utilisation that decides this is not on the leader's dashboard — a sixteen-way leader saturates a single-threaded replica at 6% of its own capacity. Isolation anomalies orphan 1 rig The isolation levels you can name are not the levels you get. Locking REPEATABLE READ and snapshot isolation are incomparable — each prevents an anomaly the other permits — so "at least REPEATABLE READ" is not a safety requirement. And the anomaly snapshot isolation cannot see commits both transactions cleanly, raises no error, and moves no metric. Heap files & page layout 1 rig A table's rows live on fixed-size pages whether anyone thinks about it or not — a page header, a slot array, and packed row data. This lesson counts what naive division gets wrong about how many rows fit, and shows why the same range scan costs a sequential handful of pages on one physical layout and a random fetch per row on another, with nothing about the query or the index changing at all. Row vs column storage 1 rig A row-oriented table packs one row's columns together; a column-oriented table packs one column's rows together. That single layout choice decides how many bytes a query reads off disk — by a factor that swings from "23 times fewer" to "worse than the alternative" depending on how much of the row you actually need — and it is also the reason a column store can process data in tight, branch-free batches instead of one row at a time. Compression techniques 1 rig Dictionary encoding, run-length encoding and delta encoding are each a bet on one specific shape a column can have — how few distinct values it has, whether the same value recurs next to itself, and whether consecutive numbers are close together — and a general-purpose LZ/entropy codec is the shape-blind fallback that catches a little of each bet without ever winning any of them outright. Applied to the wrong shape, a specialized technique doesn't just fail to help: dictionary encoding on a near-unique column and RLE on a column with no runs both make the column bigger than storing it raw. This lesson computes exactly when each one wins, by how much, and what it costs to guess wrong. WAL & fsync 1 rig Every durable system keeps a write-ahead log for one mechanical reason: appending sequentially is vastly cheaper than the random, whole-page rewrite a B-tree needs for the same change, and replaying the log after a crash reconstructs whatever was lost. But the log only helps if it is actually on the device — and calling `write()` does not put it there. This lesson computes the gap between the two, what group commit trades to close it cheaply, and the honest data-loss window a system accepts depending on exactly when it tells a client "committed." Shared-nothing vs shared-disk 1 rig A shared-nothing node bundles compute and local disk into one purchase, so scaling one always drags the other along, wanted or not. A shared-disk architecture — Aurora, Snowflake, Neon, ClickHouse-on-S3, Kafka's tiered storage — unbundles them into two independently billed, independently scaled layers, and pays for that split with a network round trip on every access the local-disk version never had to make. This lesson prices both halves of that trade: the stranded capacity a bundled node forces you to buy, and the cache hit rate a network-backed storage layer needs to stop that round trip from being the whole story. Object storage durability 1 rig Object storage's API has no call to seek into a live object and write just the changed bytes — PUT replaces the whole object, by key, every time. That single missing call is why every system built on top of an object store ends up append-only. This lesson prices what the missing call costs as an object grows, and shows the one primitive an object store DOES offer in place of a lock — the conditional write — actually preventing a lost update that a naive read-modify-write race would silently suffer. Replica placement 1 rig Replication factor N says how many copies of your data exist. It says nothing about WHERE those copies sit — and where turns out to dominate durability as a cluster grows. Naive random placement draws every item's copyset from a pool of combinations that grows combinatorially with cluster size, so a correlated failure of just a few nodes gets more and more distinct ways to land squarely on one item's entire copy set — even though the replication factor and the FRACTION of the cluster failing never change. This lesson computes that rise, computes the bounded-copyset scheme that stops it, and separately computes the simpler question of whether one item's own copies share a single rack or AZ at all. Erasure coding 1 rig Split the data into K pieces, compute M extra parity pieces, and any K of the K+M survive-together. That single trick is why cold storage at Facebook, Azure, and every major object store runs erasure-coded instead of triple-replicated — the storage bill drops by multiples. The bill nobody puts next to that saving is what happens when a disk actually dies: the same K that shrank the storage cost is exactly what it now costs, in nodes touched and bytes moved, to get one fragment back. Checksums & integrity 1 rig A disk's own checksum only proves the disk read back what it itself wrote. TCP's own checksum only proves the packet wasn't damaged in a way its 16-bit sum happens to catch — and that sum was chosen in the 1980s for cheap arithmetic, not strong detection. Neither layer can vouch for corruption introduced by the bus, the RAM, the controller, or a bit that flips after every check already ran. This lesson computes what closes that gap — an end-to-end checksum, verified by the application itself — and quantifies the two failure modes that motivate it: how a checksum can be blind to a whole class of real corruption, and how a scrub schedule is a race against silent, independent rot that shrinking the interval wins by more than the interval shrank. What is an index 1 rig Every later lesson in this subsection — clustered vs. secondary, composite and covering, selectivity, EXPLAIN plans — assumes one thing is already obvious: what an index actually IS, mechanically, and why having one changes a lookup's cost class at all. This lesson answers that, with nothing left implicit: an index is a second, separate structure, built from just the key plus a pointer back to the row, kept in sync with every write to the table it serves — and that is exactly why it is not free. Clustered vs secondary 1 rig A table has exactly one physical row order, so exactly one index can match it — the clustered index, whose leaf pages ARE the rows. Every other index on that table is secondary: sorted on its own key, but pointing back at a row it does not physically sit next to. This lesson prices what that pointer actually costs — a point lookup that doubles from one tree traversal to two, and a range scan that can collapse from a sequential read into one random fetch per matching row, past a crossover this lesson computes rather than guesses at. Composite & covering indexes 1 rig A composite index over `(a, b, c)` is one physical structure, sorted by `a`, then by `b` within each `a`, then by `c` within each `(a, b)` — a phone book sorted by (last name, first name), not three indexes glued together. Which queries that single sort order can serve efficiently depends entirely on the ORDER the columns were declared in, not on which columns happen to appear in the query. This lesson computes exactly which of several realistic query shapes a given column order serves cheaply, which it serves at a partial cost, and which it cannot serve at all — plus the separate question of a covering index, which stores enough extra columns to skip the base-table hop `clustered-vs-secondary-indexes` already priced. Index write cost 1 rig `what-is-an-index.mdx` stated the basic rule and stopped there on purpose: N indexes roughly multiply a write by (1 + N). This lesson is the part it deferred — the honest mechanics that make the real number worse than that flat multiplier in one direction and better than it in another, depending on which column actually changed, and depending on whether each index's own write lands as a cheap sequential append or an expensive random one. Partial & functional indexes 1 rig Every index so far in this subsection has covered the whole column, for every row, exactly as written. This lesson is about two refinements that make an index cheaper by covering LESS — deliberately, precisely — rather than more: a partial index that only indexes the rows a WHERE clause names, and a functional index that indexes the result of an expression instead of the raw column. Both buy a smaller, cheaper-to-write index. Both also come with a sharp edge: stray one predicate or one expression off the index's own definition and the database gives that query nothing at all, not partial credit. Selectivity & cardinality 1 rig "The table has an index" and "the query will use it" are not the same fact — a query planner compares two real costs and takes the cheaper one, and past a small, computable share of the table matching, a plain sequential scan wins every time. This lesson names the property that decides it (selectivity: how much of the table a condition matches; cardinality: how many distinct values a column has) and reuses `clustered-vs-secondary-indexes.mjs`'s own crossover to find exactly where that flip happens — not "most rows," a single-digit percent. Reading EXPLAIN plans 1 rig `index-selectivity` computed the crossover a cost-based planner uses to choose between an index scan and a full scan — and that math is never wrong. What it quietly assumed is that the SELECTIVITY fed into it is current. A real optimizer doesn't measure selectivity per query; it reads whatever its last `ANALYZE` recorded, on a fixed schedule, while the table itself can change the instant a bulk update runs. This lesson prices the gap between those two clocks — how long a correct decision keeps running after it stops being correct, and the three-number readout (`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`'s access method, estimated rows, actual rows) that is how you catch it happening. Inverted index 1 rig `what-is-an-index.mdx` already showed what a sorted or hashed structure buys a structured column: a lookup that costs 3 page reads instead of 1,000,000 row-reads. Full-text search needs the same trade against unstructured text, and the structure that buys it has its own name and its own mechanics: the inverted index — the term→documents mapping that flips a document collection's natural document→terms shape, so a search for a word becomes one posting-list lookup instead of a scan of every word of every document. This lesson builds that flip, the merge-walk that answers a multi-term query against it, and the compression a sorted posting list gets almost for free. Bloom filter 1 rig `btree-vs-lsm.mdx` already charges an LSM point lookup `1 + fpr × (runs − 1)` and moves on, treating the false-positive rate as a given constant. This lesson is where that constant stops being asserted and starts being derived — from a bit array of size M and K independent hash functions — and where the one-sided guarantee that makes it safe to use at all (it can never, ever, produce a false negative) gets its own proof and its own number. Skip lists, tries, sparse index 1 rig A B-tree's height is a formula: plug in the row count and the page size, get one deterministic number, every time. Three other structures give that up on purpose, each trading the guarantee away for something else — a skip list trades it for no rebalancing logic at all, a trie trades per-key memory for a lookup cost that never depends on how many keys exist, and an LSM's sparse index trades index completeness for a footprint 1/K the size. This lesson puts a number on each trade, reusing `btree-vs-lsm`'s own cost model rather than re-deriving the B-tree side of it. Dense vs sparse indexes 1 rig `skip-lists-tries-sparse-index.mdx` showed one instance of this: an LSM SSTable's in-memory index holding only every Kth key. This lesson pulls the same property up to where it actually lives — dense versus sparse is a choice available to ANY index over ANY sorted data, B-tree or SSTable alike, and it is only available at all when the underlying data is sorted by the indexed key. This lesson prices the general trade-off: how index storage shrinks with the sparseness factor K, how the resulting local scan grows, and the real sweet spot in between — plus why an unsorted heap table can never use it, stuck at K=1 forever. Geospatial indexes 1 rig Every index this course has built so far assumes a key has one natural sort order: numbers sort by magnitude, strings sort alphabetically, and "nearby" in the sorted order means "nearby" in the data. A pair of coordinates breaks that assumption outright — sorting by latitude alone scatters points that are metres apart in longitude, and vice versa. This lesson surveys the four families that answer "what's nearby" anyway: geohash fakes a 1D key out of 2D space (and pays for it at cell boundaries), quadtree and R-tree give up on a fixed key order entirely, and S2/H3 fix the specific distortion a naive lat/lng grid has near the poles. Join algorithms 1 rig A query planner never has "the" way to compute `A JOIN B` — it has three structurally different algorithms, each with its own cost shape, and it picks whichever one is cheapest for the specific tables in front of it. Nested-loop pays per outer row times an inner cost that a good index can crush to a handful of page reads. Hash join pays once per row on each side, as long as the smaller side's hash table fits in memory. Sort-merge pays to sort whatever isn't already sorted, then walks both sides once. This lesson computes all three costs for the same pair of tables and shows exactly where each one wins — not by rule of thumb, but by the crossover a real cost-based planner is doing the arithmetic for. Scatter-gather 1 rig Any query without the shard key in its WHERE clause can't be answered by one shard — it has to be scattered to every shard in parallel and gathered back by a coordinator. The query's latency is set by the slowest of those N parallel replies, not their average, and the same tail-amplification arithmetic this course has already used for generic fan-out applies here without a single new assumption: more shards touched means a near-certain straggler, even when every shard is almost always fast. Materialized views 1 rig A materialized view precomputes and stores a query's result so a read is a lookup instead of a re-run. The read speedup is never free — someone has to keep the stored result in sync with the base table, and how that maintenance is done changes its cost by two orders of magnitude. This lesson prices full refresh against incremental maintenance, shows exactly which aggregate shapes earn a cheap incremental update and which don't, and puts a number on the staleness a fixed refresh cadence leaves a reader exposed to. Vertical partitioning 1 rig A wide row packs a table's frequently-queried columns and its rarely-touched or oversized ones together, and a row store's unit of I/O is the whole row — so a query that only needs the small hot columns still pays for every byte of the large cold one, on every page it touches. This lesson quantifies that waste, splits the cold column into its own table to fix it, and shows Postgres's TOAST mechanism doing the same separation automatically, per oversized value, with no schema change at all. Lock taxonomy & granularity 1 rig A database enforces "don't let two transactions touch the same data unsafely" at row, page, or table granularity — the same protection, three different price tags. This lesson computes those prices directly: the lock-object overhead and unrelated-row blocking at each granularity for one fixed workload, why a granularity hierarchy needs intent locks to stay cheap to check, why a plain row lock cannot stop a phantom insert into an empty gap, and the exact row-lock count at which an engine gives up on fine-grained locking and blocks a table's worth of transactions at once. Normalization & normal forms 1 rig Normalization is not rule-following for its own sake — it is a disciplined way of arranging a schema so a fact is stored exactly once, which is what makes update, insert, and delete anomalies structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely. This lesson prices those anomalies, walks 1NF through BCNF against one concrete schema apiece, and then reverses course honestly: denormalization is the same trade-off, made on purpose once its cost is actually understood. Schema-on-read vs schema-on-write 1 rig A relational table checks every field against its schema before a write is accepted; a document store or event log accepts whatever arrives and leaves the checking to whoever reads it later. Both are real engineering positions, not one correct answer and one mistake — this lesson prices what each one actually costs: a per-field validation tax paid on every write, a detection delay that is exactly 0 on one side and can run to years on the other, and a parsing tax every reader pays instead of the writer paying it once. It also separates a distinct, often-conflated idea: data locality, which is about where related data physically sits, not when its shape gets checked. Dimensional modelling 1 rig Normalization asks how to arrange a schema so a transaction never corrupts. Dimensional modelling asks a different question of a different workload: how to arrange a schema so an analyst's aggregate query — revenue by category, by quarter, by region — stays fast across millions of rows. This lesson prices the three answers side by side: a star schema (one join hop to any attribute), a snowflake schema (normalize the dimensions too, for less storage and more hops), and one big table (flatten everything, for zero hops and the most storage). Graph query languages 1 rig A relational join can express any FIXED number of hops — "friends of friends" is a 2-way self-join, "friends of friends of friends" is a 3-way one. What it cannot express is "however many hops it takes": a variable-depth traversal has no fixed arity, so no fixed number of JOIN clauses can write it. SQL's late answer is `WITH RECURSIVE`, which runs the same join once per hop and, naively, has to re-check every new row against everything already visited — a check that gets more expensive every additional hop. A purpose-built graph engine skips all of that: every node already stores direct pointers to its neighbors, so following an edge is one O(1) pointer read, full stop. This lesson computes both costs as depth grows and shows exactly why the gap between them widens rather than staying flat. Unique ID generation 1 rig Generating a value that has never been used before sounds like it should be free — call a function, get a number. But that number becomes a primary key, and a primary key's VALUE decides where every insert lands in a B-tree, not just whether it's unique. This lesson prices four real ID schemes — UUIDv4, UUIDv7, ULID, Snowflake — on the two axes that actually differ: how many bytes each one costs to store, and what its value layout does to insert locality, reusing the page-split model this course already built rather than re-deriving it. ACID vs BASE 1 rig ACID is usually taught as four letters to memorize. This lesson prices three of them — atomicity's WAL, isolation's locks-or-retries, durability's fsync — as one per-commit latency number, and is honest about the fourth: consistency isn't a mechanism with a cost at all, and it is not the same word CAP uses, which is the single most common confusion this topic produces. BASE is the other side of the same arithmetic: a distributed system that declines to pay this tax, on purpose. MVCC 1 rig Snapshot isolation says a transaction sees the database as it stood at one fixed moment. This lesson is about the machine that makes that true without a reader ever waiting on a writer: every write creates a new row version instead of overwriting the old one, and a reader picks the version its own snapshot can see. The mechanism is nearly free per read — and it has a bill, paid in versions that pile up for as long as the oldest open snapshot in the system stays open. 2PL, SSI, deterministic execution 1 rig Serializable execution has three real, shipping mechanisms, not one — a database can enforce "runs as if transactions ran one at a time" by making transactions wait (two-phase locking), by letting them run free and aborting one at commit time if it turns out unsafe (serializable snapshot isolation), or by refusing to let it be ambiguous in the first place (deciding the execution order before anything runs). This lesson prices all three the same way — cost per transaction against one shared variable, how often transactions overlap — and finds the contention level where the optimistic bet stops paying off. Deadlocks 1 rig Two transactions each hold a lock the other needs, and each is waiting for the other to release — neither can proceed, and without intervention neither ever will. This lesson computes the three practical answers: detecting the cycle in an actual wait-for graph and aborting a victim, preventing it structurally with a consistent lock order, and what the application owes once a victim is aborted. `lock-taxonomy-and-granularity.mdx` covers what a lock IS; this is what happens once two of them collide. Optimistic vs pessimistic 1 rig Two philosophies answer "what do I do about another transaction that might touch the row I'm about to change" — take a lock first and make it wait, or don't take a lock at all and check for damage when you're done. This lesson is the application/query-level version of that decision: `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` and `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` as pessimistic concurrency's actual SQL, `WHERE id = ? AND version = ?` as optimistic concurrency's actual SQL, and the numbers that say which one to reach for. Long transactions & bloat 1 rig `mvcc.mdx` proved that the reclamation horizon — the age of the oldest open snapshot in the system — sets a floor on dead-version count with no vacuum term in the formula. This lesson turns that floor into bytes: how many pages a table and its indexes actually occupy once a transaction has been held open, the exact write rate past which VACUUM falls permanently behind no matter how long that transaction stays open, and why letting the fixed-width transaction-id counter wrap is not a performance problem but a data-corruption one. Leader–follower replication 1 rig A leader can acknowledge a write the instant it hits its own disk, the instant the fastest follower confirms it, or only once every follower has — and those three choices are one dial, not three features. Multi-leader replication 1 rig A single leader has exactly one place a write can land, so two writes to the same key can never disagree about which one happened first — there is only ever one leader to ask. Chain replication 1 rig A third replication topology, next to the leader-follower star and the leaderless quorum: nodes form a straight line, writes enter at the head and walk it node by node, and only the tail's reads are ever served — which buys strong consistency without a single vote. Why you shard 1 rig Replication already solved reads — more copies of the whole dataset, spread across machines that each hold everything. Rendezvous hashing 1 rig Consistent hashing earns its minimal-disruption property with a ring: sorted points, virtual nodes, a binary search. Rebalancing cost 1 rig Consistent hashing, range partitioning and rendezvous hashing all answer the same question — which partitions move when a node joins, leaves or the assignment scheme changes. This lesson prices the two genuine ways to handle that — an index local to each partition, which makes every read a scatter-gather, or an index partitioned by its own indexed value, which makes every cross-partition write pay for a distributed transaction or accept staleness — and computes the exact read:write ratio where one stops beating the other. Federation is not a third name for the same thing. This lesson untangles the three words with a real decision procedure, then quantifies the practical reason federation and sharding are usually combined rather than compared. CAP & PACELC 1 rig Almost every explanation of CAP treats consistency, availability and partition tolerance as three properties a system picks two of, forever. That is not what the theorem says, and it is not how any real system works — a partition is not a design choice, it is a network failure that happens whether anyone wants it to or not. This lesson is the map connecting them: the full lattice of system-wide consistency models, strongest to weakest — linearizable, sequential, causal, eventual — with each one's real coordination cost computed from three already-built lessons, and the one genuinely load-bearing result: causal consistency is not an arbitrary stop on the way down, it is the provably STRONGEST model that never has to block during a partition. The outbox pattern 1 rig A service writes a row to its own database, then separately publishes a message about it. There is no transaction spanning the two, so a crash between them either loses the message or invents one. At this lesson's checkout-scale operating point that is about 43,240 events lost or invented a day — not a rare accident, a computable rate. The outbox pattern makes both writes land in the same local transaction instead, which drops the loss to exactly zero — and charges for it in publish latency instead: about half a second, mean, at a 1-second poll. CRDTs and operational transform 1 rig `multi-leader-replication.mdx` priced last-write-wins (a coin flip that silently deletes a write) and named application-level merge (domain-specific code, e.g. union two carts) as the better alternative — then pointed forward to CRDTs as "the general-purpose, principled version of exactly that idea." This lesson delivers on that pointer, and puts the other production answer next to it: operational transform, the mechanism behind Google Docs. Both automate merge without a lock and without one write overwriting the other. Neither is free — this lesson computes what each one actually costs. Three-phase commit 1 rig Two-phase commit has exactly one serious flaw: if the coordinator dies after some participants have voted yes but before it sends the final decision, those participants are stuck — locked, waiting, unable to safely guess. Three-phase commit was built to fix precisely that, by adding a phase whose whole point is letting participants finish the job themselves. It works — under an assumption the protocol needs and cannot enforce: that the network stays synchronous enough for a timeout to mean "dead" rather than "partitioned." Under a real partition, the fix can make DIFFERENT groups of participants decide differently on the same transaction, which two-phase commit's design cannot even do. Timeliness vs integrity 1 rig `cap-and-pacelc.mdx` and `consistency-models-lattice.mdx` both use "consistency" the way CAP does — an ordering property — and both flag, without developing it, that the word is doing at least two different jobs. This lesson is that untangling: timeliness (when you see a value, relative to when it was written) and integrity (whether the data is ever corrupted or made nonsensical) are two independent axes, not one dial, and most of what people fear about "eventual consistency" is a timeliness worry wearing an integrity costume. OLTP vs OLAP 1 rig OLTP and OLAP are not two kinds of database — they are two workload shapes, and row-vs-column-storage.mdx's own crossover formula already says which layout wins for either one. This lesson runs that formula on a mix instead of a single query, then asks the honest question the roster demands: does HTAP actually escape the trade-off, or does it just move where you pay for it, and does zero-ETL fix the underlying mismatch or only automate the plumbing between two systems that stay separate. This lesson computes exactly how much that costs, what a rolling hash buys back by cutting on content instead of position, and the real CPU and metadata price of that fix. This lesson prices each one: the backfill's duration as a direct function of batch size, the batch-size lever's other side (a bigger batch finishes sooner and pins more dead row versions per batch while it runs), what a shadow-read verification step buys against a silent backfill bug before it reaches production, and what "drop the old column before every reader migrated" actually costs when a straggler is still reading it. Backward vs forward compatibility 1 rig Backward compatibility means new code can read old data; forward compatibility means old code can read new data — two independent properties, not two names for the same caution. Both matter at once because of one fact neither direction alone explains: data outlives code. A live database holds records from every schema version it has ever written, in shrinking-but-nonzero proportion, while the code reading it is at most one or two versions at a time. Binary schema encodings 1 rig Protobuf, Thrift, Avro and MessagePack are all "binary and schema-based," and that sameness hides three genuinely different mechanisms for the one property they all chase: compact on the wire, and survivable across a schema change. Protobuf and Thrift number every field and never repeat its name. Avro deletes the field markers entirely and makes the reader fetch the writer's exact schema to know how to parse the bytes at all. This lesson builds an actual byte-size model of the same record under all three mechanisms plus JSON as the non-binary baseline, and reuses this course's own backward/forward-compatibility classifier against Avro's very different resolution rule. SQL vs NoSQL 1 rig "SQL vs NoSQL" is usually argued as a tribe you join, with "scalability" doing the work of a reason. It isn't one axis and there is no winner — there are eight storage families, each built to make one SHAPE of access pattern cheap at the expense of every other shape. This lesson builds the actual decision framework: a scoring model that takes a workload's join complexity, consistency need, write pattern, query flexibility, and schema variability, and computes which family's mechanical cost is lowest — reusing this course's own join-cost and traversal-cost math to show the number behind "relational" or "graph" instead of asserting it. Query-first data modelling 1 rig A relational schema is designed once, normalized, and queried however you like later — a JOIN is always available. Cassandra and DynamoDB remove that option: there is no ad-hoc join, and no efficient WHERE on a non-key attribute. So the design process inverts. You enumerate every access pattern FIRST, then build one denormalized table per pattern, keyed so each one is a single-partition lookup. This lesson prices what that buys on reads, what it costs on writes, and reuses `hot-partitions`' own math to show the one thing this discipline does not exempt you from: choosing a partition key with enough cardinality. Blob storage vs database 1 rig A large or rarely-queried binary payload — an image, a video, a PDF, a big JSON blob, model weights — can live two places: inline in the database row next to the record that owns it, or in cheap object storage with only a pointer left behind. Both are "correct." This lesson prices the difference: what every replica and backup copying that payload for free actually costs per month, what an inline blob does to a scan that never even reads it, and the byte-size threshold past which externalizing stops paying for a fixed extra round trip and starts paying for itself.

Cache coherence across replicas 1 rig `cache-invalidation.mdx` asked how ONE cache learns its data went stale. This lesson asks the question that opens up the moment there is more than one cache — a Redis cluster's read replicas, a CDN's edge PoPs, N app-server-local caches — all serving the SAME key at the SAME moment. `consistency-models-lattice.mdx` already built the vocabulary for "how much do replicas of anything agree, and what does agreement cost": this lesson applies it, unchanged, to a cache tier specifically, and prices the two strategies real systems actually run. CDNs orphan 1 rig A CDN cannot make a packet travel faster — the floor is distance over the speed of light in glass. What it removes is round trips, and a cold HTTPS request is four of them. Then the bill arrives: the same request stream divided over more locations gives each location a thinner slice, and thin slices do not cache. There is an interior optimum, and it moves with your traffic. Cache writing strategies 1 rig Cache-aside, write-through, write-behind, refresh-ahead, write-around, read-through — six names, one axis. Every one of them is an answer to a single question: when a write happens, does the cache get touched before, after, instead of, or independent of the database — and what does that ordering cost in latency, data loss, and staleness. Multi-tier caching 1 rig Client, CDN, gateway, app and DB are not one cache — they are five, stacked, each with its own TTL and its own audience. This lesson does not re-explain any one of them; it computes what happens when a request passes through all five at once: the staleness a user sees is set by the SLOWEST layer to invalidate, not the fastest, and the load reaching the database is the PRODUCT of every layer's miss rate, not any single layer's. Caching granularity 1 rig A row, a query's output, a hydrated object, or a rendered page fragment are all "a cache entry" — but they disagree on how much compute a hit saves and, far more consequentially, on how many independent writes anywhere in the system are allowed to make that entry wrong. The coarser the unit, the bigger the payoff per hit and the bigger the blast radius per write. Queue vs. log 1 rig Both look like "send a message, something else picks it up." A queue's job is to deliver each message to one consumer and then erase it; a log's job is to append a message and keep it for a configurable window while any number of consumers read it at their own pace, tracking their own offset. That single difference in what the broker is obliged to hold decides three things this lesson computes exactly: how much storage each model needs at identical throughput (orders of magnitude apart), how expensive it is to let N independent systems see the same event stream (linear for a queue, flat for a log), and whether a crashed consumer can ever be rebuilt by replay (never for a queue, bounded by retention for a log). Schema registry 1 rig Avro's writer/reader-schema resolution needs the reader to hold the writer's EXACT schema to parse a message at all — `binary-schema-encodings.mdx` names the two ways to supply it: attach it to every message, or fetch it by ID from a registry. This lesson is the registry case, worked through with real numbers: how much smaller a message gets when it carries a 5-byte ID instead of a full schema definition, how many of a real stream of schema changes a registry with compatibility enforcement turned on actually rejects before they reach a consumer, and exactly how far a registry outage's blast radius reaches — bounded by cache TTL for schemas already known, unbounded for anything brand new. Segments, offsets & compaction 1 rig A log's defining property — append-only, read-by-position — says nothing about how it is stored, and "one growing file" is not the answer any real system uses. Kafka splits a partition into bounded-size segment files, each covering a contiguous range of monotonically increasing offsets; a consumer's "position" is simply the last offset it committed. This lesson computes three consequences of that segmented structure: how often a segment rolls over and how many stay live under a retention window, why deleting by segment age can never reclaim space for one expired key — which is the actual reason log compaction exists as a second, different mechanism — and why finding "everything after offset X" is a cheap lookup with a segment index instead of a scan of the whole log. Consumer groups & rebalancing 1 rig A consumer group is what lets several consumers split a partitioned log's work while each partition still gets exactly one reader at a time — the mechanism `queue-vs-log.mdx` already named but never opened. Opening it means asking what happens the moment that assignment has to change: a consumer joins, one crashes, or a heartbeat times out, and the group runs a REBALANCE. The classic protocol stops the entire group — every consumer relinquishes every partition and waits for the slowest member to rejoin before anyone resumes — which means a single flaky consumer can cost the whole group far more than its own share of the work. This lesson computes that stop-the-world cost, the storm that repeated rebalances produce once a group is unlucky (or slow) often enough, and how much of both problem incremental rebalancing actually removes. Competing consumers, work stealing 1 rig `consumer-groups-and-rebalancing.mdx` covered what happens when a partition's OWNER changes. This lesson asks a different question about the same competing-consumers pattern: once ownership is fixed, how evenly does the actual work drain? A Kafka-style consumer group caps load-balancing granularity at the partition — a partition's messages are married to whichever consumer owns it, so a slow partition stays slow even while siblings sit idle. Work stealing is the alternative mechanism traditional task queues and thread-pool schedulers use instead: any idle worker pulls the next available unit of work, full stop, no partition to be married to. This lesson computes exactly what that buys, and exactly what it costs — the same ordering-vs-throughput trade `message-ordering-per-key.mdx` already priced, with both ends of it real numbers apart this time. Pub/sub vs. point-to-point 1 rig `queue-vs-log.mdx` asked what a broker is obliged to KEEP — delete-on-ack or append-and-retain. This lesson asks an orthogonal question: for a given message, how many consumers actually RECEIVE it? Point-to-point delivers each message to exactly one consumer of a competing set, so total delivery work never grows no matter how many consumers are added — they only split who does it. Pub/sub delivers each message to every subscriber independently, so N subscribers means N full, separate deliveries of the same bytes. This lesson computes that delivery-cost gap exactly, classifies which topology a scenario actually needs with a real scoring function rather than an assertion, and prices the hybrid model most real systems actually run — Kafka's consumer-group topic — where cost scales with the number of independent groups and not at all with how many members split the work inside one. Broker vs. brokerless 1 rig `queue-vs-log.mdx` and `pub-sub-vs-point-to-point.mdx` both assume an intermediary already exists and ask what it keeps and how many consumers it reaches. This lesson asks the question underneath both: does an intermediary exist at all? A broker (Kafka, RabbitMQ, SQS) is a separate always-on service that adds one network hop to every message and durably holds anything a slow consumer hasn't picked up yet. Brokerless messaging (ZeroMQ, nanomsg, a direct socket or RPC call) skips that hop entirely — lower latency, nothing extra to run — but has nowhere to put a message if the consumer is unreachable: it either drops the message or blocks the producer until the consumer returns. This lesson prices both outcomes in the same unit and computes the exact consumer-availability level at which brokerless's expected message-loss cost overtakes the broker's fixed, permanent hop tax. DLQs and retry topics 1 rig Retry timing — how long to wait, how many hops, how a shared budget bounds amplification — is already covered elsewhere in this course. This lesson is the queue-specific decision underneath all of that: when a message keeps failing, where does it GO? Retry it in place and a message that can never succeed blocks every message behind it, forever. Move it to a retry topic and the queue keeps moving while a genuinely transient failure gets a second chance later. Exhaust a bounded ladder of those chances and move it to a dead letter queue — the one policy that admits infinite retry is not a strategy for a message that is permanently broken. Consumer lag death spiral 1 rig `queue-vs-log.mdx` established that a consumer tracks its own offset into a retained log, and `append-only-log-mechanics.mdx` established that retention deletes whole segments once they age out regardless of whether anyone has read them. This lesson puts those two facts together: what happens when a consumer's offset falls behind the log's head, and keeps falling behind. It computes three things — the growth rate of that gap (lag), why the growth is often self-reinforcing rather than self-correcting, and the two hard ceilings that follow: a retention window past which the gap becomes permanent data loss, and a partition count past which adding consumers stops helping at all. Backpressure through a pipeline 1 rig `backpressure.mdx` covers one queue, one worker, one signal: refuse or hold. Almost nothing in production is one stage. A real pipeline is a chain — ingest, parse, enrich, write — and when the last stage slows down, the question isn't "does it push back," it's "how far back, and how fast." Each stage's buffer is a shock absorber: enough slack and a short slowdown never leaves the last two stations. Not enough, or a slowdown that doesn't end, and it walks the whole chain back to the front door. Priority and delay queues 1 rig Two ways a broker changes WHEN a message becomes available, as opposed to `queue-vs-log.mdx`'s question of how long it's kept. A priority queue reorders by importance — and if high-priority traffic never lets up, a low-priority message has no guaranteed bound on how long it waits, unless an aging mechanism eventually promotes it anyway. A delay queue holds a message invisible until a future time — for scheduled jobs, retry backoff, or debouncing — and if the visibility timeout is shorter than processing actually takes, the same message gets delivered twice. Fan-out: write-time vs read-time 1 rig `write-path-read-path.mdx` already proved that any derived value is either produced eagerly at write time or lazily at read time, that cost is linear in the split, and that the optimum is therefore always a corner — with "fan out a feed on write" and "fan out ONE post to 50M followers" among its own six worked instances. This lesson takes that formula and turns it into a policy: given a whole population of accounts spanning ordinary users to a celebrity, what is the single follower-count threshold a real system actually ships — push below it, pull above it — and how close does that one number get to deciding every account separately? Then it adds the mechanism the amortized-cost formula cannot see at all: a single post pushed to F followers is F jobs landing on the fan-out pipeline in one burst, and that burst has to drain inside an acceptable window regardless of what the average cost says. Event sourcing 1 rig Every data model discussed so far answers "what is true right now" by overwriting a row. Event sourcing answers a different question: instead of storing `order.status = shipped`, store the sequence of events that produced it — `OrderPlaced`, `OrderPaid`, `OrderShipped` — and derive "shipped" by replaying them. That's `queue-vs-log.mdx`'s "a log keeps what it appends" idea applied to the domain model itself, not just the transport between services. This lesson computes what that costs (storage that grows with every state change, forever, against a mutable row that never grows), what buys the cost back down (a periodic snapshot bounds replay to events since it, with a real crossover point where the snapshot pays for itself), and what the design gets for free that a mutable store has to bolt on separately (the entire history, usable for audit or time-travel, at no cost beyond what's already paid). CQRS 1 rig `event-sourcing.mdx` is about HOW the write side stores state — a log of events instead of a mutable row. This lesson is about something orthogonal: WHETHER the read side is served off that same store at all. CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) splits the write path (a command model that enforces invariants and is the single source of truth) from the read path (one or more query models — denormalized, precomputed views, each shaped for one access pattern). The two ideas compose — event-sourced writes projected into CQRS read views — but neither requires the other. This lesson computes CQRS's two real costs (the read model is usually built asynchronously, so it's eventually consistent, not immediately; and maintaining several read models multiplies write-side work) against the one thing that buys both back: an O(1) precomputed lookup instead of an expensive join, which is worth paying for once a workload is read-heavy enough. CDC and log tailing 1 rig `the-outbox-pattern.mdx` mentioned CDC once, as one implementation choice for its own publisher — a poller or a log-tailer, reading one table. This lesson is the general technique itself: a background process turns a mutable database's writes into a stream of events for everything downstream, by either tailing the database's own write-ahead log (log-based CDC, what Debezium does) or periodically re-querying the table (polling CDC). The two are not interchangeable. At this lesson's default, a row that changes 6 times between two polls produces 6 events for a log-tailer and exactly 1 for a poller — 5 states gone for good — and a plain `DELETE` with no tombstone column produces 0 events for a poller at all, silently. What log-based CDC buys back costs its own price: a tailer that cannot keep up with the write rate falls behind until it needs a full re-snapshot, and until then its own lag pins write-ahead-log bytes on the source database's disk that a caught-up tailer would have let go. Windows and watermarks 1 rig Every stream aggregate — a per-minute count, a rolling average — first needs the unbounded stream cut into finite chunks. Tumbling, sliding, and session windows are three different answers to "which events go together," but none of them answer the harder question underneath: when is it safe to finalize a window and emit its result? That answer depends on a fact stream processing can never fully verify — whether every event for that window has actually arrived — because a stream processor only ever sees events in PROCESSING time (when it happened to observe them), while windows are defined in EVENT time (when they actually happened), and those two clocks diverge under network delay, retries, and backpressure. A watermark is the processor's running guess at "I've seen everything up to T," and this lesson computes what tightening or loosening that guess costs: faster results against more silently dropped late data, or fewer dropped events against a longer wait for every result. Stateful operators 1 rig Some stream operators only ever need the event in front of them — a filter, a map — and a crash costs them nothing, because there was nothing to remember. Others need to remember something across many events: a running count, a set of keys already seen, a buffer of rows waiting for a join partner. Those operators lose that memory the instant their worker dies, unless it was written somewhere durable first. Checkpointing is that write — and once it exists, two numbers you control (how often you checkpoint, how big the state has grown) decide whether recovery costs seconds or an hour, and whether checkpointing itself becomes the thing slowing the pipeline down. Checkpoint + replay + sink 1 rig `stateful-operators-and-checkpointing.mdx` prices what a checkpoint recovers: operator state consistent as of the last checkpoint, bounded by an interval you control. `exactly-once-and- delivery-semantics.mdx` proves true exactly-once delivery is impossible and prices what idempotent processing buys against a wire-level duplicate. Neither one, alone, is exactly-once stream processing — this lesson is the composition: a checkpoint plus a source that can replay from it plus a sink that survives a replay, and what breaks if exactly one of those three is missing. The concrete payoff: checkpoint interval controls how MANY events get reprocessed after a crash; sink idempotency controls whether that reprocessing duplicates anything at all. They are separate knobs, and this lesson computes both so the difference stops being a slogan. Stream joins 1 rig Joining two tables that already sit still only ever has to answer "which rows match" once. Joining two streams has to answer the same question against data that hasn't finished arriving — the matching event on the other side might show up in milliseconds, or hours, or never. That single fact forces a stream-stream join to buffer BOTH sides while it waits, for as long as its join window says to wait, using the exact window/watermark machinery `stream-windows-and-watermarks.mdx` already built. A stream-table (enrichment) join escapes that buffer entirely by holding a queryable snapshot instead — at the cost of staleness, not memory. This lesson prices the buffer a stream-stream join pays (it scales linearly with join window times combined arrival rate) against what a wider window actually buys (a match rate that rises with sharply diminishing returns) — two costs that move together, never apart, and a direct, unavoidable unmatched-event residue on top of both. MapReduce and its legacy 1 rig Dean and Ghemawat's 2004 paper gave large-scale data processing a shape simple enough to teach in three verbs: map transforms each record on its own, shuffle regroups every result by key across the whole cluster, reduce folds every value sharing a key into an answer. The API itself mattered less than what building it forced two decisions, both of which outlived it. Move the code to the data instead of the data to the code, because a datacenter network moves bytes far slower than a local disk does. And when a task fails, don't try to save it — just run it again somewhere else, because a map or reduce function is small and pure enough that redoing it from nothing is cheaper than protecting anything inside it. Neither idea needed the specific API to survive; the API itself didn't, once chaining jobs meant paying the same storage round trip between every step. Idempotent, replayable pipelines 1 rig `idempotency.mjs` already prices what one non-idempotent operation costs when a retry duplicates it. `determinism-as-design-tool.mjs` already prices what one non-deterministic operation costs when a replica replays it. `queue-vs-log.mjs` already prices whether a source can hand back history at all. This lesson does not re-derive any of that — it composes the three into the one question every crash recovery, backfill, and deliberate replay actually asks: is this pipeline, as a whole, safe to run again? The answer is a conjunction, not an average: one stage that duplicates, one stage that reads the wall clock, or one source that cannot reach back far enough breaks the guarantee for the entire chain, regardless of how many other links are flawless. A $97 charge replayed three times through a bare balance increment costs exactly $194 of duplicate damage; the identical charge behind a fully-guarded idempotency key costs about three billionths of a cent — roughly sixty billion times less, and still never exactly zero. Lambda vs. kappa 1 rig A pipeline that needs a fast, approximate answer right now AND an eventually-correct exact answer has two classic architectural answers. Lambda architecture runs TWO parallel pipelines over the same data — a batch layer that reprocesses everything periodically and is slow but exact, a speed layer that covers the gap since the last batch run and is fast but approximate — merged in a serving layer where the batch layer's output eventually supersedes the speed layer's for the same time range. Kappa architecture runs only ONE stream pipeline, and answers "reprocess the past" by replaying the same log through the same logic instead of maintaining a second implementation. This lesson computes what each answer actually costs: lambda's bill is a fixed engineering premium for keeping two implementations of the same logic in sync as it evolves, modeled as a duplicate-implementation cost plus an expected rate of "drift defects" only two independent copies can produce. Kappa's bill is different in kind — it pays nothing extra to implement the logic once, but its "just replay it" answer only works when the log's retention still reaches back far enough AND the stream engine can replay that much history inside whatever window still makes the result useful, reusing `append-only-log-mechanics.mjs`'s retention math directly for the bytes a reprocessing job has to redo. Past a computable crossover, replay-from-scratch stops being practical — which is exactly the moment a nominally "kappa" system quietly grows a second, batch-shaped path for the jobs replay cannot reach. ETL vs ELT 1 rig Extract, Transform, Load: pull data out of a source system, reshape it on a separate processing layer, and only load the already-cleaned result into the destination. Extract, Load, Transform: pull the same data out, load it AS IS into the destination, and reshape it there, using the destination's own compute — a swap DDIA names in one sentence while defining ETL itself: "sometimes the order of the transform and load steps is swapped..., resulting in ELT" (Kleppmann & Riccomini, ch. 1, p. 7). The swap looks cosmetic. It commits you to a different place for the transform's compute to live, a different moment for a bad record to get caught, and a different answer to whether the raw extract survives past the load at all. This lesson prices all three: what a dedicated ETL layer costs annually against ELT's per-query warehouse bill, as a function of how often the transform's own logic changes — and what keeping the full raw extract around costs in storage, as a function of how much of it any transform ever actually keeps. Approximate algorithms 1 rig `bloom-filters.mdx` already spends a bounded amount of memory on one question — is this element (probably) in the set? — and prices the false-positive rate that buys. Four more questions come up just as often at stream scale, and none of them is membership: how many DISTINCT elements went by, how many TIMES did this one element go by, what's the p99 of everything that went by, and can I keep a uniformly-random SAMPLE without knowing how long the stream will run. This lesson is where the same bet — a small, fixed structure, an error you can name in advance, a memory bill that never grows — gets placed four more times, once per question, each with its own formula for exactly what the memory buys. The shuffle primitive 1 rig Between map and reduce sits the step every treatment of MapReduce names and prices only in passing: shuffle takes every mapper's output, sends each key to the one reducer holding its partition, and turns "process this dataset" into a fully-connected mapper-to-reducer exchange. This has nothing to do with *shuffle sharding* -- a load-balancing technique for isolating tenants across a shard pool, covered in `shuffle-sharding.mdx` -- beyond the word itself; this lesson's shuffle is a distributed sort. Three costs follow from that. Every mapper talking to every reducer means the transfer count scales as M times R even though the total bytes moved does not -- a cost axis most treatments never separate from data volume. A reducer's partition that outgrows its memory spills sorted runs to disk and pays to merge them back. And a hash that lands unevenly across keys concentrates load onto one reducer, so the whole stage waits on its slowest one -- a maximum, not an average. Batch joins 1 rig A join or a GROUP BY over data too big for one machine still needs the exact same two ingredients this curriculum already built separately: `shuffle-the-batch-primitive.mdx`'s distributed sort, which gets every record with a given key onto the same reducer no matter which shard it started on, and `join-algorithms.mdx`'s sort-merge cost, which prices what that reducer does with its own now-small partition afterward. This lesson is the composition, not a fourth algorithm: total cost is the shuffle (a function of the FULL dataset, over a shared network link that more machines can't widen) plus one reducer's local sort-merge (a function of its own PARTITION, which shrinks as more reducers split the work). Two wrinkles fall out of taking that composition seriously. Secondary sort — ordering records within a group, not just grouping them — needs a composite sort key, a distinct technique from sorting twice. And a GROUP BY is not a different operation from a join at all: it is the identical shuffle-then-local-reduce pattern with one input instead of two, which this lesson proves by showing the two cost formulas produce the same number, not by asserting the resemblance. Dataflow engines 1 rig MapReduce forces every multi-stage pipeline into a chain of independent jobs, each one reading its input from durable storage and writing its output back to durable storage -- even when the very next job is the only thing that will ever read it. A dataflow engine (Spark, Flink, Tez) drops the chain: express the whole pipeline as one DAG of operators, and let the engine's own scheduler decide where a stage actually has to end. Two consecutive operators that don't need to see each other's partitions -- a filter after a map, a filter after that -- fuse into one stage and never touch storage at all; only an operator that genuinely repartitions data, a real shuffle, draws a boundary. That is the entire mechanical difference, and it prices exactly: how many storage round trips a naive chain pays versus how few a fused DAG actually needs, and what removing the durable boundary between stages costs once, instead of the happy path, a task simply fails. Serving derived data from batch 1 rig `mapreduce-and-its-legacy.mdx` already covers what happens INSIDE a batch job; `cqrs.mdx` already covers splitting a read model from a write model in general, kept in sync by an incremental projector that updates on every write. This lesson is the specific mechanism many batch jobs use instead: build the ENTIRE output somewhere nobody is reading yet, verify it, then flip a single pointer to make it live in one atomic step. Readers only ever see last cycle's complete output or this cycle's complete output, never a mix, because the swap itself is one operation, not a gradual cutover -- the same principle `deployment-strategies.mdx` applies to code, applied here to data. It prices what that buys and what it costs: staleness that sawtooths between the job's own build time and a full cycle beyond it, and a storage bill that doubles for as long as the previous version is kept around as a rollback option, sized against how long it actually takes to notice a bad build. Standing queries and CEP 1 rig A database stores DATA persistently and treats QUERIES as transient: a query arrives, gets matched against data that's already indexed for fast lookup, and is forgotten the moment it returns an answer. A STANDING QUERY — the mechanism underneath complex event processing (CEP), streaming-SQL continuous queries, and saved-search alerts like "notify me when a new listing matches my search" — inverts that completely: the query is registered once and persists indefinitely, while each event flows through once and is gone. Checking a new event against every registered query, one by one, is the naive move, and it doesn't survive contact with scale. The fix is to index the queries themselves, the way a database indexes its data — but how much that buys depends entirely on how selective the average registered query's filter actually is. And once a query has to match a SEQUENCE of events rather than one, it needs state to remember what it's partially matched so far, which makes a large CEP engine a stateful operator with a real, checkpoint-able memory footprint, not a free abstraction. Open table formats 1 rig `etl-vs-elt.mdx` used "data lake" as a place raw files sit, waiting to be shaped; DDIA's own "Table format" section names Apache Iceberg and Databricks's Delta format as the thing that turns that pile of files into something a query engine can treat as a real table -- but only names time travel, GC, and transactions as features, not how any of them work. An open table format is a metadata layer on top of plain, immutable files (Parquet, typically) already sitting in object storage: a tree of small metadata files instead of one big one, a compare-and-swap commit protocol instead of a lock, and a periodic cleanup job instead of a naive delete. Four mechanisms, each with a real number: the metadata tree's own indirection is why adding five files to a million-file table costs 9,524x less than rewriting one flat manifest would; that same indirection is why keeping years of time-travel history costs the churn between commits, not a full copy per version; optimistic-concurrency commits turn `object-storage-as-coordination-primitive.mdx`'s conditional-write race into something that has to retry rather than just lose; and the small-file problem streaming ingestion creates has a genuine optimal compaction frequency, not "as often as possible." Incremental view maintenance 1 rig `cqrs.mdx` covers WHETHER a read model is split out and kept fresh at all, by any mechanism. `serving-derived-data-from-batch.mdx` covers one such mechanism: rebuild the WHOLE output on a fixed cadence and atomically swap it in. `materialized-views.mdx` covers the periodic middle ground — apply only the delta since the last cadence tick, but still gated by a tick. This lesson is the mechanism underneath "streaming SQL" products (Materialize, RisingWave, ksqlDB): remove the tick entirely, so every write to the base table applies its own delta to the view immediately. It reuses that periodic-vs-full-recompute crossover rather than re-deriving it, and adds the two things a per-write, per-operation view of the same problem exposes that a per-refresh, per-batch view does not: AVG needs two running numbers, not one, and MAX/MIN's delta is NOT symmetric — inserting is always cheap, but deleting the tracked extreme forces a scan an insert never has to pay for. Object-storage-first streaming 1 rig A classic Kafka broker's durability comes from replicating every write to other brokers, in other zones, over a link the cloud bills by the gigabyte — `cross-az-traffic.mdx` already prices that bill exactly. A "diskless" broker (KIP-1150, WarpStream, AutoMQ) skips that bill by writing straight to an object store instead, which already replicates across zones as part of its own per-GB price. That swap sounds like a pure win, and at one setting of one knob it swings by 34× — from 3.1× cheaper to 26× more expensive, same throughput, same retention. The knob is batch size: an object-store PUT is priced per request, not per byte, so writing one message per PUT can cost more than the cross-AZ bill it was supposed to eliminate. This lesson computes exactly where that break-even sits, and the buffering latency batching spends to get past it. Lineage & Catalogs 1 rig Two questions every pipeline in this section has been quietly assuming an answer to: "if I change or drop this column, what breaks downstream" and "where does this table's PII actually live." Lineage is the dependency graph that answers the first — which upstream columns feed which downstream columns, through however many chained ETL/ELT/dbt-style transform steps — walked forward from a change to every dependent before you break it, not after. A catalog is the searchable inventory layer built ON TOP of that graph, plus schema, ownership, and classification metadata, so "who owns this table" or "which tables are tagged as PII" doesn't require reading every pipeline's source code. DDIA names the catalog piece directly, if briefly — "a data catalog defines which tables are contained in a database... [decoupling it from query engines] has enabled data discovery and data governance systems... to access a catalog's metadata as well" (Kleppmann & Riccomini, ch. 4, p. 135) — but only gestures at lineage itself, in a single paragraph on auditability: "being explicit about dataflow makes the provenance of data much clearer, which makes integrity checking much more feasible" (ch. 13, p. 576). This lesson prices what that gesture skips: how fast the graph you'd have to walk explodes with pipeline depth, and why a catalog answering either question is only as good as how its metadata got there. Push vs. Pull 1 rig `cdn.mdx` priced push and pull as two edge-caching strategies. `fanout-write-vs-read-time.mdx` priced them as two ways to fan out a social post. `realtime-menu.mdx` priced them as two ways a client learns of a server-side change. Each names the same choice in its own vocabulary and prices its own specific cost — none of them states the axis underneath: whoever decides WHEN the exchange happens controls one thing and gives up the other. Push (the producer decides) buys latency near zero, by construction, at the cost of handing the receiver whatever rate the producer's own delivery generates — smooth or bursty. Pull (the consumer decides) buys the opposite: its own incoming rate is exactly what it chose, but it only learns of a change at its next scheduled check. And pull's safety is not free either — it relocates the identical "someone bears a rate they didn't choose" problem onto whoever is being pulled from, the moment more than one independent consumer starts asking.

Backpressure, load shedding, retry storms, cascading failure, percentiles, SLOs. Nearly absent from every repo surveyed, and most of the actual job.

Backpressure orphan 1 rig A server running at 100% throughput, dropping nothing, logging no errors, and delivering nothing that anybody is still waiting for. Backpressure is refusing work you cannot do, and telling whoever sent it. Everybody skips it and says "circuit breaker" instead. Load shedding orphan 1 rig Bounding the queue stopped the outage. It also started refusing paying customers in exact proportion to their traffic, and kept serving a sidebar widget nobody looks at. Shedding on purpose is easy; shedding the right work is the part that pays. Graceful degradation 1 rig Load shedding decides whether a request gets an answer at all. This lesson is about the request that DID get admitted: does one peripheral dependency failing take the whole response down with it, or does the response just get smaller? Retry budgets and amplification 1 rig Retry storms are about many clients retrying in sync. Metastable failure 1 rig Cascading-failure's outage was sustained by retries. Deploying Stateful Systems 1 rig `canary-analysis-and-rollback.mdx` priced a stateless rollback's only real cost: a fixed mechanical delay after the gate fires. Metric cardinality 1 rig A time-series database stores each unique combination of a metric's label values as its own series, with its own memory and disk footprint. Two small labels compose fine. This lesson computes that multiplication, the memory bill an in-memory series index pays for it, and the one fix (bucket it, or move it out of metrics entirely) that actually bounds it. Structured logging and sampling 1 rig Logs are one full record per occurrence — `metrics-logs-traces.mdx` already priced that at 43.2 GB/day for a modest fleet. Capacity planning & load testing 1 rig Two teams read "we have 30% headroom" and both are wrong the same way, because they are treating a curve that runs to infinity as if it were a straight line. Formal methods and DST 1 rig Three ways of establishing distributed-system correctness now, and they catch three different bug classes for three different reasons. AuthN/AuthZ, OAuth flows, encryption, PII, deletion in a distributed store.

JWT revocation orphan 2 rigs A signed token is a cached authorization decision, and the lifetime you set is a cache TTL. That makes revocation cache invalidation, which is the hard part everywhere else too. The arithmetic nobody publishes: the stateless design only reduces load while the token outlives the gap between one user's requests, and shortening it past that point costs more than the sessions it replaced while still leaving a window. OAuth flows orphan 1 rig Every explanation of OAuth is a sequence diagram, and a sequence diagram answers neither question anyone has: how long does this make login take, and what happens if the thing in the URL is stolen. Both are countable. Login is four navigations — 1.3 seconds before your app draws anything — and the flow that saves one of them was removed from the specification. Envelope encryption orphan 1 rig A key management service will not encrypt your object — AWS KMS refuses anything over 4 KB — so you encrypt locally with a data key and let the KMS wrap only the key. That leaves exactly one decision: how many objects share a data key. It sets the request bill, what one leaked key exposes, and the smallest thing you can cryptographically delete, and the first two are a conserved product you can only slide along. ReBAC and Zanzibar orphan 1 rig "Can this user read this document?" is not a lookup. The answer is not stored anywhere — what is stored is relations, and the answer is whether a path exists. So authorization is a graph traversal on the hot path of every request, its latency is the nesting depth rather than the number of groups, and the obvious fix creates a staleness window with a failure mode ordinary caches do not have. Deletion at scale orphan 1 rig A row lives in about eighty places and a DELETE reaches eight of them. The rest expire on their own schedule, and if any of those schedules outlives your deletion promise, the promise is false by arithmetic rather than by accident. There are exactly three honest resolutions and two of them are expensive. Isolation tiers orphan 1 rig Every agent product now runs code a model wrote, which means picking a boundary to run it behind. Startup time is not a latency problem — it is a fleet-size problem, by Little's Law — and once you price it that way the ordering everyone assumes inverts: a shared-kernel container costs more memory than a microVM whose escape has to defeat hardware virtualisation.

Monolith to services, decomposition, sagas, outbox, and the distributed monolith.

When to split orphan 1 rig The decision to split one process into several is made almost entirely with adjectives, and it goes wrong for a structural reason: the costs of splitting are measurable and the benefits mostly are not. So the costs get computed, the benefits get asserted, and whoever speaks last wins. This lesson computes the costs precisely — availability, latency, amplification — and then states the benefits honestly, which means admitting that exactly one of the four usual ones produces a number. The distributed monolith orphan 1 rig "You have a distributed monolith" is used as an insult, which is why nobody agrees they have one. It is a measurement, and the measurement is not about runtime — it is how many services must ship together for an average change. If that number is above one you are paying for k services and collecting the independence of k divided by it. Splitting further makes it worse. Saga vs transaction orphan 1 rig A saga replaces one atomic transaction with a sequence of local ones plus an undo for each. The guarantee it offers is real and it is not atomicity: either everything committed, or a prefix committed and was undone. Read that for what it does not say. TIER 9 Cost, capacity, economics 6/13

An entire tier missing everywhere else. Dollars per million requests, egress, cross-AZ, when a cache stops paying for itself.

Cost per million orphan 1 rig Every system-design resource sizes a system in requests per second and bytes, and then stops — which leaves engineers optimising the one term that is a twentieth of the bill. Decompose a million requests into dollars and the dominant term is almost never compute, the thresholds are ratios of prices rather than prices, and the cheapest fleet is the one with the worst tail. Cross-AZ traffic orphan 1 rig Availability zones exist so that one datacentre failing does not take you down, and the bill for that is invisible in the architecture diagram — because the diagram draws a replica as a box and the charge is on the arrows between them. Replication, the line everyone blames, is 1.5% of it. Cache economics orphan 1 rig A cache pays when its hit rate clears a break-even set by the ratio of two costs — and that break-even rises as you make the cache bigger. Hit rate grows like a logarithm, rent grows like a straight line, so the size that maximises savings sits an order of magnitude below the size that maximises hit rate. Past a certain point a 94.7% hit rate saves exactly nothing. Egress costs orphan 1 rig Almost every line on a cloud bill is priced per unit of work — an instance-hour, a request, an operation. Egress is priced per byte, and response size appears in no capacity model, no load test and no autoscaler. Worse, the same byte is billed on four or five boundaries on its way out, only one of which has the word egress on it. And the fix everyone reaches for — a CDN — turns out to be a price arbitrage that clears its cost at a 23% hit ratio, or cannot clear it at any hit ratio at all, depending entirely on your response size. Read/write asymmetry orphan 1 rig A write unit covers 1 KB. A read unit covers 4 KB, or 8 KB if you can live with eventual consistency — so per byte a write costs four to eight times a read, and asking for strong consistency doubles the read bill exactly. Then the units are rounded up per operation, which moves the bill off your total bytes and onto your item size distribution. The mean is the wrong statistic, and the right one is computable before you build. Provisioned vs spot orphan 1 rig Three prices for identical hardware, and only one of them is a cost. Preemptible capacity is 60-90% off and can be taken back with a couple of minutes' notice, so its real cost is its price times the wall clock it wastes — and the discount it has to beat turns out to equal that waste exactly. The waste has a minimum at a checkpoint interval of sqrt(2c/λ), which nobody computes. The committed half is one line and nobody computes that either: a 40% discount needs 60% utilisation.

Prefill vs decode orphan 1 rig One API call, two workloads with opposite bottlenecks.
